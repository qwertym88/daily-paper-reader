## Abstract 

Looped (weight-tied) Transformers apply a shared residual block N times ( h ← h + ε f (h), same f

at each step), increasing effective depth without adding parameters. Prior depth-scaling analyses prescribe ε = 1 /√L for depth-L residual networks. We show that this is insufficient for looped architectures: weight sharing makes residual updates correlated across iterations, requiring the stronger scaling ε = 1 /N . For multi-layer blocks ( L unique layers looped N times), we derive a factored parameterization ε = λ/ (N√L) that separates the two sources of growth: 1/N controls the within-layer loop correlation, and 1/√L controls the across-layer variance. A key consequence is that the optimal learning rate depends only on the number of unique layers L, not on the loop count N , enabling direct hyperparameter transfer from small to large N without retuning. Experiments on looped Transformers confirm that 1/N scaling improves trainability and yields better loss than 1/√N scaling across loop counts.

## 1 Introduction 

Looped (weight-tied) Transformers reuse a single block f for N iterations ( h ← h + f (h), same f

at each step), increasing effective depth without adding parameters. This design appears in Uni-versal Transformers [ 5], ALBERT [ 11 ], and recent work on algorithmic reasoning and latent computa-tion [7, 8, 17, 20, 27, 30]. In practice, increasing N often leads to training in-stability such as exploding hidden states and high sensitivity to the learning rate [ 30 ]. A standard rem-edy is to scale each residual branch by a factor ε that shrinks with depth, giving h ← h + ε f (h). Prior depth-scaling analyses prescribe ε = 1 /√N for deep residual networks [ 2, 6], but whether this rule trans-fers to looped architectures—where the same f is reused at every step—has not been established. We find that 1/√N scaling is indeed insufficient for looped models. Consider the residual-stream norm 

∥hN ∥ after N iterations. For standard (non-shared) deep networks, ε = 1 /√L successfully keeps ∥hL∥

bounded as depth L grows (Figure 2, top row). For looped networks, however, ε = 1 /√N fails to con-trol ∥hN ∥, which grows rapidly with N ; in contrast, 

ε = 1 /N keeps it bounded (Figure 2, bottom row). Our theoretical analysis (Section 3) explains this dis-crepancy: the 1/√N rule relies on the assumption that each layer has independent weights, but weight sharing makes successive updates correlated, ampli-fying residual-stream norm growth from Θ( √N ) to 

Θ( N ).Beyond stabilizing the forward pass, the 1/N scaling also fixes the learning rate. The same constructive ac-cumulation that drives Θ( N 2) squared-norm growth also amplifies weight updates: the output change

Beyond the theoretical contri-bution, the resulting parameterization directly solves two practical problems: it improves trainability at large loop counts and eliminates the need to re-tune hyperparameters when varying N . By making the loop count stable and tunable, these results estab-lish reuse as a practical scaling axis for weight-tied Transformers. 

## 2 Related Work 

Looped and parameter-shared Transformers. Using one block repeatedly has been explored as recurrent depth in Universal Transformers [ 5] and as cross-layer parameter sharing in ALBERT [ 11 ]. More re-cent looped models show strong behavior on iterative algorithm learning and multi-step in-context proce-dures [ 8, 27 ], length generalization [ 7], and latent-reasoning style test-time compute scaling [ 20 , 30 ]. Related parameter-sharing formulations also appear in looped neural networks [ 17 ]. Our work addresses the complementary question of how to parameterize the residual scaling so that training remains stable and hyperparameters transfer.

Hyperparameter transfer and parameterization. 

Tensor-program and μP-style analyses establish trans-fer principles across model scales and motivate sys-tematic parameterization choices [ 26 ]. Our work is complementary, targeting the loop axis and showing that shared weights create cross-step correlations that change the stability threshold and transfer regime.

## 4 Extension to Multi-Layer Blocks 

Section 3 analyzed a single shared layer; practical looped architectures repeat a block of L distinct layers 

N times. We now extend the scaling analysis to this setting. 

4.1 Setup 

Each layer ℓ has its own weight matrix Wℓ, but the same Wℓ is reused across all N iterations. We index hidden states as hn,ℓ , where n ∈ { 0, . . . , N − 1} is the loop iteration and ℓ ∈ { 0, . . . , L − 1} is the layer index within the block. The recursion is: 

hn,ℓ +1 = hn,ℓ + ε W ℓ ϕ(hn,ℓ ),ℓ = 0 , . . . , L − 1, n = 0 , . . . , N − 1, (2) where the output of the last layer feeds into the next iteration: hn+1 ,0 = hn,L , starting from h0,0 = h0.The model output is hN −1,L , equivalently hN, 0 after the final loop pass; our goal is to determine ε as a function of both N and L.

Theorem 3 (Multi-layer block scaling; informal) .

Assume that :(i) each layer’s branch output has nondegenerate normalized norm ;(ii) the conditional mean of each layer’s branch out-put, given the other layers’ weights, has normal-ized norm at most O(εN );(iii) replacing any single weight matrix changes other branches’ outputs by at most O(εN ).

Then ε = λ/ (N√L) yields a residual-stream norm bounded by a function of λ alone, independent of both N and L. For sufficiently small λ, the bound is tight : the unscaled total branch variance is Θ( LN 2).The formal statement and proof are in Appendix A.4, Theorem 6.

## 5 Experiments 

We test the scaling predictions on looped Transform-ers trained for language modeling, progressing from a controlled initialization diagnostic (Section 5.1) to full-scale training with learning-rate transfer (Sec-tions 5.2–5.3) and validation that the theoretical assumptions hold beyond initialization (Section 5.4). 

Model structure. All experiments use decoder-only Transformers with a Llama-style pre-norm architec-ture [ 21 , 22 ]. The token embedding and unembedding head sit outside the loop; the looped stack contains 

L unique Transformer blocks, each with two resid-ual branches (attention and MLP), and the entire 

L-block sequence is repeated for N passes. The resid-ual scaling factor ε is applied to each branch; in the single-layer theory (Section 3), one “layer” corre-sponds to one branch, so the constant factor of two per block is absorbed into λ.

Protocol. We train L=12 looped Transformers on FineWeb-Edu [ 18 ] (10B tokens) with loop counts

Findings. (i) improved performance: at N = 8 , lin-ear scaling achieves a lower minimum loss than sqrt scaling (a reduction of 0.025 nats), indicating better trainability at larger loop counts. (ii) predictable transfer : the optimal learning rate for linear scaling remains nearly invariant across N (Figure 5b), consis-tent with our theoretical prediction ( η ∝ N 0); under sqrt scaling, the optimum shifts with N (Figure 5a), requiring separate tuning for each loop count. Note that Theorem 2 requires εN = O(1) (Assumption 3); sqrt scaling ( α=1/2) violates this premise—forward-pass norms still grow with N (Figure 2e)—so the theorem does not predict the direction or magnitude of the optimum shift in that regime.

Protocol. The theoretical analysis characterizes residual-stream behavior at initialization. Findings. (i) correlation weakens but remains positive : compared to initialization (Figure 3), the pairwise cosine similarities between loop-step incre-ments decrease after full training, but the correla-tion matrix remains positive for most loop-step pairs across all three depths, with early and middle iter-ations concentrated in the 0.3–0.6 range; only the latest steps show near-zero or mildly negative val-ues (Figure 6). scaling produces similar training dynamics regardless of loop count. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We showed that weight sharing in looped Transform-ers requires 1/N residual scaling, and derived a pa-rameterization that makes the stable learning rate independent of the loop count. Experiments on lan-guage modeling confirm that this recipe transfers hyperparameters across both loop counts and depths without additional tuning. By decoupling the loop count from optimization, our results open the possi-bility of treating weight reuse as a freely adjustable scaling axis. Natural next steps include scaling up to production-sized models and extending the theory to Post-Norm architectures [ 25 ], where the interaction between normalization placement and weight sharing may yield a different scaling regime. 

## Limitations 

Our theoretical analysis models the looped block as a shared-weight MLP, abstracting away the multi-head attention mechanism present in Transformers. The formal results further assume ReLU activations, whereas our experiments use SwiGLU; the predicted 

Θ( N 2) growth and 1/N scaling nonetheless hold em-pirically (Figures 3, 6). More broadly, the analy-sis does not account for optimizer state dynamics, normalization variants, or data-dependent feature learning. On the empirical side, our experiments cover a finite set of model sizes, loop counts, and training bud-gets. Validation on larger models, longer training horizons, and heterogeneous multi-stage looped ar-chitectures remains future work. In addition, each reported language-modeling result uses a single ran-dom seed; multi-seed replicas of the full configuration sweep were not conducted due to computational con-straints. 

## Ethical Considerations 

This work is a methodological study of residual scal-ing for weight-tied Transformers. It involves no hu-man subjects and no personal or sensitive data. All language-modeling experiments use the publicly re-leased FineWeb-Edu corpus [ 18 ], which is derived from open web text and inherits the well-known bi-ases, factual inaccuracies, and content distribution of large-scale web crawls; any model trained on such data carries those risks. We do not release new model weights, datasets, or downstream applications. The largest model trained in this work has 438 M pa-rameters and is trained for 10 B tokens, which is small relative to current frontier systems but still consumes meaningful compute.
