Abstract 

We propose Gaussian Process Reinforcement Learning-Augmented Model Predictive Control (GP– RL–MPC), which uses a learned critic as the MPC terminal penalty to account for costs beyond the prediction horizon. Gaussian process regression (GPR) enables data-efficient critic learning from limited, noisy rollouts within optimistic approximate policy iteration (OAPI), with soft up-dates mitigating policy chattering. A tunable risk coefficient weights GP epistemic uncertainty in the terminal cost, regulating sensitivity to uncertain critic predictions. The postdecision state for-mulation shifts disturbance averaging offline to account for aleatoric uncertainty from the terminal transition onward. Under matching objectives and admissible actions, a one-step horizon recovers greedy value-space RL. After fitting GP hyperparameters by exact marginal likelihood, we con-struct a sparse deterministic training conditional (DTC) posterior using inducing points selected by pivoted Cholesky (PC), reducing online evaluation costs as the training dataset grows. In a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) subject to exogenous disturbances, GP–RL–MPC with an appropriately selected risk coefficient and policy screening reduces the sum of squared errors (SSE) by an average of 80 .52 % relative to an otherwise matched baseline MPC across five random seeds (sample standard deviation: 3.48 %). 

Keywords: Reinforcement learning, Model predictive control, Gaussian process regression, Post-decision state-value functions, Uncertainty-aware optimization and control 

1. Introduction 

Model predictive control (MPC) handles nonlinear dynamics, multivariable interactions, and op-erating constraints through repeated finite-horizon optimization (Lee et al., 2026). Computational demands and growing prediction uncertainty limit practical horizon lengths, making terminal costs important for accounting for long-term consequences beyond the prediction horizon. Under ap-propriate assumptions, suitable terminal ingredients can support recursive feasibility, closed-loop stability, and performance guarantees. However, constructing such terminal ingredients remains challenging, particularly for nonlinear stochastic systems. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a complementary approach by learning long-term cost in-formation from closed-loop rollouts to approximate terminal values in MPC (Bertsekas, 2024). Previous studies have explored approximate dynamic programming (ADP) for control (Lee et al., 2006), RL-based parameterization of economic nonlinear MPC (NMPC) (Gros and Zanon, 2020), and terminal-value learning using approximate value iteration (Moreno-Mora et al., 2023), partially input-convex neural networks (PICNNs) (Li and Lee, 2026a), and radial basis function (RBF) net-works (Li and Lee, 2026b). A key challenge in extending these approaches to stochastic systems

The conventional (predecision) state-value function represents the expected cumulative cost conditioned on the current state, but evaluating it at a nominal terminal state omits averaging over disturbed terminal states. Explicitly computing this additional expectation online adds to the burden of stochastic MPC (Mesbah, 2016). A postdecision state captures the known effect of the control action before the stochastic transition, allowing its value function to incorporate the expectation over successor states (Sutton and Barto, 2018; Powell, 2016). Learning this function from disturbed rollouts shifts disturbance averaging offline while preserving deterministic online MPC optimiza-tion. A one-step horizon recovers greedy value-space RL, while longer horizons provide multistep lookahead. Building on the postdecision state formulation, we develop GP–RL–MPC to improve short-horizon MPC performance while preserving its computational advantages. A Gaussian process (GP) critic learns the conditional expected cost-to-go from disturbed rollouts under a fixed policy, thereby incorporating future disturbance effects into postdecision value estimates evaluated at nom-inal MPC terminal states. The main contributions of the proposed uncertainty-aware framework, illustrated in Fig. 1, are as follows: • Uncertainty propagation: A postdecision state-value critic that learns the expected effects of aleatoric uncertainty on the cost-to-go from disturbed rollouts, enabling nominal MPC to account for future disturbances without explicitly evaluating expectations during online optimization. • Uncertainty calibration: A computationally efficient GP critic that combines exact marginal-likelihood hyperparameter estimation with pivoted Cholesky (PC) inducing-point selection and deterministic training conditional (DTC) sparsification, reducing online computational costs while retaining estimates of epistemic uncertainty. • Uncertainty regulation: A terminal penalty that incorporates GP epistemic uncertainty through a tunable risk coefficient, combined with soft critic updates within optimistic approximate policy iteration, to promote reliable policy improvement while preserving the efficiency of short-horizon MPC.

2.3. Postdecision State-Value Learning as Optimistic Approximate Policy Iteration 

Starting from a baseline MPC policy π(0) without a terminal penalty, each policy iteration (PI) #n

evaluates the preceding policy π(n−1) using disturbed rollouts. The resulting data are used to up-date the GP critic, whose posterior mean μp, (n)GP approximates the postdecision state-value func-tion V pπ(n−1) . The GP posterior mean and latent posterior standard deviation are then combined to construct the uncertainty-regulated terminal value estimate V p, (n) 

> θ

, which is incorporated into the constrained MPC optimization to obtain a candidate policy π(n). Thus, the critic learning serves as approximate policy evaluation, while the MPC optimization performs policy improvement using the learned postdecision state-value function (Bertsekas, 2024). This iterative procedure constitutes optimistic approximate policy iteration (OAPI) (Bertsekas, 2022), in which policy improvement is performed following partial, sample-based policy evaluation rather than waiting for complete convergence of the value function. Such incomplete evaluation can lead to oscillations between successive policies, commonly referred to as policy chattering (Li and Lee, 2026a,b). The conven-tional predecision state-value function and its relationship to the postdecision state-value function are reviewed in Appendix C.

3.1. Soft Updates in Value Space 

At the first policy iteration, the GP critic is trained directly on the Monte Carlo returns Gi. From the second iteration onward, soft updates are introduced by constructing the regression targets as 

y(n) 

> i

= (1 − τ )μp, (n−1) GP (χpi ) + τ G i, 0 < τ ≤ 1, (7) where τ is the soft-update parameter and μp, (n−1) GP is the posterior mean of the preceding GP critic, held fixed during target construction. The update is performed in the original cost units, blending the previous critic prediction with the newly observed MC return. Smaller values of τ provide stronger damping of critic updates, thereby mitigating abrupt changes in the learned value function and the resulting MPC policy. Setting τ = 1 recovers the raw MC targets, corresponding to a hard update. The resulting input-target pairs are partitioned into training and diagnostic subsets using target-quantile stratification (Li and Lee, 2026b). Input and output scalers are fitted using the initial train-ing subset and subsequently held fixed to ensure consistent scaling across policy iterations. At each iteration, the GP hyperparameters are initialized from their values in the preceding iteration and refitted using the current training dataset. This procedure combines damped policy evaluation with consistent data preprocessing and warm-started GP training to improve the stability of successive policy updates. 

3.2. Exact GPR Training and PC-DTC Deployment 

We fit the full GP critic offline by maximizing the exact log marginal likelihood. With the fitted hyperparameters fixed, pivoted Cholesky (PC) selects M ≪ N inducing points from the N training inputs (Harbrecht et al., 2012). A deterministic training conditional (DTC) approximation incorpo-rates information from all training pairs through this inducing representation (Qui˜ nonero-Candela and Rasmussen, 2005). The latent uncertainty excludes observation noise and represents critic epis-temic uncertainty conditional on the fitted GP model. After precomputing the Cholesky factors and predictive mean coefficients offline, evaluating the mean and variance at one query requires 

O(M 2 + M n χ) operations, compared with O(N 2 + N n χ) for exact GPR using dense Cholesky factors, where nχ is the input dimension (Rasmussen and Williams, 2006). The regression model, inducing-point selection, and predictive formulas are detailed in Appendix B.

4.3. Effect of the Risk Coefficient 

To investigate the role of epistemic uncertainty in terminal-cost optimization, we compare three risk coefficients, κ ∈ {− 3.0, 0.0, 3.0}. These correspond to an optimistic lower confidence bound, the posterior mean, and a conservative upper confidence bound, respectively, with κ = ±3.0 represent-ing the bounds of a nominal 99 .7% pointwise Gaussian credible interval. Fig. 2(a)–(e) shows the resulting mean rollout SSE trajectories across policy iterations for the five seed configurations. The screening-enabled configuration with κ = 3 .0 achieves sustained, monotonically non-increasing SSE across accepted PI rounds. In contrast, the κ = 0 and κ = −3.0 configurations exhibit non-monotonic behavior, with generally larger fluctuations under κ = −3.0. These lat-ter two configurations are evaluated as diagnostic runs without screening, each continued for the same number of PI rounds as its corresponding κ = 3 .0 run. Although mean-based optimization (κ = 0 .0) and optimistic optimization ( κ = −3.0) occasionally achieve lower SSE, these improve-ments are neither predictable nor consistently sustained. For example, in Fig. 2(c), κ = 0 achieves an SSE of 0.39 at PI #6 , compared with 0.47 for κ = 3 .0, but only after several intermediate per-formance reversals. 5. Conclusion 

We developed GP–RL–MPC, an uncertainty-aware framework integrating uncertainty propagation, calibration, and regulation to improve short-horizon MPC performance while preserving its com-putational advantages. For uncertainty propagation, the GP postdecision state-value critic approx-imates expected cost-to-go under aleatoric uncertainty from disturbed MC rollouts aligned with nominal terminal queries. This formulation shifts disturbance averaging offline and avoids explicit online expectation calculations. For uncertainty calibration, exact marginal-likelihood hyperpa-rameter fitting is followed by PC-DTC sparse deployment, reducing kernel redundancy and online evaluation costs while maintaining reliable uncertainty estimates. For uncertainty regulation, the UCB-based terminal cost uses the latent posterior standard deviation to provide tunable regulation of critic epistemic uncertainty in control decisions. Numerical results provide empirical support for consistent and sustained policy improvement under the OAPI procedure. Future work will extend GP–RL–MPC to economic optimization and control.

In practical implementation, training rollouts can be generated using a simulator or world model to reduce experimental costs. Plant data can then be used for model calibration, validation, and sub-sequent refinement. The learned critic approximates expected returns under the simulated dynamics and disturbance distribution. Increasing the number of MC rollouts reduces sampling error but cannot eliminate bias arising from model-plant mismatch. Reliable deployment consequently re-quires validation over the intended operating region and, when necessary, iterative refinement of the dynamics and disturbance models using plant data. Importantly, the GP posterior uncertainty characterizes uncertainty in the learned critic conditional on the assumed model and training data; it does not directly quantify simulator-plant discrepancy. Robustness to such mismatch must therefore be assessed separately.
