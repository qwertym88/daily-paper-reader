## Abstract 

Safe in-context reinforcement learning (Safe ICRL) asks a policy with fixed parameters to improve on an unseen constrained task from its interaction history while aiming to keep its cost within a budget. Prior work studied Safe ICRL on five benchmarks in three families: a grid world with a hidden goal and obstacles (DarkRoom), continuous navigation where goals and hazards stay invisible until reached (PointGoal, CarGoal), and locomotion with a hidden velocity limit (HalfCheetah, Ant). Safe Algorithm Distillation (Safe AD), trained on the learning histories of a safe RL algorithm, has shown adaptation failures in prior evaluations (Moeini et al., 2026; Kwon et al., 2026c). For example, in the locomotion tasks its return stays below zero, and it rarely reaches the goal in CarGoal. We revisit two parts of Safe AD. First, we distill a budget-aware teacher with two phases. It searches for the hidden task, exploring more when more budget remains. After its first reward or cost, an expert policy exploits the task within the remaining budget. We collect the teacher’s histories under fixed or dynamic budgets. Dynamic budgets increase the occupancy entropy of the return and budget inputs in every environment, and at test time they improve return and lower cost in DarkRoom and HalfCheetah. Second, we propose A-Lag, a test-time adjustment that reweights the distilled policy’s actions by their predicted cost, with an adaptive Lagrange multiplier that is updated from the costs observed. In our experiments, A-Lag had lower mean episode cost than Safe AD in all five environments, with lower mean final return in four. 

## 1 Introduction 

In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) adapts to a new task through the interaction history, without updating model parameters (Moeini et al., 2025; Run & Ding, 2026). When the test task differs from the training tasks, this adaptation poses a safety-generalization problem: changes in dynamics or task conditions may remain partly unobserved, degrade policy generalization, and create safety risks (Kirk et al., 2023; Benjamins et al., 2023; Kwon et al., 2026b). Safe ICRL extends ICRL to this setting by asking the policy to improve its task behavior while controlling cost. Transformer policies can learn in-context adaptation for reward-only objectives (Xu et al., 2022; Laskin et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Raparthy et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Song et al., 2026), and prior work formalized Safe ICRL with constrained Markov decision processes (Moeini et al., 2026). Algorithm Distillation (AD) trains a Transformer on the learning histories

Safe AD, the AD baseline of that work, distills the histories of a safe RL algorithm such as PPO-Lagrangian (Moeini et al., 2026) and has shown adaptation failures on some of these benchmarks. In HalfCheetah and Ant its return stays below zero in every in-context episode, and in CarGoal, it rarely reaches the goal (Appendix J). This motivates the central question of the paper: 

can Safe AD adapt to unseen tasks at lower cost if it learns from a budget-aware teacher and adjusts to the budget explicitly at test time? 

We study two parts of Safe AD, the data it learns from and how it uses the budget at deployment. In safe RL, the learning histories usually come from a collector trained under one fixed cost limit (Achiam et al., 2017; Ray et al., 2019). Safe AD distills the learning histories. These histories show policy improvement, but they are not built to show how behavior should change with the budget or how to search for an unknown task at low cost. We instead distill a teacher whose histories show the behavior we want from the student. It searches for the hidden task while the budget allows it and then switches to an expert policy that exploits what it found with the best behavior that fits the remaining budget, similar to expert-data pretraining for ICRL (Lee et al., 2023). At deployment, Safe AD takes the remaining cost budget (cost-to-go, CTG) as an input at every step, but how it reacts to that input is learned in training. For example, if an episode overspends its budget, Safe AD can adjust the next episode only in the way it learned during training, through the history in its context. To address this, we propose Action-level Lagrangian (A-Lag), which adds an explicit correction while leaving the trained weights unchanged. Its Lagrange multiplier penalizes the predicted cost of each action inside the distilled policy rather than a training objective. A-Lag keeps Safe AD’s Transformer, its return-to-go (RTG) and CTG inputs, and its behavior-cloning objective, and it trains one extra head that predicts the immediate cost of an action from the policy’s representation. At deployment the distilled policy is reweighted by the predicted cost of each action, scaled by a multiplier λ, so that actions with high predicted cost become less likely. The multiplier is updated between episodes by a dual-ascent step on the realized episode cost relative to the budget. A-Lag therefore adapts in two ways, through its context as Safe AD does and through the multiplier, which responds explicitly to observed costs relative to the budget. Our experiments use the five Safe ICRL benchmarks of Moeini et al. (2026): a grid world with a hidden goal and obstacles (DarkRoom), continuous navigation where goals and hazards stay invisible until reached (PointGoal, CarGoal), and locomotion with a hidden velocity limit (HalfCheetah, Ant). Our contributions are the following. •Budget-aware teacher. We propose distilling a budget-aware teacher that searches for the hidden task and then exploits it. Collecting its histories under dynamic budgets makes the return and budget inputs more diverse by occupancy entropy (Table 1). •A-Lag. We propose a Lagrange multiplier that reweights actions by predicted cost and is updated from observed episode costs at test time. In our experiments, A-Lag had lower mean episode cost than Safe AD in all five environments, with lower mean final return in four (Section 5.3).

## 3 Teacher Data for Safe Algorithm Distillation 

Safe AD can only learn the behavior its histories show. The learning histories of a safe RL algorithm under one fixed cost limit show a policy improving, but not how to search for a hidden task at low cost or how to act when less budget remains. To address this, we collect histories with the two-phase teacher of Section 3.2, under the fixed- and dynamic-budget schemes defined below.

## 4 A-Lag: In-Context Dual Adaptation 

A-Lag, short for action-level Lagrangian, penalizes the predicted cost of each action with a Lagrange multi-plier and updates that multiplier during deployment. It combines the Safe AD sequence model, trained as in Safe AD (Section 4.1), a cost head that defines a family of cost-reweighted policies (Section 4.2), and a deployment-time update of the multiplier from realized costs (Section 4.3). Apart from the growing context, only this update changes how the trained network acts.

## 5 Experiments 

We ask three questions. RQ1 : do dynamic budgets, which make the teacher data cover more diverse return and budget inputs, improve test return and cost over a fixed budget? RQ2 : does A-Lag improve on the return and cost of Safe AD on unseen test tasks, and how does CDT compare? RQ3 : how often and by how much does each method go over its budget? 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We use five environments derived from the Safe ICRL benchmarks of Moeini et al. (2026). Each has separate training and test tasks. Appendix A gives the layouts and the sizes of the splits. • DarkRoom is a partially observed grid world with a hidden goal and hidden obstacles. Training goals and obstacles are center-oriented, while test goals and obstacles are edge-oriented. • PointGoal and CarGoal are continuous navigation tasks with a broken lidar, so the agent cannot sense goals or hazards until it reaches or touches them. Their training layouts are center-oriented and their test layouts are edge-oriented. • HalfCheetahVelocity and AntVelocity , below called HalfCheetah and Ant or the velocity tasks, are locomotion tasks in which cost is incurred above a hidden velocity limit that differs between tasks (Ji et al., 2023). The test velocity limits are unseen. 

Models. Safe AD (Moeini et al., 2026) and A-Lag are both trained from scratch using the backbone of Section 4.1. Unless otherwise stated, Safe AD here is trained on the teacher data, not PPO-Lagrangian histories. The main models use 32 histories per task. We report the 50% mixture. A-Lag adds the cost head and the deployment-time update. CDT (Liu et al., 2023) is an offline safe-RL sequence policy that conditions on RTG and CTG. All models train for 100,000 updates with three random seeds and the same per-environment architecture. We also show two safe meta-RL baselines from a preliminary study, MAML with a cost penalty (Finn et al., 2017) and SafeMeta (Xu & Zhu, 2025), which adapt by updating policy parameters on each new task. We do not compare with SCARED (Moeini et al., 2026) or latent Q-barrier shielding (Kwon et al., 2026c) because they are pretrained online, interacting with the training environments under their own objectives. Safe AD and A-Lag instead learn offline from fixed teacher histories, and our question is what the data and a deployment-time multiplier do for AD itself. 

Evaluation. A deployment runs one test task for K episodes with fixed parameters while the context grows. History is retained across episode resets within a deployment and cleared between deployments. K

is 50 in DarkRoom, 20 in PointGoal and CarGoal and 10 in the velocity tasks. Each deployment samples its initial RTG uniformly between two values fixed from the training data, the lowest and the median over training tasks of the teacher’s best in-context episode return, and its budget B0 uniformly over the training budget range. Both stay fixed within the deployment. Every test task receives 15 deployments, and the 6draws are shared by all methods. We report four quantities, averaged over draws, tasks and seeds. The final return is the mean return of the last three episodes. In PointGoal and CarGoal the reward is sparse. The episode return is 1 if the goal is reached and 0 otherwise, so there the return is the success rate. The cost is the mean episode cost Gc over all K episodes. The violation rate is the fraction of these episodes whose cost exceeds B0, and the excess cost is their mean of max(0 , G c − B0). Each curve point is the mean of that episode over all deployments, test tasks and seeds, so it pools de-ployments with different budgets. Table entries average these over the episodes. The shaded bands in the figures show ±2 standard errors over deployments. Text comparisons pair methods on the same seed and deployment draw. Main-text comparisons describe observed means. Appendix I gives per-seed results and intervals over test tasks. 

Tuning A-Lag. We set λmax per environment from a sweep of fixed multipliers on training tasks (Figure 6). It is the largest tested value up to which the mean final return stays above 75% of that at λ = 0. The step size η = λmax was chosen once in DarkRoom and is used in every environment (Appendix E). The primary comparison is A-Lag against Safe AD on the test tasks. In no environment does A-Lag have both a lower mean violation rate and a higher mean final return (Table 6). 

5.2 RQ1: Fixed vs. Dynamic Budgets in the Teacher Data 

We train Safe AD and A-Lag on the fixed-budget histories, in which B0 = Bmax , and compare them with the main models, which are trained on the dynamic-budget histories. Both use the same training settings, so the two differ only in the data. 

Why coverage matters. Safe AD and A-Lag choose each action from the history and two control inputs, the return still to be reached (RTG) and the budget still left (CTG). Each deployment samples a new budget, and the remaining budget falls as cost is incurred, so at test time the model sees many (RTG, CTG) pairs. It is most likely to act well at pairs that occur in its training data. Under a fixed budget every episode starts with the remaining budget at Bmax , and a small remaining budget appears only after cost has been incurred. Table 1 measures how diverse the two kinds of histories are, and Figure 5 in Appendix D shows where their steps fall. More occupied cells mean that the histories reach more (RTG, CTG) pairs, and a higher entropy means that their steps spread more evenly over these pairs instead of concentrating on a few. On a 20 × 20 grid over RTG and CTG, the dynamic-budget steps occupy all 400 cells in DarkRoom and the velocity tasks, while the fixed-budget steps occupy 179 in DarkRoom. The dynamic-budget histories also have the higher entropy in all five environments. 

Test results. Figure 1 shows how the main models and the fixed-budget models adapt over the episodes of a deployment. In DarkRoom, fixed-budget data lower the final return by 0.167 for Safe AD and by 0.123 for A-Lag and raise the mean episode cost of both by about 0.8. Both gaps open early in the deployment and last to its end. In the other environments the main models return more in every case except A-Lag in Ant. The gap is largest in PointGoal and CarGoal, where the return of the main models is nearly twice as high. Outside DarkRoom the costs move in both directions. They are lower for the main models in HalfCheetah and higher for both methods in PointGoal. In CarGoal and Ant they are lower for A-Lag and higher for Safe AD. The entropy is higher in every environment, but return and cost move in the favorable direction for both methods only in DarkRoom and HalfCheetah.

In-Context Reinforcement Learning. Adapting to new tasks without parameter updates has roots in recurrent meta-RL (Duan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Beck et al., 2025; Moeini et al., 2025). Mod-ern ICRL instead pretrains a fixed sequence policy to improve through its interaction history. Algorithm Distillation learns from complete RL learning histories (Laskin et al., 2023; Zisman et al., 2024), whereas supervised pretraining methods learn from pretrained policies or action labels (Lee et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2024), and the Decision Importance Transformer weights the imitation loss by learned advantages so that it can learn from suboptimal histories (Dong et al., 2026). Later work covers multi-task agents, varied action spaces and in-context Q-learning (Grigsby et al., 2024a;b; Sinii et al., 2024; Polubarov et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026), and theory studies when Transformers learn in context (Lin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a;b). Recent analyses connect temporal-difference policy evaluation to specific Transformer constructions, including softmax attention (Xie et al., 2026b) and chain-of-thought generation with linear Transformers (Xie et al., 2026a). These works mainly study reward maximization. 

Safe Reinforcement Learning. Safe RL commonly uses CMDPs to maximize reward subject to expected-cost constraints (García & Fernández, 2015; Gu et al., 2024; Wachi et al., 2024). Policy optimization (Achiam et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2023), safe exploration (Wachi & Sui, 2020; As et al., 2025) and runtime shielding (Kwon et al., 2026a) update a policy or model online, or filter actions, rather than distilling an adaptation algorithm into context. Offline sequence policies, including CDT and safety-conditioned variants (Liu et al., 2023; Wang & Zhou, 2024; Guo et al., 2024), condition on return and cost but do not target repeated contextual adaptation to unseen CMDPs. Other work handles changing budgets within a known task, by conditioning one policy on the cost threshold (Yao et al., 2023) or by inferring trajectories for a budget set at deployment from offline data (Lin et al., 2023). 

Safe In-Context and Meta Reinforcement Learning. Safe meta-RL adapts to constrained tasks through gradient updates or additional task-specific data (Luo et al., 2021; Khattar et al., 2023; Guan et al., 2024; Xu & Zhu, 2025). Safe ICRL instead keeps parameters fixed. SCARED formalizes this setting and uses budget-sensitive Lagrangian pretraining (Moeini et al., 2026). More recently, latent Q-barrier shielding adds an explicit deployment-time filter that reweights actions from a frozen Safe ICRL policy using predicted future cost (Kwon et al., 2026c). A-Lag also acts at deployment, but it penalizes only the predicted immediate cost, with one multiplier per deployment updated from realized episode costs. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We revisited the histories AD distills and the way the distilled policy uses the budget, with a budget-aware teacher and A-Lag, a deployment-time Lagrange multiplier. A-Lag had a lower mean cost than Safe AD in all five environments, with lower mean final return in four. Against fixed multipliers, the adaptive multiplier had lower mean cost in four environments, but higher cost and return in Ant. Dynamic budgets during data collection gave higher occupancy entropy of the return and budget inputs in every environment and, in DarkRoom, improved both return and violation rate. Unlike prior Safe ICRL evaluations, which report the mean episode cost, we also report how often and by how much each method exceeds its budget. In HalfCheetah, A-Lag has a lower mean cost than Safe AD but exceeds its budget more often, and in Ant and HalfCheetah the violation rate and the excess cost disagree, so both should be reported. Our study has limits. The cost head predicts only immediate cost and is learned only from the actions the teacher took. The teacher also requires environment-specific search, experts and switching events.

## A Environments and Task Splits 

In each environment the tasks are split into training and test sets. Following Moeini et al. (2026), layouts are sampled with density ∝ exp( α d (x, c )), where d(x, c ) is the distance from the center of the grid or arena. DarkRoom. 

•Switching event: the first reward. Its position is the goal. •Search: at each cell, the teacher tries a move it has not tried yet with probability 0 .1+0 .9 min( B/B max , 1), and otherwise waits. PointGoal and CarGoal.

Ground with no observed cost has no penalty, so the teacher avoids a hazard only after it has paid cost there in the same K in-context episodes. With no budget left, the robot only leaves ground with known cost and then stands still. •Search: the planner drives to the nearest unvisited point of a 0.5 m grid over the arena. While B < B 0/2, it skips grid points within 0.35 m of observed cost. •Expert: the planner drives to the estimated goal, the mean position of the rewarded steps. •Control: a PPO waypoint controller drives toward a point 0.6 m ahead on the planned path. 

HalfCheetah and Ant. 

•Switching event: the first cost, which means the robot ran faster than the hidden threshold. At the first cost it returns to the last cost-free speed. •Expert: it commands the slowest speed at which it has observed cost, as long as B covers that speed’s cost per step, and otherwise the fastest speed it has held without cost. Aprobe needs a reserve of at least 1 plus a running estimate of the cost paid while slowing down after a step down in speed (initially 1). Cost in the first 10 steps after a change of command is not attributed to the new command. The speed controllers of HalfCheetah and Ant see the environment observation and the target speed. ## C Training Details

## J Safe AD on PPO-Lagrangian Learning Histories 

Section 3 notes that the learning histories of a safe RL collector are not built to show the behavior a Safe ICRL agent needs. Figure 9 compares the main Safe AD, trained on the teacher data, with the Safe AD baseline of Moeini et al. (2026), trained on learning histories of PPO-Lagrangian (Ray et al., 2019). In DarkRoom, HalfCheetah and Ant its curves are per-episode means from its original evaluation. In PointGoal and CarGoal they come from a later evaluation of the same models over 20 episodes. Both evaluations used their own test tasks and targets rather than the protocol of Section 5. The comparison is therefore not paired, and its differences cannot be attributed to the source of the histories. It was also trained on 8 tasks per environment with older training settings.
