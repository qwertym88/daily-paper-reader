Abstract 

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has achieved state-of-the-art performance across diverse tasks, but its high computational cost remains a major barrier. We introduce PruneNAS, a novel framework that integrates structured pruning into NAS to efficiently explore high-performing architectures while leveraging pretrained models. Unlike con-ventional NAS methods that train each candidate from scratch, PruneNAS generates a candidate pool by pruning pretrained networks at multiple granularity levels and for-mulates the selection as a global optimization problem using training-free importance metrics. This approach enables rapid evaluation of candidate architectures under strict resource constraints, dramatically reducing search time from hundreds of GPU hours to mere minutes. Experiments on standard benchmarks demonstrate that PruneNAS dis-covers compact, high-accuracy architectures that nearly halve the computational cost (MACs) of prior methods on ResNet-50, while also showing strong generalization on object detection tasks with YOLO. Our method bridges structured pruning and NAS, offering a practical solution for resource-constrained model deployment. 

# 1 Introduction 

Deep neural networks have achieved remarkable success across diverse computer vision tasks. However, the increasing scale of these models imposes significant computational bur-dens, which complicates their deployment on resource-constrained edge devices [4, 40]. To resolve this, channel pruning [8, 20, 29, 59] has emerged as a highly effective technique. By structurally removing entire channels and their associated weights, it directly reduces the model’s memory footprint, computational cost and accelerates inference. Determining which channels to remove is commonly guided by importance criteria [19, 42, 63]. These criteria estimate each channel’s importance to the network output and are used to rank and remove channels under a target resource budget. While recent approaches [3, 36, 60] mitigate this by utilizing weight-sharing super networks or proxy evaluators, they often shift the computational bottleneck, requiring extensive training costs and GPU hours before the actual architecture search begins. As summarized in Table 1, these methods require hundreds to thousands of GPU hours simply to build the search space. Fur-thermore, while zero-cost proxies [24, 25, 39, 48, 51] offer efficient evaluation without full training, their application has largely been restricted to ranking entire architectures rather than selecting structured components under strict global constraints. To bridge this gap, we introduce PruneNAS, a framework that formulates channel allo-cation as a global constrained optimization problem. PruneNAS searches per-group channel widths within the pretrained model, retaining its layers and operators for native deploy-ment on target edge hardware. For each structural group in the network, a discrete set of pruned candidates is scored using an importance criterion and stored in accuracy and re-source lookup tables without any training. An Integer Linear Programming (ILP) solver then selects the globally optimal per-group channel configuration under a target resource budget. Finally, the same importance criterion used during search is used to prune the chan-nels. Unlike other NAS methods, we skip the need for pre-search training entirely. This reduces architecture discovery time from potentially hundreds of GPU hours to mere min-utes. Because the tables are computed once, the solver can be re-run for any budget instantly, making it straightforward to generate a family of compressed models across different deploy-ment budgets from a single pass. We make the following contributions: • We formulate channel pruning as a constrained optimization problem using a decou-pled tabulation strategy. # 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Importance and Zero-Cost Estimation 

Structured pruning reduces the parameters in a network by removing entire groups such as neurons or channels [13, 19, 54]. Traditionally, this is achieved by analyzing the con-verged weights of pre-trained models with an importance criterion that determines what to keep. Magnitude-based pruning prunes channels with smaller ℓ1-norm [28]. Taylor expan-sion estimates the impact of pruning on the loss by computing the product of activations and their gradients [41, 42]. Second-order methods utilize Hessian-based approximations sensitivity [22, 63] and Fisher information [34, 49] to evaluate parameter. Layer Adaptive Magnitude-based Pruning (LAMP) [23] improves magnitude pruning by using a rescaled score to automatically allocate sparsity, preventing layer collapse without manual tuning. Filter Pruning via Geometric Median (FPGM) [17] prunes channels that are closest to the layer’s geometric median, focusing on removing redundancies rather than magnitude. Inter-estingly, random pruning has been shown to perform competitively in some settings, ques-tioning the necessity of complex criteria [35]. Parallel to these, zero-cost proxies (ZCPs) estimate network quality without training. Representative methods include SNIP, GraSP, SynFlow, NASWOT, Fisher, Zico, and AZ-NAS, which use gradients, activation patterns, or parameter statistics to rank candidate ar-chitectures [1, 24, 25, 27, 39, 48, 51]. Although ZCPs have been widely studied in NAS, their application to structured pruning remains limited. Existing methods primarily focus on ranking entire, randomly initialized architectures rather than selecting specific structured components under strict global constraints. Furthermore, as we demonstrate in our empirical analysis, traditional untrained ZCPs can sometimes yield noisy signals when scaling channel search space (as shown in ablation). Bridging this gap is essential for a reliable training-free structured pruning framework. PruneNAS adopts the computational efficiency of ZCP but applies it via the more robust importance criteria on pre-trained weights. This enables a smooth and more reliable tabulation process for our search algorithm. 

## 2.2 Dependency Graph 

Graph-based structured pruning methods have recently gained attention for their ability to handle inter-layer dependencies in modern neural networks. In particular, DepGraph [13] models structural relationships as a dependency graph, enabling consistent pruning across operations such as residual connections and concatenations. This addresses a key limitation of earlier layer-wise pruning approaches [29, 41], which often ignore cross-layer constraints and may produce suboptimal or invalid architectures. By explicitly encoding these depen-dencies, DepGraph enables globally consistent pruning decisions while preserving network integrity. However, existing graph-based methods typically apply uniform pruning strategies across groups and rely on predefined importance criteria. In contrast, different groups may 4 SAH, CHHABRA, ROHMETRA, LI: PRUNENAS 

exhibit varying sensitivity to pruning, and our approach exploits this by adaptively select-ing pruning levels for each group under global resource constraints. Approaches based on soft masking and budget-aware regularization [26] enable end-to-end learning of structured sparsity under constraints, but often introduce additional optimization complexity. 

## 2.3 Neural Architecture Search and Structured Pruning 

Structured pruning is closely related to neural architecture search (NAS), with recent works increasingly formulating pruning as a search over channel configurations. One-Shot NAS methods such as AutoSlim [60], MetaPruning [36], Once-for-All [3], and Slimmable Net-works [62] navigate this combinatorial space by training weight-sharing super-networks or meta-networks from which sub-networks are subsequently extracted. Differentiable approaches relax channel selection and jointly optimize pruning decisions and weights [33], while ProxylessNAS [2] and FBNet [56] incorporate hardware metrics such as latency into the search objective. Pruning-as-Search (PaS) [30], TAS [10], and NAP [9] similarly cast pruning as an architecture-search problem using gradient-based or DARTS-style optimization. Many NAS methods additionally search over network structure or operators, which can complicate deployment on edge accelerators with limited operator support. LANA [43] re-places each layer with an operation drawn from a rich candidate pool, APQ [53] jointly searches architecture, pruning, and quantization, and CONNAS [5] extends ILP-based NAS to multiple hardware objectives. Training-free methods such as TTNAS [32] and PAT-NAS [57] still search over generalized supernetworks or randomly initialized architectures, while HyperNAS [38] contributes a performance predictor rather than a search algorithm. Closest to our work, PaS [30] also searches channel configurations, but learns its pruning policy by introducing depthwise-convolution layers; in contrast, PruneNAS searches the structured pruning space of a fixed pretrained model using importance scores and preserves the original operators for edge deployment. 

# 3 Method 

We propose a group-wise pruning-aware neural architecture search strategy that decomposes the global search problem into a training-free tabulation phase followed by a global con-strained optimization step. As illustrated in Figure 1, our method integrates a structured search space defined over layer groups, where each group comprises multiple layers and the search variables correspond to the number of channels retained or pruned within each group, together with zero-cost proxy tabulation and resource-aware constraint handling into a cohesive pipeline. Following a preliminary graph profiling step to partition the pretrained model into struc-turally valid pruning groups, the core of our approach centers on two primary phases. First, in the tabulation phase, we generate candidate networks by pruning one group at a time and estimate their resource reductions and accuracy trade-offs using an importance metric (e.g., Taylor). These candidates along with their KPIs are stored in lookup tables. 3.3.1 Resource Tabulation 

Resource tabulation is performed using metrics such as FLOPs, MACs, parameter count, latency,and memory (RAM) usage. Resource estimation is performed via profiling tools. The baseline model is used to establish the reference resource value Ro. We then construct the resource table by storing the delta resource values for all variations within each group relative to this baseline. For hardware aware tabulation, the model is executed on actual hardware to gather the latency, RAM, Flash and power budget, whereas hardware agnostic calculations use proxies such as latency, RAM and power. 

3.3.2 Accuracy Tabulation 

To efficiently estimate the baseline score S0 and the candidate scores Sg,k without the cost of fine-tuning, we explored the option to use zero-cost proxies (ZCP) methods such as SNIP [25] and GraSP [51] relying on gradient information, which approach zero at conver-gence for pretrained models and give noisy importance evaluation. Similarly, SynFLOW [48] is independent of training data, and does not make use of trained models. After careful com-parative analysis as explained quantitatively in section 4.3.1, we adopted importance criteria such as Hessian, [63], L1 weight magnitude, Taylor expansion [41] as a proxy to estimate the impact of pruning on performance of the model. We use the same method as an accu-racy estimator during the search phase to approximate the candidates as well as for channel pruning once the optimal configuration is finalized. Let Wc denote the set of weights associated with a specific channel c. We leverage channel-wise importance scores derived from the learned representations of a pretrained model. Specifically, the change in accuracy (delta accuracy) for a candidate is estimated as the sum of the importance values of the removed channels. 4.3.1 Accuracy Estimators 

Appendix Table 8 presents a comparison of top-1 accuracy achieved by different zero-cost estimators under a fixed 9 × MAC reduction constraint using a ResNet-18 model on the Flowers102 dataset. The dense (unpruned) baseline achieves a top-1 accuracy of 91.87%, providing a reference for evaluating the effectiveness of each estimator under aggressive compression. Taylor-based scoring achieves the highest top-1 accuracy of 82.79%, indicating its supe-rior ability to preserve salient network parameters during pruning. NWOT follows closely with 82.61%, demonstrating competitive performance despite its data-agnostic formulation. ## 4.4 Hardware Benchmarking 

We benchmarked pruned models generated using DepGraph [13] and PruneNAS on edge AI hardware (STM32H7 board) featuring 620 KB internal RAM, 16 MB external RAM, 64 KB internal flash, and 128 MB external flash. It is important to note that accesses to external RAM and flash incur significantly higher latency compared to internal memory. As a result, models that minimize reliance on external memory demonstrate superior runtime performance. Table 6 shows that, under 3 × and 6 × MAC reduction budgets, PruneNAS achieves la-tency improvements of 3.64 × and 6.42 ×, respectively, compared to the baseline. In contrast, DepGraph achieves latency improvements of only 2.40 × and 3.83 × under the same budgets. Furthermore, PruneNAS models exhibit lower RAM usage than DepGraph models at both 3× and 6 × reduction levels, which contributes to the additional speedup observed. On the other hand, DepGraph-pruned models tend to have smaller model sizes (i.e., lower flash re-quirements). ## 4.5 Limitations and Future Work 

While PruneNAS ensures strict constraint satisfaction and structural integrity by grouping dependent layers, our ILP formulation (Eq. 4) assumes independent pruning effects across groups. Furthermore, this work focuses on single-objective constraints and will transition to a unified joint-optimization solver in future work. Finally, we observe that under extreme compression regimes (e.g., < 0.15G MACs on lightweight models like MobileNetV2), one-shot pruning can suffer from severe degradation and requires iterative fine-tuning to recover performance. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, We presented PruneNAS, a structured pruning framework that efficiently gen-erates candidate architectures via training-free tabulation followed by a global search. On ImageNet, it maintains competitive accuracy on ResNet-50 while nearly halving the MACs required by prior approaches. Furthermore, it improves accuracy by 1% for ResNet, high-lighting the importance of searching for optimal, non-uniform channel distributions. At 9× MACs reduction on flowers, it yields up to an improvement of ∼ 20% over DepGraph on MobileNetV2. These savings translate directly to physical edge hardware, achieving up to a 6 .42 × reduction in inference latency and significantly lower RAM utilization on an STM32H7 microcontroller.
