# Federated TD Learning in Heterogeneous Environments with Average Rewards: A Two-timescale Approach with Polyak-Ruppert Averaging 

Ankur Naskar a, Gugan Thoppe a, Abbasali Koochakzadeh b, and Vijay Gupta b

Abstract — Federated Reinforcement Learning (FRL) provides a promising way to speedup training in reinforcement learning when one has access to multiple edge devices that can operate in parallel. Recently, it has been shown that even when these edge devices have access to different dynamic models—such as cars or robots with differing system configurations—an optimal convergence rate that has a linear speedup proportional to the number of devices is achievable. However, this result requires that the algorithm’s stepsize be chosen in a manner dependent on the unknown model parameters. Also, it applies only to a discounted setting, which has been argued to fit episodic tasks better than continuing control tasks. In this paper, we obtain finite-time bounds for heterogeneous FRL with average rewards. We show that the optimal convergence rate and the linear speedup is possible even with a universal stepsize choice, independent of the underlying dynamics. To achieve our result, we modify the existing one-timescale FRL method to a novel two-timescale variant that additionally incorporates iterate averaging. We remark that this idea also extends to other settings already studied in the literature, such as the homogeneous FRL or the heterogeneous case with a discounted cost function. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

With the proliferation of edge devices such as scanners, smartphones, medical devices, scientific instruments, and autonomous vehicles, there has been a massive growth in the amount of data that could conceivably be used for training Machine Learning (ML) models. However, transmitting this data from the edge devices to a single centralized location can easily overwhelm the network bandwidth. It can also raise privacy concerns, as the edge devices may not wish to reveal sensitive attributes embedded in their data. The paradigm of Federated Learning (FL) has evolved to address these concerns by allowing model training to be done primarily by the edge devices themselves [1], [2]. Fundamentally, FL consists of three steps that are repeated in a cyclic manner. First, the edge devices locally train an ML model. Next, the server gathers these models, possibly by sampling the devices, and then aggregates them. Finally, the server transmits this global model to the edge devices. The core challenge of FL is that the edge devices and their contributions may be heterogeneous. The most obvious form of this heterogeneity is that of data heterogeneity, meaning that the edge devices operate in distinct environments and collect data from distributions that are not identical to each

A branch of ML that has seen a surge of interest recently is Reinforcement Learning (RL) [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], wherein an agent is required to learn an optimal strategy (or a policy) for a control system. Formally, a Markov Decision Process (MDP) models the system dynamics such that the (probability distribution of the) future state of the system depends only on its current state and an action performed by an agent. After each action, the agent receives a scalar feedback, called the reward, that quantifies the immediate gains under that action. The overall goal in RL then is to find a policy for the agent that optimizes a suitable cumulative sum of the instantaneous gains. We remark that RL has been successfully used across a diverse set of applications including robot locomotion [8], the Atari [9] and Go games [10], autonomous vehicles, resource management in computer clusters [11], traffic light control [12], web system configuration [13], and personalized recommendations [14]. There are two main ways in which the above cumulative sum (of rewards) has been defined in the literature. One way is to look at a discounted sum of the instantaneous rewards (with some discount factor γ ∈ [0 , 1) ) over some time horizon. However, it has been argued that such discounting is suitable for episodic tasks but leads to high near-term performance rather than to high long-term performance in continuing or infinite-horizon control tasks [15], [16], [17]. The alternative is the average-reward setting, the focus of this work. Some recent works have merged the FL and RL paradigms and studied Federated Reinforcement Learning (FRL) [18], [19], [20], [21]. One advantage of FRL is that a ‘divide and conquer’ approach—to generate the usually enormous amount of data required to explore the different aspects of the environment—becomes conceivable. Moreover, the different edge devices can now perform computations in parallel, leading to a possible speedup in terms of the number of participating devices. Initial works show that this intuition is true at least in the case when each node has access to the same model for the system process. However, as stated above, heterogeneity is a key concern in FL. In FRL, this may manifest as different edge devices work-ing with (slightly) different dynamic models. For instance, to design a controller for an autonomous car, we can try to utilize data from several cars; however, every car operates in a different environment with different configurations. Similarly, even if multiple robots are operated under similar conditions, it is not possible to have all the parameters in their physical models to be identical. It is important to note that, due to the arXiv:2611.21370v1 [cs.LG] 16 Nov 2026 global aggregation step in FL, the designed controller may end up not being optimal for the dynamic model at any single edge device. Hence, one typically works with an alternative goal in heterogenous FRL, which is to find a ‘universal’ controller that performs well across all the edge models. An intriguing question is whether the speedup from the homogeneous model case can be achieved in the heterogeneous case as well for finding this universal controller. Some recent works [22], [23] have studied the above question. Specifically, [23] has considered policy evaluation with linear function approximation using Temporal-Difference (TD) learning and proved that the optimal convergence rate and a linear speedup persists even with heterogeneity. However, their setup is of discounted rewards, which as stated above, is less suitable for continuing control tasks than average rewards. Moreover, for the optimal rate, the algorithm in [23] is required to choose stepsizes in a manner that depend on the unknown dynamic models at the edge devices, which is practically infeasible. In this work, we consider heterogeneous FRL with an average-reward criterion and show that the optimal rate and the linear speedup are still possible. More significantly, we show that this speedup can be achieved by choosing stepsizes that do not depend on the unknown dynamics at the edge devices. A formal summary of our key contributions is as follows. We propose a novel two-timescale variant of TD learning, complemented with the Polyak-Ruppert averaging ,for policy evaluation in heterogeneous MDP settings with the 

average-reward criterion and linear function approximation. Our main result shows that our algorithm achieves the optimal rate with a linear speedup in iteration complexity in terms of the number of agents. Importantly, we show that this optimality occurs for a universal stepsize choice that is independent of the underlying dynamics. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We begin with formulating the problem in Section II. Our proposed algorithm and our main result (Theorem III.1) are given in Section III. We prove our main result in Section IV and conclude with some open directions in Section VII. II. S ETUP AND PROBLEM FORMULATION

In this section, we evaluate our proposed algorithm against the vanilla single-timescale federated TD algorithm obtained by combining the approaches of Wang et al. [23] and Zhang et al. [17], as described in Section III. Our experiments address three questions: (i) How does performance vary with the number of agents and the level of heterogeneity? (ii) How does the choice of stepsize affect performance? (iii) How does heterogeneity affect long-term convergence? The results show that our algorithm benefits from increasing the number of agents and achieves performance comparable to, and sometimes better than, the vanilla baseline, without requiring knowledge of unknown model parameters for stepsize selection. We also observe a residual error due to heterogeneity, consistent with our theoretical findings. 

A. Experimental Setup 

We consider the heterogeneous federated policy evaluation setting described in Section II. All agents share the same state and action spaces, with |S| = |A| = 100 , and use a common feature matrix Φ ∈ R100 ×20 . We consider four choices for the number of agents: N ∈ { 2, 5, 10 , 20 } and two levels of heterogeneity: ϵp = ϵr = 0 .1 and ϵp = ϵr = 0 .7. The reward and transition functions of the first agent’s MDP, M1, are generated randomly and kept fixed across experiments. For each choice of the heterogeneity parameters, the reward and transition functions of the remaining agents are generated randomly, subject to the bounds in Assumption A2.We compare our proposed Algorithm 1 with the vanilla single-timescale algorithm described in Section III. Our algorithm uses stepsizes 1/(t+1) and 1/(t+1) β , respectively, for updating the average-reward estimate rit and the value-function parameters θt, where β ∈ (0 , 1) . B. Effect of the Number of Agents and Heterogeneity 

Figure 1 compares the performance of our proposed algorithm with that of the vanilla single-timescale algorithm for different numbers of agents and levels of heterogeneity. In both heterogeneity settings, increasing the number of agents reduces the mean-squared error. Also, our algorithm achieves performance comparable to the vanilla algorithm, despite using stepsizes that do not depend on unknown parameters. These observations are consistent with Theorem III.1. In particular, increasing N reduces the stochastic estimation error, while the error due to heterogeneity need not vanish. Thus, the experiments illustrate the benefits of federated learning while highlighting the practical advantage of our model-independent stepsize selection. 

C. Effect of Stepsize Selection 

We next examine how the choice of the stepsize exponent β

affects the performance of our algorithm. Figure 2 shows the mean-squared error for different choices of β ∈ [0 .01 , 0.99] ,with N = 5 agents and two levels of heterogeneity. Each curve is averaged over 10 independent runs of 5000 iterations. We observe that smaller values of β generally lead to faster error reduction in both heterogeneity settings. This is interesting because Theorem III.1 guarantees the same asymptotic convergence rate order for every β ∈ (0 , 1) , while the experiments suggest that the finite-time performance can depend significantly on its choice. Determining an optimal choice of β remains an open question. 

D. Long-Term Behavior under Heterogeneity 

We finally examine the long-term behavior of the two algorithms in a heterogeneous environment. Figure 3 shows their performance over 200,000 iterations with N = 10 agents and ϵp = ϵr = 0 .7. We observe that the mean-squared error eventually levels off rather than converging to zero.
