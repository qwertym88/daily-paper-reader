# ReLoco: Unified Humanoid Locomotion and Fall Recovery via Behavior-Conditioned Motion Priors 

Lequn Fu 1, Yufeng Hu 1, Yijun Zhong 1, Jie Zhang 1, Xiao Li 1, Lingxiang Ji 1, Zhiyuan Xu 2,Jian Tang 2, Fellow, IEEE , and Shiqi Li 1,†

Abstract —Humanoid robots must recover from falls and resume commanded motion to sustain locomotion. Learning these behaviors in one policy requires supervision that ac-commodates both upright movement and ground-contact re-covery. We present ReLoco, a framework that uses behavior-conditioned adversarial motion priors to learn locomotion, get-up from arbitrary fallen postures, and command resumption within one proprioceptive policy. During training, robot posture selects the expert pool and conditions a shared discriminator with two behavior-specific outputs. Task rewards and shared regularization support command tracking and recovery, while temporary assistance facilitates exploration. A simulation-based refinement stage supplies robot-executed motion references for this supervision. Ablations examine conditional supervision, out-put separation, and reference quality under matched settings. At a common training iteration, the full policy achieves 96 .80% 

independent get-up success and 96 .96% push-recovery success, including command resumption. Its torso planar velocity root-mean-square error is 0.1266 m/s, 14 .7% lower than the condi-tional single-output variant, with similar joint-angle discrepancy measured by dynamic time warping. Deployment on the 64.5-kg Tiangong 3.0 humanoid demonstrates walking, running, get-up, and locomotion resumption after falls using one proprioceptive actor, without controller switching or on-robot policy fine-tuning. Index Terms —Humanoid robots, reinforcement learning, uni-fied locomotion control, fall recovery. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A humanoid robot that falls during locomotion must regain support, stand up, and continue its task. These abilities require coordinated motion across markedly different postures and contacts. Walking emphasizes command following and upright support, whereas recovery can involve rolling, arm support, and substantial torso rotation. A velocity command specifies the motion to be resumed, but does not describe how to establish support from a fallen posture. A single policy must therefore learn recovery actions that restore support before returning to velocity tracking. Legged control has advanced through model-based plan-ning [1], combined planning and learned tracking [2], and reinforcement learning (RL) in parallel simulation [3], [4]. Learned controllers have achieved adaptive bipedal locomo-tion [5], real-world humanoid walking [6], versatile dynamic

Sustained mobility additionally requires recovery when disturbances take the robot outside its nominal locomotion states. Human motion provides useful structure for whole-body coordination. Reference-tracking methods learn physically simulated skills from prescribed trajectories [12]–[14]. Ad-versarial motion priors (AMP) learn a motion reward from examples [15], while skill embeddings and conditional models support diverse behaviors [16]–[18]. Human-reference and generative-prior approaches extend motion supervision to hu-manoid locomotion [19]–[21]. Related systems demonstrate expressive and agile whole-body control [22]–[25], human-to-humanoid teleoperation [26]–[28], and contextual skills learned from video [29]. Retargeting addresses embodiment differences in human-to-robot transfer [30] and semantic con-sistency across characters [31]; recent frameworks further support humanoid retargeting and motion imitation [32], [33]. Recovery has also become a learned robot capability. HoST addresses standing-up exploration, optimization, and deploy-able motion through a force curriculum, multiple critics, and motion constraints [34]. Other learning-based recovery methods include HumanUP, HiFAR, and FRASA [35]–[37]. AHC consolidates behavior-specific policies through distilla-tion and RL fine-tuning [38], while X-Loco trains a vision-based generalist through specialist supervision [39]. These studies motivate learning recovery together with the motion that the robot must resume. After a disturbance, the robot’s configuration depends on the preceding gait, disturbance direction, and sequence of contacts, and need not coincide with a reference frame. Recovery must also begin from the velocity and support conditions produced by the fall, which can differ from those in ademonstration. AMP guides locally observed motion without prescribing frame-by-frame reference alignment [15], allowing the feedback policy to respond from the state actually reached. We present ReLoco (Recovery and Locomotion), a frame-work for unified locomotion and fall recovery through behavior-conditioned motion supervision. A posture-derived supervision route selects the locomotion or get-up reference pool. The discriminator scores motion histories together with their recorded conditions, using a shared feature network and two behavior-specific outputs. Posture-appropriate task rewards support command following or getting up, while shared penalties regulate actuation and joint motion across both routes. This formulation accommodates recovery-specific contacts and postures while retaining the velocity command arXiv:2611.23916v1 [cs.RO] 18 Nov 2026 2

that the robot must eventually track. To provide suitable references for this supervision, we refine retargeted human motions in simulation. Kinematic retargeting preserves human coordination but can introduce contact and joint-limit errors on the robot. An offline tracking policy refines locomotion and get-up motions under the contact and actuator models used for final policy training. The resulting robot-executed examples supply the expert data used to train the final controller through adversarial motion supervision. Recovery training covers configurations that need not align with individual reference frames. Reference-state initialization exposes the policy to fallen and transitional postures, while velocity perturbations generate falls during motion. Temporary assistance supports early recovery learning and is removed as training progresses. These settings provide experience for learning the transition back to commanded locomotion. De-ployment uses a proprioceptive actor with velocity commands, without the reference tracker, recovery assistance, or an exter-nally supplied behavior label. We evaluate the resulting policy through torso planar ve-locity tracking, motion similarity, independent get-up, and command resumption after disturbances. AMP ablations com-pare pooled and conditioned supervision; a conditional single-output comparison holds expert sampling and task objectives fixed to assess output separation. Reference-refinement exper-iments examine the effect of expert-motion quality. Indepen-dent get-up trials use settled fallen states, whereas disturbances during locomotion test recovery from moving states. Hardware tests on the 64.5-kg Tiangong 3.0 humanoid demonstrate walking, running, get-up from arbitrary fallen postures, and locomotion resumption after falls. The main contributions are: 1) A behavior-conditioned motion-prior scheme that selects reference pools using robot posture and scores motion histories with their conditions, supporting command tracking, fall recovery, and resumption of commanded motion within the same actor. 2) A simulation-based refinement procedure that converts retargeted locomotion and get-up motions into robot-executed references, reducing contact and joint-limit violations in the expert data used for adversarial super-vision. 3) Demonstration of locomotion, autonomous get-up from arbitrary fallen postures, and resumption of the com-manded motion after falls on a full-size humanoid using the same deployed proprioceptive policy. II. M ETHOD 

ReLoco trains one proprioceptive actor for locomotion, fall recovery, and resumption of commanded motion through behavior-conditioned AMP supervision. Robot posture selects the expert pool and conditions a shared discriminator with two outputs. Task rewards, shared regularization, and a recovery curriculum support policy optimization. Offline refinement of human motions supplies the robot-executed locomotion and get-up references used for this supervision. The discriminator and route labels are used only during training. D. Training Strategy 

Recovery training covers varied initial postures and transi-tions following a loss of balance. Reference-state initialization broadens posture coverage, velocity perturbations induce falls, and temporary assistance supports early exploration. 

Reference-state initialization. At reset, half of the environ-ments start from randomly chosen get-up frames, ranging from fallen to nearly upright. The other half sample from the full library of locomotion and recovery motions. We initialize the robot’s global pose and velocity, together with joint positions and velocities, from the selected frames. 

Push disturbance randomization. Training also includes translational and yaw-velocity perturbations. They occur inde-pendently in each environment, with intervals sampled from 

[15 , 20] s. The offsets are drawn from 

∆vx ∼ U (−5, 5) m /s, ∆vy ∼ U (−3, 3) m /s,

∆ωz ∼ U (−4, 4) rad /s. (15) These planar and yaw-velocity offsets can cause loss of balance and complete falls. 

Recovery assistance curriculum. External assistance has been used to facilitate humanoid standing-up exploration [34]. In our training setup, an upward force is applied to the robot’s head while it is non-upright:

E. Sim-to-Real Transfer 

Robot and actuator dynamics are identified following PACE [42]. Domain randomization covers uncertainty in contact, inertia, and actuation parameters (Table I(c)), with actuator-command delay randomized separately. Training uses these calibrated and randomized dynamics. Deployment runs the same proprioceptive actor throughout locomotion and recovery, without the reference tracker, dis-criminator, assistance force, or supervision router. III. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate how the unified policy combines commanded locomotion and fall recovery. Reference-refinement experi-ments assess the effect of expert-motion quality, while the AMP ablation distinguishes the joint effect of conditioning and expert routing from the additional effect of output sepa-ration. Recovery-only comparisons and hardware trials assess autonomous get-up and continuation of the commanded task after disturbances.

B. Simulation-Based Reference-Motion Refinement 

Kinematic retargeting provides reference poses but does not ensure that a robot can execute them. We compare 43 matched sequences before and after simulation-based refinement. The raw set Mraw contains the retargeted motions; Mref contains their refined, robot-executed counterparts. Table IV reports mean ± standard deviation across the 43 sequences. Refinement lowers all three mean constraint-violation rates to below 1% . Mean body-position jerk also decreases by 

35 .5% . To test whether cleaner references help learning, we train the same AMP setup once with raw references and once with refined references. Fig. 2 compares the first 20 ,000 

iterations. At 20 ,000 iterations, refined references yield a mean task reward of approximately 25 .5, compared with 22 .0 for raw motions, and logged get-up success of about 95% versus 85% .Together with the lower reference-violation rates, these results support using robot-executed motions as AMP references. The comparison uses one training run per condition.

E. Discussion 

Task recovery requires the robot to stand up and resume the commanded motion. The push-recovery tests therefore require renewed command tracking after standing up, and hardware trials demonstrate locomotion resumption after falls. Tracking accuracy and recovery capability must be consid-ered together when evaluating unified control. Task-only RL tracks velocity well but fails the independent get-up tests, while pooled AMP combines high recovery success with substantially higher tracking error. Conditioning and routed sampling improve tracking and reference similarity; output separation adds recovery gains within that setup (Table V). Reference refinement further supports this learning process by reducing violations in the expert motions. IV. C ONCLUSION 

We presented ReLoco, a framework that uses behavior-conditioned motion supervision to learn humanoid locomotion, fall recovery, and resumption of commanded motion within one proprioceptive policy. Robot posture conditions expert sampling and motion scoring across locomotion and recovery, with simulation-refined reference motions supporting policy learning. On the 64.5-kg Tiangong 3.0 humanoid, the deployed policy performs walking, running, autonomous get-up from arbitrary fallen postures, and resumption of commanded loco-motion after falls, without on-robot policy fine-tuning.
