# Approximate Dynamic Programming with Basis Function Learning 

Mohammadreza Fathi, Chi-Guhn Lee 

Dynamic Optimization and Reinforcement Learning (DORL) Lab, Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering, University of Toronto. m.fathi@mail.utoronto.ca, chiguhn.lee@utoronto.ca 

This work presents a novel approach for approximating the value function in Markov decision processes (MDPs). Our approach is grounded in the approximate linear programming (ALP) framework, wherein basis functions (BFs) play an important role. Traditionally, BFs have been pre-defined, randomly generated, or restricted to specific structures, which both limits the accuracy that can be achieved and leaves no mechanism for improving a given approximation. We instead learn BFs from the model itself, by repeatedly applying the Bellman operator to the current value estimate. We introduce the Bellman operator basis function (BOBF) learning method, and prove that each learned BF improves the approximation by a quantifiable amount and that the method converges to the optimal value function. Its shrunken counterpart (SBOBF) retains this guarantee while capping the number of BFs, and therefore the computational effort per iteration. For large-scale problems, where the number of constraints is prohibitive, we develop hybrid variants (HBOBF and SHBOBF) that combine constraint sampling with BF learning, and we show that the improvement and finite-termination guarantees are preserved under explicitly stated conditions. Numerical experiments on (continuous) perishable inventory and queueing control problems demonstrate that our BF learning techniques significantly outperform existing methods in the literature. 

Key words : Basis Function Learning; Bellman Operator; Approximate Dynamic Programming; Markov Decision Processes; Value Function Approximation. 

1. Introduction 

Exact linear programming (ELP) provides a powerful framework for solving Markov decision pro-cess (MDP) problems (Puterman (1994), Section 6.9). Its well-established theoretical properties have contributed to its extensive application in both operations research (Adelman and Mersereau (2008), Ye (2011), Wang (2020)) and artificial intelligence (Wang et al. (2007), Nachum and Dai (2020), Kamoutsi et al. (2021)). However, ELP becomes computationally intractable as the state and action spaces grow. The approximate linear program (ALP) framework was introduced to improve the scalability of ELP by reducing the number of decision variables (de Farias and Van Roy (2003)). ALP has since been applied across diverse domains, including network revenue management (Ke et al. (2019)), real

ALP employs a vector of basis functions (BFs) as the foundation of its approximation scheme. In most cases, BFs are predefined using domain knowledge or constructed by imposing specific structures, such as affine functions, as commonly seen in the literature. This limitation is not unique to the ALP framework. It is prevalent across the broader field of value function approximation (see Menache et al. (2005), Kveton and Hauskrecht (2006), and Kretchmar and Anderson (1999) for example). However, such predefined or parameterized BFs often result in poor approximation accuracy and substantial optimality gaps. Despite the critical role of BFs in ALP, the development of high-quality BFs has received limited attention. Recently, Pakiman et al. (2025) proposed a universal random approach for generating BFs, which is both cost-effective and easy to sample. However, their method requires a large number of BFs to guarantee convergence to the optimal value function, potentially exceeding the size of the state space in some cases. Moreover, the performance of the optimal policy derived from their approach is highly sensitive to the choice of a suitable state-relevance distribution, which determines the relative importance of each state in the approximation. In contrast, the convergence of our exact BF learning methods holds for any state-relevance distribution that is positive component-wise, with no simulation-based calibration required. Our study is closely related to the broader class of value-based reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, including Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan (1992)), double Q-Learning (Hasselt (2010)), actor-critic RL (Barto et al. (2012)), deep Q-networks (Mnih et al. (2015)), double Q-networks Van Hasselt et al. (2016), and soft actor-critic RL (Haarnoja et al. (2018)). Early RL methods typically offer convergence guarantees but suffer from limited scalability. In contrast, more recent approaches rely heavily on large-scale neural networks to achieve strong empirical performance, yet they often lack theoretical convergence guarantees. Several studies address value function approximation through the lens of approximate dynamic programming (ADP). For instance, Jiang and Powell (2015) proposed a method for approximating monotone value functions. Their approach estimates the value of sampled states and general-izes these estimates to unobserved states by exploiting the monotonicity of the value function. The method is proven to converge under certain conditions and achieves near-optimal solutions. Haskell et al. (2019) introduced empirical value learning algorithms for continuous-state MDPs that approximate the Bellman operator using simulation-based samples. Their approach combines this with universal function approximators constructed from randomly selected basis functions and provides convergence guarantees. In another application, Maxwell et al. (2010) developed an ADP method for ambulance redeployment to maximize timely responses to emergency calls. This Fathi and Lee: ADP with Basis Function Learning

BF generation for general MDP problems, beyond the ALP framework, has been explored through various methodologies, including directed graph Laplacian (Johns and Mahadevan (2007)), Krylov sub-spaces (Petrik (2007)), neighborhood component analysis (Keller et al. (2006)), diffu-sion models (Mahadevan and Maggioni (2007)), and Bellman residual BFs (Parr et al. (2007)). Our BF learning approach is most similar to Parr et al. (2007). However, instead of relying on the Bellman residual, we utilize the Bellman operator. Another stream of research seeks to eliminate the need for defining high-quality BFs in ALP. For example, Desai et al. (2012) reformulated ALP to allow constraint violations, introducing the concept of a violation budget to control the weighted sum of violations. By adjusting this budget parameter, the performance of the learned policy can be experimentally improved. However, guaranteeing tight optimality bounds under their approach requires prior knowledge of the state-visit distribution associated with the optimal policy. Building on this idea, Bhat et al. (2012) integrated kernel methods into ALP, thereby removing the necessity of explicitly defining BFs. Their approach demonstrated promising results in the Tetris game. Nevertheless, similar to Desai et al. (2012), it relies on an idealized sampling distribution to ensure near-optimality. Furthermore, their formulation results in a quadratic program, which is computationally more expensive than a linear programming problem. In contrast, our approach learns the BFs of ALP from the model itself, by applying the Bellman operator to the current value estimate, and it does so in two regimes. When the entire state–action pair can be enforced, we prove that the learned approximation converges to the optimal value function V ∗ and that the returned estimate is ϵ-optimal upon termination. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application-agnostic BF learning methodology for ALP in stochastic discounted MDPs carrying such a guarantee. When the state and action spaces are too large for that, we retain a sampled constraint set and prove that each learned BF still yields a certified improvement and that the procedure terminates after finitely many iterations. In both regimes, the distinguishing feature is the ability to improve a given approximation rather than merely to select one from a fixed span. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. 4. Constraint Sampling Scheme 

While our BF learning approaches offer promising theoretical implications, they are hindered by the large number of constraints in the ALP(Φ) problem, which must be solved at each iteration. In this section, we rigorously analyze a constraint reduction technique integrated into our BF learning framework and demonstrate its benefits. Previous studies have proposed various strate-gies to address the extensive constraint set in ALP(Φ), such as constraint sampling (de Farias and Van Roy 2004), column generation (Adelman 2007), and constraint violation learning (Lin et al. 2020). Among these, we adopt the constraint sampling method due to its simplicity and generalizability.

4.2. Sampling Design of X , Y, and Z

The two samples are governed by different design requirements. The set X underlies RLP(Φ, X )and is the object to which Proposition 1 applies. That guarantee is a scenario-optimization result whose proof bounds the number of support constraints of ˜ w by a symmetrization argument treating all M sampled constraints as exchangeable, which holds only under independent draws from a single common distribution (Calafiore and Campi 2006). Hence X must remain exactly what it was defined to be. The set Y plays no role in certifying a probabilistic feasibility guarantee. Its purpose is to make 

LY computationally tractable, so that it can be used to expand Φ. Since LY replaces the exact minimization over A with a minimization over YA(s) at each s ∈ Y S , the quality of the resulting operator depends on how densely YA(s) covers the action space at each retained state, not on any distributional property of how the states in YS were chosen. What is needed is only that YS be finite and that YA(s) be rich enough at each retained state. The sample Y should collect the states at which a high-quality value approximation is most desired, with YS as large and YA(s) as action-rich as computational resources permit. In this regard, any approach to construct Y is admissible. 4.6. Discussions 

Theorems 4, 5, and 6 together describe what the hybrid sampling scheme can, and cannot, deliver. The arguments behind Theorems 4 and 5 use the finiteness of S only through the finiteness of YS

and Z. The framework therefore provides at each basis-expansion step, an improvement certificate that holds with probability at least 1 − δ both in the classical finite-state setting and in continuous (see Section EC.1 of the e-companion for more details) or otherwise intractably large state spaces, where purely tabular LP-based ADP methods do not apply at all. Termination and per-iteration improvement do not, on their own, imply that the returned V is uniformly close to V ∗. By (21), the bound collapses toward a uniform guarantee precisely when 

ψ˜π (YS ) → 1, and that condition is less elusive than it appears. ψ˜π (s) ≤ Z ψ S (s) for any greedy policy ˜ π, where ψS is the state marginal of ψ, so covering supp( ψS ) covers supp( ψ˜π ) whichever policy the algorithm returns, and YS may be built accordingly. The genuine limitation is thus an accuracy–tractability tension rather than a structural disconnect. Driving ψ˜π (YS ) toward one calls for a dense YS , whereas the purpose of Y is to keep LY cheap. Two routes to relaxing it are sketched in Section EC.4.3 of the e-companion. More broadly, the paper decouples two roles that LP-based ADP has generally conflated: certi-fying feasibility of the approximate value function and generating basis functions through Bellman Fathi and Lee: ADP with Basis Function Learning

backups. One may therefore begin from a modest Φ 0, for which classical suboptimality bounds are available (de Farias and Van Roy 2003, 2004), and sharpen the approximation exactly where it is wanted, iteration by iteration, through Theorem 4. To our knowledge this unconstrained enlarge-ment of a deterministic basis-generating sample has not previously been exploited in the LP-based ADP literature, and we regard it as the central practical advantage of the framework. A comparison with the sample-complexity results known for Q-learning and temporal-difference learning is given in Section EC.5.4 of the e-companion.

6. Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced a BF learning approach for ALP and showed that it converges to the optimal value function V ∗, which is a novel contribution to the literature. Our methodology uses the Bellman operator as the foundation for two learning mechanisms, BOBF and SBOBF. We proved that appending a BOBF improves the approximation by at least the weighted Bellman error, together with a dual-weighted term scaled by γ/ (1 − γ), so that the improvement is strict whenever the Bellman error is non-zero. Iterating the construction returns an ϵ-optimal value estimate. The SBOBF approach caps the number of BFs retained and therefore the computational effort per iteration, while achieving convergence with performance comparable to BOBF. We then incorporated constraint sampling and developed the HBOBF and SHBOBF learning approaches, which substantially enhance scalability. Within this scheme we established, with prob-ability at least 1 − δ, a bound on the net approximation improvement obtained from a single HBOBF. We showed that the procedure terminates after finitely many iterations, and we bounded the Bellman residual at termination. That last bound is deliberately diagnostic rather than tight. It makes explicit that termination alone does not certify a small Bellman error uniformly over the state space. We discussed the conditions that can remedy this limitation in Section 4.6 and Section EC.4.3 of the e-companion. We compared our BF learning approaches against existing methods that rely on fixed or randomly generated BFs. In both applications considered, the PIC and QC problems, the gap of our methods decreases substantially within a few iterations for both ALP and RLP, whereas the benchmarks exhibit persistently high gaps. Two limitations point to future work. The first is a tension between accuracy and tractability. Tightening the residual bound calls for a dense curated sample, whereas the purpose of that sample is to keep the hybrid Bellman operator cheap. Extrapolating the values obtained on the curated states to unsampled ones, or learning a statistical surrogate for the operator off the curated set, would relax this tension, and working out the corresponding approximation-error and sample-complexity trade-offs is a substantial undertaking in its own right. The second is the constraint reduction technique itself. Alternatives such as constraint violation learning (Lin et al. (2020)) may prove complementary.

EC.1. Continuous State and Action Spaces 

Throughout the paper, all results are established for finite S and finite A. This section states what is required for each of them when S and A are continuous and compact, which of them extend unchanged, and where an extension is unavailable. One obstruction is that the concentrability constant of Assumption 2 is finite under mild support conditions when S is finite, whereas in the continuous case it additionally requires the discounted occupancy measure of the optimal policy to be absolutely continuous with respect to the sampling distribution. 

EC.1.1. Standing Assumptions 

The following conditions replace finiteness of S and A and are invoked individually below. (C1) Model regularity. S and A are compact, the cost function c is continuous, and the transition kernel p(· | s, a ) is weakly continuous. These conditions guarantee that the minimum in (1) is attained and that T maps bounded continuous functions to bounded continuous functions. (C2) Constraint qualification. ALP(Φ) admits at least one strictly feasible solution ¯ w, i.e. one at which all constraints hold strictly. The feasible region then has non-empty interior and Slater’s condition delivers the strong duality used in Property 2. (C3) Absolute continuity. The condition of Section EC.1.3, invoked wherever Assumption 2 is used. If S and A are continuous, we assume that each ϕi is continuous for all i = 1 , · · · , ℓ in Sections 2 and 3 of the main paper.

The bound (18) decomposes the guaranteed net improvement of an HBOBF into four terms, each admitting a distinct interpretation. The certification sample of the framework obeys M = Θ  ξ−1(ℓ ln ξ−1 + ln δ−1) + ℓ and does not depend on |S × A| . EC.8.1.1. Discussion on Sample Average Approximation Throughout the paper we assume that the expectation appearing in the constraints of each ALP and RLP, namely 

Es′∼p(·| s,a )[(Φ w)( s′)], can be evaluated exactly. This assumption is satisfied whenever the transition kernel has modest support, so that the expectation reduces to a finite sum over a manageable number of successor states. When the kernel is dense, exact evaluation is no longer practical, and the expectation must instead be approximated. The standard remedy is sample average approxi-mation (SAA), in which the expectation is replaced by an average over independent draws of the successor state from p(· | s, a ). This substitution is also standard practice in this literature: Paki-man et al. (2025) approximate the expectations in their self-guided ALP formulations in precisely this manner. Two features of our formulation make this approximation particularly well behaved. First, the constraints depend on the transition kernel only through expectations of bounded basis functions. It is therefore unnecessary to estimate the kernel itself, which would require a sample size growing with the size of the state space. It suffices to estimate a finite collection of scalar expectations, and the sample size required to do so accurately is independent of the size of the state space. This distinction was established for Markov decision processes by Kearns et al. (2002), who show via a standard concentration argument that a sample may approximate the successor-state distribution poorly and nonetheless yield an accurate estimate of the expectation of any fixed bounded function under it. Second, because our value function approximation is linear in its weights, these expectations do not depend on the decision variables. The sample may therefore be drawn once, prior to solving the linear program, and only finitely many expectations need to be estimated. We do not analyze sample average approximation rigorously within our framework. Quantifying the basis-generating operator and the scenario certificate under SAA is a separate undertaking that lies beyond the scope of this study and that the available space does not permit. The approximation itself, however, has been studied extensively, and the results most relevant to our setting are summarized below. The error introduced by sample average approximation has been examined from several direc-tions. The analysis of Petrik and Zilberstein (2009) is closest to our setting, in two respects. First, they study precisely the substitution described above: an approximate linear program in which the constraint coefficients are estimated from sampled transitions rather than computed exactly. They bound the resulting error by combining a concentration inequality for the estimated coefficients with a sensitivity argument for the linear program, and obtain a bound on the deviation between the solutions of the exact and sampled programs that depends on the number of samples per con-straint, the number of basis functions, and the discount factor, but not on the size of the state space. instructive. They enlarge the constraint set by replacing single-step constraints with multi-step, or rolled-out, constraints.
