Abstract — Learning human motion tracking policies through reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a core capability that underpins a majority of recent progress in humanoid whole-body control. However, achieving generalization to un-seen motions at high fidelity requires jointly scaling training to thousands of GPU and dataset hours. This work hypothesizes that motion tracking is inherently compute-bound because training on a diverse set of motions introduces learning in-terference, similar to multi-task RL. To tackle this, we propose Quantile Tracking Optimization (QTOP), which improves sam-ple efficiency by introducing a distributional advantage estimate conditioned on state and reference pairs. QTOP outperforms on and off-policy RL baselines both in simulation and real-world tracking scenarios on multiple datasets by up to 10%. Furthermore, we establish a scaling law for QTOP and find that, aside from scaling predictably, dataset size has minimal impact on performance for moderately sized GPU hour regimes.

behaviors in real time. Over the past few years, motion track-ing has rapidly evolved from learning individual skills [1], [2] to training generalist policies on thousands of motions [3], [4], [5], [6] with remarkable results on real robot hardware. A key enabler of modern trackers is the utilization of human motion datasets as a rich source of behavioral supervision as well as massively parallelized RL in simulation to converge to a stable and general controller. As a result, the central challenge is no longer learning individual motions, but optimizing a single policy over a heterogeneous collection of tracking tasks. While the desired behavior is explicitly specified through reference frames at high frequency, different motions can induce distinct state visitation and return distributions, making optimization challenging [7]. Conventional RL algorithms, such as PPO, typically aggregate these diverse learning signals through a single scalar value function, which can lead to overly con-servative policies [8]. The resulting tracking errors, although often small, can accumulate over the course of a rollout and become particularly detrimental for loco-manipulation tasks, where accurate global position, orientation and timing are required to grasp objects with high dexterity. arXiv:2611.20791v1 [cs.RO] 16 Nov 2026 A common strategy to eliminate such tracking errors is to scale both motion datasets and simulation throughput [4], [6], [9]. Larger datasets expose policies to a broader range of be-haviors, while increased compute enables faster convergence to robust imitation. This scaling recipe has proven highly effective, but it requires substantial compute resources of over 10 4 GPU hours, which is out of reach for most robotics researchers. Secondly, it does not address whether additional data and compute contributes equally to performance. We hypothesize in this work that general motion tracking is inherently multi-task, which makes the task compute-bound even at small dataset scales. To address this, we introduce 

Quantile Tracking Optimization (QTOP), a PPO-based training recipe for large-scale humanoid motion tracking that combines Muon optimization with a distributional value critic. Instead of assigning credit using only a scalar return residual, QTOP estimates the return distribution conditioned on each observation-reference pair and reinforces rollouts according to how favorable their realized returns are relative to this local distribution. This converts tracking difficulty across motions and contact regimes into a calibrated advan-tage signal, enabling more effective policy improvement. Using QTOP, we study how motion-tracking performance scales jointly with training compute and motion-dataset size. We construct nested subsets of the same motion corpus for multiple seeds and train policies over a grid of dataset sizes and optimization budgets, allowing us to separate the marginal benefit of reference data from additional RL compute. Across the low-to-moderate data regime considered in our experiments, we find that increasing the dataset size improves performance only when paired with sufficient com-pute, while additional training compute remains the dominant bottleneck. Together with existing results at substantially larger data scales [6], our findings suggest that an important understudied direction is not only how to collect larger motion corpora, but how to design learning algorithms that can effectively exploit them. Finally, we evaluate QTOP against strong PPO baselines and representative off-policy alternatives on both academic held-out tracking benchmarks and our own motion capture dataset. QTOP consistently achieves the best tracking per-formance and transfers to improved real-world tracking on a Unitree G1 under a fixed data and training budget. This points to learning algorithms that can more effectively exploit large reference-conditioned motion datasets as an important direction for future research. A. Motion Tracking 

Learning physical controllers of humanoid characters is a deeply studied problem in computer graphics and robotics. Foundational works quickly evolved from basic RL formula-tion and single clip learning [10], [11], [12], to larger scale imitation [13]. This has sparked great interest in improving and deploying trackers for real robot hardware, yielding expressive and dynamic replay across a wide variety of motions and robots [14], [1], [15], [16], [3], [17], [5]. Nonetheless, learning a unified tracker that accurately imitates novel, unseen motions remains challenging. Recent works have increasingly turned to scale as a solution. SONIC [4] demonstrates the benefits of scaling up data collection and compute budgets, and Humanoid-GPT [6] further expands on this philosophy by spending spending 75% of a 15 , 000 GPU hour budget on training 384 separate PPO experts before distilling it into a single model. More closely related to ours, concurrent work Scaling BFM [9] studies how tracking performance scales with increased GPU budgets and dataset sizes. In contrast, we study the algorithmic foundations of scaling in limited compute regimes of up to 1, 300 GPU hours, introducing QTOP and fitting explicit scaling rela-tionships to disentangle how tracking performance depends jointly on dataset size, training compute, and the learning algorithm. 

B. Massively Parallelized RL 

Massively parallel RL utilizes multiple simulation envi-ronments to speed up rollout collection alongside a learning policy [18], [19]. Across most tasks, PPO is the most com-monly chosen algorithm due to its stability, scaling properties [20], and highly optimized implementations [21]. While, on the other hand, off-policy methods struggle to scale out of the box [22], there has been a greater effort to revisit off-policy methods for parallel simulated robotics tasks [23], [24], [25], [26] due to its sample efficiency. Akin to motion tracking, multitask RL introduces an additional problem of task interference [7]. However, while PPO is the dominant paradigm for motion tracking, most multi-task RL works focus on using off-policy algorithms such as SAC [27] and combine it with additional strategies such as gradient surgery [28], [29] or return normalization [30]. TOPPO [31] similarly builds on on-policy PPO, but tar-gets discrete multitask RL benchmarks, whereas we address interference within motion tracking, where a continuously changing reference induces a smooth continuum of objectives rather than explicitly separated tasks. IV. EXPERIMENTS We evaluate QTOP along three dimensions. Firstly, we compare tracking performance in simulation against strong on-policy and off-policy RL baselines under a fixed compute budget. Secondly, we establish a scaling law with respect to motion dataset and RL compute hours. Finally, we deploy the resulting policies on a Unitree G1 humanoid and evaluate real-world tracking behavior. For our training dataset, we use the union of AMASS [36] and LAFAN1 [37]. Although this corpus is significantly smaller than those used by recent large-scale systems [4], [6], our objective is not to build a state-of-the art foundation model, but to isolate the effect of the learning algorithm under limited data and compute budgets. As shown in Section IV-B, even within this com-paratively small data regime, tracking performance requires substantial compute to approach saturation.

B. Scaling Law 

Experiment Setup. The previous experiments evaluate QTOP under a fixed compute budget. However, an effective learning algorithm should not only perform well at a single operating point, but should also continue to improve as additional resources become available. Inspired by the vast literature on scaling laws for language modeling [40] and, more recently, reinforcement learning [20], [41], we study how the tracking performance of QTOP scales jointly with respect to training compute and motion data. Specifically, we fit a predicted error surface as 

E(C, D) = E0 + ADγ C−α + BD−β ,

where (C) denotes GPU-hours and (D) denotes dataset hours. The constant E0 represents an irreducible error floor. Traditionally, scaling laws do not feature the interaction component Dγ , but we include it due to providing a better fit that better captures optimization error. Overall, ADγ C−α

always decreases with compute, but increases with dataset size as a larger motion corpus can require more optimization, while BD−β captures the typical behavior of data scaling. We focus on compute and dataset size while holding the model architecture fixed, allowing us to isolate which resource provides the larger marginal improvement. Prior motion-tracking work has generally increased data and com-pute simultaneously, making their individual contributions difficult to disentangle. SONIC [4], for example, reports separate curves for increasing dataset size and increasing compute, but does not evaluate an interaction over a com-plete compute and data grid. Our study instead varies both quantities systematically, enabling us to characterize their joint effect and compare the benefits of allocating additional resources to either data or optimization. 

Training and evaluation split. We construct three training sets containing approximately 4, 12 , and 24 hours of motion data, where the 24 -hour set corresponds to the full training corpus used in the previous experiments. For the 4- and 12 -hour settings, we independently sample three subsets of the corresponding size from the full corpus to eliminate sampling bias. We train QTOP three times for different seeds at each dataset size and evaluate checkpoints at log-spaced compute intervals. At each checkpoint, we then measure R-MPKPE on the held-out MOSAIC test set and average the metric across Fig. 4. D. Real World Evaluation 

Finally, we investigate whether QTOP’s simulation gains translate to improved tracking on physical hardware. To do so, we deploy our PPO and QTOP policies on a Unitree G1 humanoid, with policy inference running at 50Hz on an external workstation. We evaluate on 32 diverse motions from our inertial motion-capture dataset, ranging from 5 to 

15s in duration. The selected motions form a representative and diverse subset including walking in different styles, shelf restocking, whiteboard cleaning, playing pool and cooking. For each trial, the robot is initialized at the first reference frame and tracks the motion until completion while its achieved pose is recorded. Each motion is repeated three times for both methods using checkpoints trained on our 24-hour motion dataset with 10 3 GPU-hours of compute. Figure 6 summarizes the resulting real-world tracking accuracy in terms of R-MPKPE and absolute joint error (joint MAE), averaged across trials. We note that joint MAE is computed over all actuated joints, whereas R-MPKPE is evaluated only on the key bodies included in the training re-ward. We find that QTOP significantly improves both metrics over PPO, reducing R-MPKPE by approximately (8%), with a smaller but consistent improvement in joint MAE. These results indicate that the tracking improvements obtained by

Finally, we qualitatively evaluate both policies by replay-ing challenging out-of-distribution motions, such as martial arts sequences in Figure 7. While both policies produce robust behavior overall, we find that the PPO policy tends to rely on additional stabilization steps during high-velocity up-per body movements, leading to a substantial positional drift over time. In this scenario, the differences are more visually subtle, but we find that the marginal tracking gains by QTOP can significantly improve the positioning of the robot end effectors. As a result, we conclude that even modest improvements in tracking accuracy can translate into meaningful gains for real-world loco-manipulation tasks. V. D ISCUSSION 

We introduce QTOP, a motion tracking algorithm that con-sistently improves over strong on- and off-policy baselines in simulation and transfers these gains to real hardware. Our scaling analysis further shows that, within the measured regime of 4–24 dataset hours and up to approximately 1, 300 

GPU-hours, tracking remains primarily compute-limited, as increasing optimization compute generally yields larger gains than adding reference data. The main limitation of our work is that, due to limited resources, our experimental regime is one magnitude smaller in compute than state-of-the-art trackers. We therefore cannot yet determine whether the fitted relationship or QTOP’s advantages persist at substantially larger data and compute scales. In particular, the importance of dataset size may increase once policies receive sufficient compute to exploit larger and more diverse motion cor-pora. Rather than advocating for scaling data and compute indiscriminately, our results motivate greater attention to algorithms that handle the continuous multi-task structure of motion tracking more efficiently. We view QTOP and our compute-data analysis as a starting point for developing such methods and for studying scaling behavior at larger regimes. ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS
