Abstract 

One fundamental challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) is enforcing temporal safety constraints, such as satisfying formal specifications. Existing methods often transform logic formulas into automata for state augmentation and reward shaping. However, this approach assumes that automaton construction is both feasible and tractable, which limits its applicability to small specifications and linear temporal logic (LTL). This paper proposes Actor-Predictor, a framework that learns a surrogate representation of logic progress without explicitly constructing an automaton. The predictor continuously estimates whether a partial trajectory will satisfy the formal specification in a later step, and its learned features are provided as auxiliary inputs to the actor, which is then optimized using GRPO and RWR. We show theoretically that, under sufficient predictive accuracy, the learned representation is equivalent to the automaton state. Experiments on grid world and robotic tasks in Isaac Lab demonstrate that Actor-Predictor outperforms automaton-based baselines on LTL-constrained tasks. It also performs well on non-regular formal specifications, such as panbnq, for which finite-automaton-based approaches are not applicable. 

# 1 Introduction 

Formal specification is a framework for describing complex, time-dependent behaviors, such as safety constraints. Reinforcement learning (RL) with formal specifications is a data-driven approach to train an agent to learn policies that optimize objectives described by temporal logic formulas or non-regular specifications, which faces two fundamental challenges. First, formal specifications introduce long-range temporal dependencies, meaning that the optimal policy is generally non-Markovian, which may depend on the history of observations and actions rather than solely on the current environment state. Second, formal specification objectives yield binary, trajectory-level feedback, causing extreme reward sparsity that results in challenging cold-starts. The standard approach to overcome this challenge is through automata theory and reward shaping (Shah et al., 2024; Manganaris et al., 2025), which converts a temporal logic (TL) formula into a finite-state automaton and then optimizes the TL objective over the product state space including both the automaton state and the environment state. It often further modifies the reward function to provide dense feedback throughout the trajectory. However, it has been shown that translating an LTL formula into a limit-deterministic Büchi automaton (LDBA) can be computationally infeasible, where in the worst case, the complexity is double-exponential in the number of Atomic Propositions (APs) (Sickert et al., 2016). Furthermore, any language requiring an arbitrary counter is non-regular and, therefore, cannot be represented by a finite automaton. 1arXiv:2611.26313v1 [cs.AI] 19 Nov 2026 This paper takes a different approach. Our algorithm learns a surrogate representation of the automaton state using the features from a predictor that predicts the future satisfaction probability of a given partial trajectory. The intuition behind this approach is that an agent’s progress through an automaton uniquely maps to a probability of reaching a future accepting state. The predictor network can be trained from a set of collected trajectories with binary satisfactory labels. The feature of the predictor, when given a partial trajectory, can represent the automaton state, thus avoiding the construction of a finite-state automaton. This leads to our proposed Actor-Predictor approach. 

1.1 Main contributions 

The key contributions of this paper include: • Theoretical Guarantees: For deterministic automata with accepting states, we prove that: (i) a well-trained predictor’s hidden representation sufficiently captures the automaton state (Lemma 1); and (ii) policy optimization over the surrogate product state yields an optimal policy for the formal specification objective (Theorem 1). • Algorithm & Evaluation: We implement Actor-Predictor using a Mamba predictor (Gu and Dao, 2024) and a GRPO-optimized actor (Guo et al., 2025) with exponential reward weighting. Across quadruped navigation (LTL) and grid-world (non-regular) tasks, experiments show: (1) Actor-Predictor outperforms baselines like CyclER (Shah et al., 2024), (2) Mamba predictors outperform Transformer and LSTM baselines (Vaswani et al., 2017; Graves, 2012); (3) learned representations exhibit transferability and subformula compositionality; and (4) predictor features empirically suffice to reconstruct the automaton state. 

# 2 Related Work 

Combining formal specification with reinforcement learning to provide a formal framework for safety and task satisfaction is an active topic in RL (Li et al., 2017; Bozkurt et al., 2020; Hasanbeig et al., 2020, 2019; Li et al., 2018; Cai et al., 2021; Chou et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). Temporal logic enables specifications over trajectories as a time-ordered sequence of steps. The standard approach to address RL in formal specification is to convert the Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formula into a limit-deterministic Büchi automaton (LDBA). A product Markov decision process (PMDP) is then constructed by combining the system’s state space and the Büchi Automaton, yielding the product state space Sϕ “ S ˆ B. This transformation renders the task Markovian, enabling stationary policies over the augmented state space. Using the LDBA, methods such as Automaton Constrained Q-Learning (ACQL), Cycle Experience Replay (CyclER), and LTL-guided Counterfactual Experience Replay (LCER) leverage progress within the automaton to automatically densify the reward signal (Shah et al., 2024; Manganaris et al., 2025; Voloshin et al., 2023). Although these methods improve learning efficiency, they fundamentally rely on access to the explicit structure and observability of the automaton. This restricts the expressivity of formal logic to regular languages. Consequently, tasks requiring arbitrary counting form non-regular languages and cannot be solved using these approaches. Our approach is inspired by predictive state representations (PSRs) for POMDPs (Littman and Sutton, 2001). In PSR, the latent state is represented by predictions of a collection of future tests, where each test corresponds to a sequence of future observations. # 4 An Actor-Predictor Approach 

We name our architecture Actor-Predictor , which consists of a neural network predictor and an actor as shown in Fig. 1(a). At step k, the predictor takes the entire history τk “ p s1, a 1, s 2, a 2, ¨ ¨ ¨ , s kq as input and estimates the probability that, under the current policy, the formal specification φ will be satisfied in a later step (after step 

k). Note that the predictor predicts future satisfactions, not past ones. Specifically, let the feature vector output (second-to-last layer) of the predictor neural network be ϕθpτkq, where θ is the parameter, and let σp¨q be the logistic function. The predicted probability of satisfaction is σ `ϕθpτkqJw˘ where w are the linear weights of the last layer. For the actor, denoted as a probability distribution πψ where ψ is the actor parameter, it takes the feature 

ϕθpτkq of the predictor and the state sk as input. Then, it outputs an action sampled from πψpϕθpτkq, s kq. The predictor is trained with a binary cross-entropy loss, and the actor is trained using GRPO with reward-weighted regression (RWR). The full algorithm is summarized in Alg. 1. Intuitively, to accurately predict the eventual satisfaction probability of the partial trajectory, the predictor feature should expressively represent useful formal specification features such as the automaton state. Integrating these features into the RL state as the input of the actor should reduce the history-dependent formal specification based decision-making problem to a stationary MDP problem, where the optimal policy is Markovian. We will prove this property under mild conditions.

Third, Mamba’s computational complexity scales linearly with sequence length during training and remains constant per step during inference. 6.3 Ablation 

We ablate key design choices in our method: the number of prediction heads, the predictor architecture, and exponential weighting. RL with formal specification is challenging because of the sparse reward nature. We propose Actor-Predictor, a framework that learns a surrogate representation of logic progress without knowledge or observability of the automaton. We show theoretically that, with sufficient predictive accuracy, the learned representation is equivalent to the automaton state. Empirically, Actor-Predictor outperforms automaton-based baselines on LTL specifications without access to the automaton, learns non-regular specifications such as L “ t anbnu, and learns environmental features that can be transferred across specifications.

# B Experimental Details 

B.1 Hardware 

All experiments were performed on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell. 

B.2 Details 

We began from standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Adam defaults, together with the values reported in the Cycler Experience Replay (CyclER) paper, and adjusted them to perform well across all algorithms. All experiments in both the grid world and Quadruped environments were run with random seeds 0–4. One-step temporal-difference (TD) returns were used to estimate the advantage for vanilla PPO and PPO with Eventual Discounting. PPO with Eventual Discounting was used with the reward shaping of CyclER and LTL-guided Counterfactual Experience Replay (LCER). 

Grid World The grid world had a batch size of 4096 environments, the maximum that fit on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell. We tested time horizons of {350, 400, 500, 600, 700} and found 400 to be the shortest horizon that consistently scaled with n. The actor learning rate was swept over {2e-5, 4e-5, 6e-5, 8e-5, 1e-4, 3e-4, 5e-4}. The entropy bonus was swept over {1e-5, 1e-4, 1e-3, 5e-3, 1e-2}. The clip ratio was tested over {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, and PPO epochs over {2, 3}. The discount factors tested were 0.99 and 0.999. β was tested at {1,2,4,6,7,8,10,12}, with 12 β “ 10 giving the best performance. We used a Lagrangian λ of 10, guided by the original CyclER paper.
