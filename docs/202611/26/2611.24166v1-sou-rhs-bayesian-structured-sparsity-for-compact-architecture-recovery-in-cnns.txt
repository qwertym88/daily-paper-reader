# SoU-RHS: Bayesian Structured Sparsity for Compact Architecture Recovery in CNNs 

Rafael Mouallem Rosa rafael.mouallem-rosa@inria.fr Univ. Grenoble Alpes, Inria, CNRS, Grenoble INP, LJK, 38000 Grenoble, France 

Julyan Arbel julyan.arbel@inria.fr Univ. Grenoble Alpes, Inria, CNRS, Grenoble INP, LJK, 38000 Grenoble, France 

## Abstract 

Highly parameterized convolutional neural networks often achieve strong predictive per-formance, but their memory, computation, and deployment costs motivate the search for methods that recover compact architectures from large reference models. We propose SoU-RHS, a Bayesian structured-sparsity procedure that augments Regularized Horseshoe Shrinkage (RHS) with the so-called Sources of Uncertainty Sparse Global-Local mechanism (SoU-SGL). The resulting model sharpens the separation between active and inactive filters, providing an effective mechanism for architecture recovery. A key property of SoU-RHS is stability: across different overparameterized starting widths and under a reasonable range of pruning thresholds, it recovers compact architectures with similar total size and layer-wise allocation. This supports the interpretation that the procedure is identifying a compact structure adapted to the data, rather than merely compressing each initial model separately. Across standard image-classification benchmarks, SoU-RHS recovers substantially smaller architectures than deterministic structured-sparsity baselines, while maintaining competi-tive accuracy, achieving lower negative log-likelihood in every evaluated configuration and generally lower expected calibration error. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recovering compact architectures. Deep neural networks are often trained in highly parameterized regimes. Although such models can achieve strong predictive performance, they also incur substantial costs in memory, computation, and deployment. As machine learning systems are used at increasing scale, these costs are no longer secondary considerations, which has intensified the search for models that are not only accurate but also compact and efficient. In many cases, such highly parameterized models are effectively overparameterized for the task at hand. An appealing alternative to manual architecture design is therefore to recover a compact architecture from a large reference model. Rather than postulating a small network a priori, one may start from a large model and let the data determine which structural components are actually needed. 

Structured sparsity for architectural simplification. This naturally brings structured sparsity into focus. Not all forms of sparsity lead to meaningful reductions in computational cost: unstructured sparsity removes individual weights, but often leaves the overall architecture unchanged. By contrast, structured sparsity removes entire architectural components, thereby simplifying the network itself. It is this architectural simplification, rather than sparsity per se, that most directly translates into computational savings. 

From Bayesian shrinkage to robust filter selection. In the convolutional setting considered here, compact architecture recovery is most directly posed as deciding which filters to retain. A natural Bayesian 1arXiv:2611.24166v1 [cs.LG] 18 Nov 2026 approach is then to place a horseshoe-type shrinkage prior on filter-wise components. The horseshoe prior was originally introduced for sparse-signal settings (Carvalho et al., 2010), encoding the prior belief that many coefficients are negligible while a few strong signals should remain active. In our setting, the same logic is transferred to filters: some filters are expected to be essential, while others should be shrunk toward inactivity. However, shrinkage alone does not necessarily produce sparsity that is sufficiently pronounced for robust pruning. Horseshoe shrinkage can strongly reduce weakly supported filters while still leaving them at small nonzero magnitudes. The Sources of Uncertainty Sparse Global-Local mechanism (SoU-SGL) (Mouallem Rosa et al., 2026) addresses this by selectively driving the local shrinkage scales toward an optimization-like treatment, producing a clearer separation between active and inactive filters. This is essential for compact architecture recovery, since the goal is not only to reduce filter magnitudes but also to robustly identify which filters should remain in the final architecture. 

Contributions. We propose SoU-RHS, a Bayesian structured-sparsity approach for compact architecture recovery in convolutional neural networks (CNNs). SoU-RHS combines filter-wise regularized horseshoe shrinkage with SoU-SGL: • It produces pruning scores that more clearly separate inactive and active filters. • Across standard image-classification benchmarks, it recovers substantially smaller architectures than deterministic structured-sparsity baselines, and its recovered widths are more stable across pruning thresholds and overparameterized starting widths. • These compact architectures maintain competitive accuracy relative to the baselines while achieving consistently lower negative log-likelihood and generally lower expected calibration error than the deterministic baselines. • Retraining on the recovered architectures further reveals an architecture–inference interaction, with deterministic methods deteriorating while Bayesian models remain strong or improve. 

Outline. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents the baseline models and introduces SoU-RHS and its filter-level pruning mechanism. Section 4 describes the experimental setup, and Section 5 presents results on architecture recovery, predictive perfor-mance, and retraining. Section 6 discusses limitations, and Section 7 concludes. Appendix A examines the trade-off between posterior sampling and predictive cost. 

## 2 Related work 

Deterministic structured sparsity in CNNs. Within CNNs, structured sparsity is often operationalized by pruning filters or channels, thereby turning sparsity into an explicit change in architecture. One line of work follows a train–prune–fine-tune pipeline, removing filters or channels according to magnitude, reconstruction, or saliency criteria (Li et al., 2017; He et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2017; Molchanov et al., 2017). Another approach induces structured sparsity during training through explicit regularization. Structured Sparsity Learning (SSL) uses group-lasso penalties over predefined structures such as filters and channels (Wen et al., 2016), while Network Slimming promotes channel sparsity through an ℓ1 penalty on batch-normalization scaling parameters (Liu et al., 2017). Related approaches such as MorphNet and AutoSlim formulate channel-width selection as resource-constrained structure learning or architecture search (Gordon et al., 2018; Yu & Huang, 2019). More recent work addresses dependencies between structures across layers. DepGraph (Fang et al., 2023) groups coupled parameters for consistent structural pruning across diverse architectures. Such dependencies are also relevant when extending filter-wise shrinkage to networks with residual connections. 

Bayesian structured sparsity. Bayesian approaches to structured sparsity differ mainly in where the sparsity-inducing random variables enter the network. One line places dropout-like multiplicative noise directly on structural units, such as neurons or channels, and prunes units whose scales collapse toward zero 2(Neklyudov et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2024). Another line places global-local shrinkage priors on groups of weights associated with structural units, allowing entire weight groups to be shrunk toward inactivity (Louizos et al., 2017; Ghosh et al., 2018; 2019). Our work follows this second line: we apply regularized horseshoe shrinkage to filter-wise weight groups in CNNs and use SoU-SGL to sharpen the separation between active and inactive filters. Spike-and-slab approaches make structural selection explicit. Jantre et al. (2025) combine group Lasso and group horseshoe shrinkage with spike-and-slab priors to induce node sparsity in Bayesian neural networks. Our approach instead retains continuous filter-wise shrinkage and sharpens structural decisions through the inferential treatment of local scales. Recent Bayesian methods also connect pruning to model evidence. BMRS (Wright et al., 2024) combines multiplicative-noise models with Bayesian model reduction, including a variant that avoids pruning-threshold tuning. SpaM (Dhahri et al., 2024) uses marginal-likelihood optimization to encourage sparsifiability and derives a pruning criterion from Laplace posterior precision. Tazawa & Isomura (2026) connect synaptic pruning with online Bayesian model selection, using connectivity parameters to remove uninformative connections in generative models. Their work shares our view of pruning as statistical structure learning, while addressing individual connections rather than filter-level architecture recovery in CNNs. These approaches provide relevant alternatives to our use of local-scale uncertainty to stabilize filter selection. 

Pruning as architecture selection. Liu et al. (2019) show that, in their structured-pruning experiments, training recovered architectures from scratch can match or outperform fine-tuning inherited weights. This motivates treating pruning as architecture selection and evaluating the recovered structure independently of its learned weights. Our retraining study further examines how the value of a recovered architecture depends on the inference procedure.

This decomposition is particularly useful here because the different model components play different roles: the local scales τℓc govern the filter-specific structural decision, whereas the remaining components govern the predictive model and its shrinkage hierarchy. SoU-SGL exploits this distinction by setting the local-scale confidence near zero, γτ ≈ 0, while keeping γβ = γν = γc = 1, corresponding to the standard Bayesian treatment for the remaining components. The local scales are therefore driven toward an optimization-like regime while the rest of the model remains probabilistic, yielding an “optimize structure, infer parameters” configuration. Applying this SoU-SGL specification to the regularized horseshoe defines our proposed model, SoU-RHS. In SoU-RHS, the regularized horseshoe prior and the pruning score sℓc = τℓc νℓ remain unchanged; what changes is the inferential treatment of the local scales. The near-zero confidence drives q(τℓc ) to concentrate more sharply around its data-driven solution. For weakly supported filters, this limits posterior mass at larger local-scale values that would otherwise allow them to escape the global shrinkage, driving sℓc close to zero. Supported filters, in contrast, retain local scales large enough to escape the global shrinkage. This selective collapse of the local-scale posterior therefore yields a clearer separation between active and inactive filters, turning horseshoe shrinkage into a more decisive architecture-selection mechanism. Unlike ℓ1-based methods such as SLIM and SSL, where λ directly tunes the strength of the sparsity penalty and therefore the compression–accuracy trade-off, γτ is not used as a sparsity-strength parameter. We fix it near zero to sharpen the local-scale posteriors and the resulting filter-level structural decisions; the amount of pruning then emerges from the fitted global-local hierarchy. ## 4 Experiments 

Datasets. We evaluate on four standard image-classification datasets: CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, and Tiny-ImageNet. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 are 32 ×32 natural-image datasets with 10 and 100 classes, respectively. SVHN is a 32 ×32 real-world digit-image dataset with 10 classes. Tiny-ImageNet is a more 5challenging 64 ×64 natural-image dataset with 200 classes. We use the standard training and test splits and do not use a separate validation split for checkpoint selection. 

Architecture and overparameterized widths. All methods are implemented on the same VGG-style backbone with three convolutional blocks, each of the form Conv → ReLU → Conv → ReLU → MaxPool .

All convolutions use 3 × 3 kernels with stride 1 and same padding. The final convolutional representation is followed by global average pooling and a linear classifier. We consider three overparameterized starting architectures, where all convolutional layers are assigned the same nominal width, equal to 128, 256, or 512 filters. 

Baselines. We compare SoU-RHS against four baselines in the main comparison: a standard deterministic neural network (NN), a Bayesian neural network (BNN) with a Gaussian prior, and the structured-sparsity methods Network Slimming (SLIM) and Structured Sparsity Learning (SSL), each evaluated with regularization strengths 10 −3 and 10 −4. We additionally include RHS in the stability analysis to isolate the effect of the SoU-SGL mechanism. All methods use the same backbone. 

Training and checkpointing. All models are optimized with Adam using batch size 128 and learning rate 10 −3. We do not use data augmentation or dropout. Deterministic models are trained for 500 epochs, whereas Bayesian models are trained for 4000 epochs. For Bayesian models, we use KL annealing: the KL coefficient starts at zero, increases linearly by 5 × 10 −4 per epoch, and is capped at 0 .5. Normalization differs across model classes. Deterministic models use batch normalization, following standard CNN practice, whereas Bayesian models rely on global RMS normalization since channel-wise batch normalization would cancel the filter scale sℓc , which serves as the pruning score in RHS and SoU-RHS. We set the regularized horseshoe hyperparameters to b0 = 1 for the local half-Cauchy scale and bg = 10 −1

for the global layer scale. For SoU-RHS, following the SoU-SGL construction, we set γτ = 10 −9 and keep 

γβ = γν = γc = 1. For evaluation, we use the final checkpoint for Bayesian models. Unless otherwise stated, predictive statistics for Bayesian models are estimated by Monte Carlo averaging over 100 posterior samples. For deterministic models, we report the checkpoint with lowest training objective over the last 5% of training epochs. This reduces sensitivity to the final iterate while avoiding a separate validation split.

At common thresholds, both methods generally retain much of their pre-pruning accuracy, except for the larger Tiny-ImageNet models at δ = 10 −2. However, SoU-RHS achieves this with substantially fewer active filters, while RHS typically undergoes much milder pruning. To assess whether this difference merely reflects threshold calibration, we also adjust the RHS threshold to match the number of filters retained by SoU-RHS at δ = 10 −3, using filter counts alone. At matched sizes, RHS remains competitive on CIFAR-10. On CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet, however, the more aggressive pruning leads to severe accuracy degradation for RHS in most configurations. Overall, SoU-RHS achieves stronger, more threshold-stable pruning while retaining higher accuracy than RHS at matched filter counts.

## 6 Limitations 

Our evaluation is restricted to VGG-style convolutional networks and datasets no larger than Tiny-ImageNet. While this controlled setting is well suited for studying the stability of architecture recovery across starting widths and pruning thresholds, it does not establish how SoU-RHS scales to more complex architectures or larger-scale datasets. SoU-RHS incurs a higher training cost than the deterministic baselines, mainly because we use longer training schedules for the Bayesian models. Consistent with the behavior observed for SoU-SGL, sparsity can continue to increase after predictive accuracy has largely stabilized. Since our primary goal is compact architecture recovery, we use long training runs to allow the shrinkage scales and the recovered active set to stabilize. ## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced SoU-RHS, a Bayesian structured-sparsity procedure that combines regularized horseshoe shrinkage with SoU-SGL for compact architecture recovery. By selectively treating the filter-specific local scales, SoU-RHS produces a clearer separation between active and inactive filters, turning horseshoe shrinkage into a more decisive architecture-selection mechanism. The central empirical finding is stability: SoU-RHS recovers compact architectures with similar total active-set sizes and layer-wise allocations across overparameterized starting widths and under a reasonable range of pruning thresholds. Across the image-classification datasets considered, the recovered architectures are substantially smaller than those obtained by deterministic baselines while maintaining competitive accuracy and improving probabilistic performance, as measured by NLL and calibration. Retraining these same architectures further suggests that they are better suited to Bayesian models than to deterministic ones. Although we applied the method here to CNNs, the procedure is more general and can be naturally extended to other architectures by placing a horseshoe prior on their structural components and strengthening the induced shrinkage mechanism with SoU-SGL.
