A B S T R A C T 

Humanoid robots can reduce personnel exposure in electrical switching, but safe control is chal-lenging because the tool must reach a target switch next to similar devices, where an imprecise operator command can contact a non-target switch. This study proposes a runtime safety filter that checks each operator command during virtual-reality teleoperation of a humanoid robot. The filter integrates a scene model estimated from the robot’s head camera and a target-conditioned control barrier function quadratic program that keeps the tool away from the non-target switch while passing safe commands unchanged. In real-world experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, with six rounds at each of two standing positions, target operations succeed in all ten rounds with an unchanged target and no non-target actuation. A simulation study achieves 9/9 safe completions, compared with 5/9 for raw teleoperation, demonstrating the feasibility of the method for humanoid switching operations. 

## 1. Introduction 

Electrical substations are critical infrastructure in modern power systems, where transmission and distribution equipment must be switched, isolated, restored, and verified to maintain grid reliability. Although a switching action may appear to be a simple physical operation, such as opening or closing a disconnect switch, breaker, or switchgear handle, power-system switching is a safety-critical procedure performed near equipment that is or may be energized. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) requires safety-related work practices to prevent electric shock and other injuries when employees work on or near energized equipment or circuits; exposed live parts shall generally be de-energized before work is performed unless de-energization is infeasible or introduces additional hazards, and only qualified persons may work on energized equipment under appropriate precautions (Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 2024). These requirements indicate that switching is not merely a manipulation problem, but a hazardous intervention task involving electrical risk, procedural control, and human safety (Fig. 1, left). Utility switching manuals further show that switching must be executed as an authorized and verified sequence rather than as an isolated device actuation. The Snohomish County Public Utility District switching and clearance manual establishes the authority, responsibilities, and procedures for system operation, clearance, and switching, and states that system operators issue orders to open and close switches, place or remove safety cards, and de-energize or clear equipment (Snohomish County Public Utility District No. 1, 2026). Similarly, the Platte River Power Authority power-system switching procedure requires the switchman to carry the switching program, verify the device identification nameplate and location, recheck the correct sequence, operate the device, and verify that the device has moved to the desired position (Platte River Power Authority, 2021). These procedures highlight that electrical safety switching requires not only physical manipulation, but also perception, target identification, communication, operator supervision, and post-operation verification (Fig. 2). This work addresses the physical operation step of this workflow. Robots have increasingly been introduced into substations to reduce human exposure and improve inspection efficiency. Existing substation robots are commonly designed for autonomous patrol, visual inspection, thermal imaging, gauge reading, and anomaly detection. Recent work on substation inspection robots focuses on navigation, multi-sensor fusion, obstacle perception, and route-based inspection in complex substation environments (Yang et al., 2025). Industrial deployments of mobile and quadruped robots similarly emphasize routine visual and thermal

These systems are valuable for monitoring and condition assessment, but they do not fully address contact-rich switching operations that require human-scale reachability, accurate end-effector alignment, controlled force interaction, and verification of a mechanical state change on legacy equipment. In contrast, device-specific remote switching or racking systems can reduce human exposure for particular switchgear, but they often require pre-installed hardware, compatible equipment, or task-specific adaptation. This motivates the use of humanoid robots for safety switching. The goal is not to claim that humanoids are universally superior to wheeled robots, quadrupeds, fixed manipulators, or specialized remote actuators. Rather, the motivation is that many electrical facilities and operating procedures are designed around human morphology and human tool use. Switch handles, access holes, cabinet layouts, doors, stairs, narrow passages, and hot-stick-style interfaces implicitly assume human reach, posture, bimanual operation, and visual confirmation. Humanoid robots provide a human-scale body, arms, mobile base, and whole-body redundancy that can potentially reuse existing human-centered infrastructure with less environmental modification. This advantage is particularly relevant when the target facility is not redesigned for automation and when the robot must act as a remote physical avatar in a workspace originally intended for human operators. A fixed or rail-mounted arm can operate a known device reliably, but it must be installed at each panel and reaches only the devices within its workspace. A mobile manipulator avoids this installation, yet its base and arm height are usually chosen for a particular task, and stairs, doorways and raised thresholds in older facilities can restrict where it can go. A humanoid can, in principle, walk to a panel, stand where a worker would stand and use the same handles and tools. The method proposed in this paper, however, does not depend on the humanoid form: it filters the arm command and could be placed on another manipulator. What the humanoid adds to the safety problem is that the robot stands at a different place each time it approaches a panel, so the position of the neighboring devices relative to the arm changes from one visit to the next and cannot be fixed by a one-off calibration. For this reason, the constraints in this work are rebuilt from the robot’s own head camera at its current standing position. Using robots can reduce human exposure to electrical hazards, but it does not eliminate the hazards themselves. Human workers near energized equipment rely on arc-rated clothing and insulated tools, and a robot near the same equipment would also need protection, since its sensors, motors and onboard computers can be damaged by an arc or disturbed by electromagnetic fields. This study focuses on the motion of the robot relative to neighboring devices, and protection of the robot itself is discussed in our Discussion section. Fig. 1 (right) shows the humanoid used in this study at a pair of neighboring safety switches. Safety-switch operation is not only a precision-control problem. Its main safety challenge comes from intentional contact with a selected switch in a confined workspace, next to neighboring devices of the same type, with limited operator visibility and imperfect or delayed teleoperation commands. The robot must be allowed to contact the target switch with its designated tool or finger, while its arms, hands, torso and tool stay clear of the cabinet and neighboring switches, and which device counts as the target changes with the operator’s selection. A safety layer for this task therefore has to correct unsafe operator commands continuously, adapt its constraints to the selected target, and stop inward motion toward a non-target device in a way that an unchanged operator command cannot restart. In this paper, we present a runtime safety filter for virtual-reality (VR) teleoperation of a humanoid robot on neighboring safety switches. Here, runtime means that our filter operates inside the control loop while the robot is being

This differs from approaches that plan a safe motion in advance or train safe behavior offline. An operator commands the robot’s arms through a PICO headset, and an existing retargeting and whole-body control stack generates the nominal command. Instead of replacing this stack with a task-specific learned policy, we insert a minimum-intervention filter between its upper-body command and the robot. A scene model estimated from the robot’s head red-green-blue-depth (RGB-D) camera defines the constraints, and the operator’s choice of target decides which objects they apply to. Safe commands pass through unchanged, and at the boundary the arm returns to its initial pose and waits for re-authorization. No reinforcement- or imitation-learning training is required. Separately, in a MuJoCo pushbutton task, we extend it with part-region contact permissions and phase-dependent motion directions, and compare it with raw teleoperation and two ablations. We make the following contributions: • We propose a target-conditioned runtime safety filter for humanoid teleoperation, which uses an onboard RGB-D scene model to keep the tool away from non-target devices while leaving the selected target reachable, passes safe commands unchanged, and adds part-region and phase constraints in simulation. • We conduct experiments on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot (10/12 operator-reported target operations, no non-target actuation) and in a paired simulation study (9/9 safe completions versus 5/9 for raw teleoperation), to demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Robots for Electrical Substations 

Surveys of substation robots cover navigation and inspection technologies (Allan and Beaudry, 2014; Lu et al., 2017), and a technical brochure of the International Council on Large Electric Systems (CIGRE) reports that more than 1,000 outdoor patrol robots are in full operation worldwide (CIGRE Working Group B3.47, 2020). Robots that physically act on equipment are less common. The same brochure describes a teleoperated patrol robot whose arm can operate a disconnect switch, and a breaker-racking prototype (CIGRE Working Group B3.47, 2020). Lin et al. (2024) automate the hanging and removal of grounding rods, reporting 19 successes in 20 trials on a practical robot. Jia et al. (2025) use an end-to-end learned perception network so that an autonomous robot can operate switchgear buttons, knobs and handcarts. Zhao et al. (2025a) teleoperate a Unitree H1 on an electrical box with buttons, breakers and rotational switches, comparing teleoperation devices and manipulator controllers. Inspection robots mainly monitor equipment rather than operate it, while the robots of Lin et al. and Jia et al. execute specific tasks autonomously, without an operator in the loop. Zhao et al. keep an operator in the loop on a humanoid, but their controller, based on a quadratic program (QP), enforces joint limits, and the paper does not describe environment-aware constraints around the box. Our work examines this gap in teleoperation: a runtime filter constrains the tool of a teleoperated humanoid against a neighboring switch while leaving the selected target unconstrained.

2.3. Safety Filters and Perception-Informed Constraints 

Control barrier functions (CBFs) let a quadratic program minimally modify a nominal command to keep the system in a safe set (Ames et al., 2019). Table 1 compares the closest work that adds such filters or scene-aware constraints to robot motion. On humanoids, p-SSA filters live teleoperation of a G1 using obstacle locations sensed by an Apple Vision Pro (Chen et al., 2025), and ConstrainedMimic enforces runtime constraints on pre-trained tracking policies on a G1, in simulation and on hardware (Morton et al., 2026). ARMOR uses arm-mounted time-of-flight sensors on a GR1 to train a collision-avoidance policy (Kim et al., 2024), and RECAL adapts whole-body commands on a Digit V3 from egocentric point clouds (Gadde et al., 2026). On non-humanoid arms, Selvaggio et al. (2016) generate virtual fixtures on the fly from an eye-in-hand stereo camera, Brunke et al. (2025) enforce semantic constraints with CBFs using a prebuilt scene map, and VLPSA builds safety functions online for a CBF-QP filter and clears pick-and-place targets from its occupancy map (Wilkinson et al., 2026). As Table 1 shows, these works leave a specific combination open. Selvaggio et al., Brunke et al. and VLPSA do not use a humanoid. Live teleoperation is not reported for ARMOR, covers only locomotion in RECAL and only simulation in ConstrainedMimic, and is used in VLPSA only to collect demonstrations. p-SSA does not use robot-mounted sensing, and the authors of ConstrainedMimic assume environment geometry and leave onboard perception to future work. Only VLPSA reports clearing a target at the object level. RECAL allows intended contact surfaces to be removed from its input point cloud, but its authors note that it treats perceived geometry as forbidden contact. In contrast, our hardware study combines live humanoid teleoperation, constraints from a robot-mounted RGB-D camera, and object-level exclusion of an operator-selected target. Part-region permissions and phase-dependent motion directions, which no compared work reports, are studied separately in simulation, on a different task with ground-truth geometry.

The camera records about 900 color and depth frames at 640 ×360 pixels and 30 fps. Four kinds of image region mark the objects of interest: the visible front of each cabinet, each red handle, and two wall regions below the cabinets. The regions are selected per scene by viewing the current frame and are entered through per-session scripts.

3.3. Environment CBF-QP Filter 

Section 3.2 ends with a fixed model: three boxes 𝑂 𝑘 that the tool must not approach. This section describes how that model is used during control. At every control step, the native interpolator turns the operator’s motion into a joint command for the arms. Before the command is sent, the filter predicts how it would change the model gap between the tool head and each box. Because the boxes do not change after binding and the arm pose comes from the joint encoders, this prediction needs no new image. If the command keeps the tool at least a safety margin 𝑑 away from every box, and approaches that margin only gradually, it is sent unchanged. Otherwise, the filter replaces it with the closest command that meets these conditions. We express this rule as a CBF on each model gap and compute the corrected command by solving a small QP. 3.4. Non-Blocking Scheduling 

Equation (8) defines which command the filter should send, but not how to obtain it within one control cycle. The loop runs at a nominal 50 Hz, so a command is due about every 20 ms, while the time of a QP solve varies from cycle to cycle. If the loop called the solver and waited for it in every cycle, a slow solve would delay the command to the robot. We utilize two properties of the problem to achieve a non-blocking schedule. First, when the tool is far from every box, the nominal command already satisfies all rows, so it is the exact optimum of Eq. (8) and no solver is needed. Second, the state changes little between consecutive cycles, so the rows that were active in the last solution are likely to be active again, and the new solution can be recovered from them by one small linear solve. The scheduler therefore tries these cheap candidates first, checks each against the optimality conditions of the current QP, and passes the full QP to a background worker only when they fail. A further issue is that the rows of Eq. (6) are linearized at the current pose, so a command that satisfies them can still shrink a barrier more than allowed at the pose the robot actually reaches. Every candidate is therefore checked again at its next pose before it is sent. If no candidate is ready, or none passes this check, the arm holds its last reference instead of waiting for the solver. If it does not trigger, the candidate is checked at step scales 𝛼 ∈ {1 , 1∕2 , 1∕4 , 1∕8} , with the trial command 𝑞 ref + 𝛼 (𝑞 c − 𝑞 ref ) for candidate command 𝑞 c. Each trial must satisfy the linear rows and, after re-running the kinematics at the next pose 𝑞 +, the nonlinear condition 

ℎ𝑘𝑣 (𝑞 +) ≥ ℎ𝑘𝑣 (𝑞 ) − 𝛾 max( ℎ𝑘𝑣 (𝑞 ), 0) (9) for all rows, and must not leave the tool at the boundary while closing. The first trial that passes is sent. If every scale fails, the arm holds its last reference. We call any of these holds a constraint hold, shown as Wait in Fig. 3. The same operator command is retried in the next cycle, and following continues without operator action once a candidate passes. 

3.5. Boundary Return and Re-Authorization 

The filter of Sections 3.3 and 3.4 only limits motion. At the boundary, where a barrier reaches zero, the rows forbid further approach but do not move the tool away (Eq. 6). If the operator keeps commanding inward motion there, the filter keeps removing the inward part of each command, or the scheduler keeps holding, and the tool stays at the boundary for as long as the operator pushes. We therefore treat continued inward motion at the boundary as a separate event. Before a candidate is sent (Section 3.4), the scheduler runs the boundary check defined below. When the check triggers, control leaves the filter, the arm returns to its initial pose, and teleoperation stays paused until the operator re-authorizes it. A return starts when, for some constrained object, the exact model gap is at or below 𝑑 at the encoder or reference pose and the commanded closing speed toward that object at that pose exceeds 10 −7 m/s. The arm then moves from the last sent command to the initial pose of the trial, stored when teleoperation first starts, through the original interpolator at no more than 1.5 rad/s. This pose lies outside the boundary, which the pre-binding check of Section 3.2 enforces. On completion, the arm holds, the operator’s last target is discarded, and the system stays paused. 

3.6. Operator Interface 

A panel in the PICO application (Fig. 4) shows the live head camera image, labeled with its local delivery age, together with the active target, the nearest constrained object with its estimated model gap, and the filter state. 3.7. Simulation Filter for Part-Region and Phase Constraints 

Our system of Sections 3.2-3.6 grants permission at the object level: the whole target assembly is removed from the constraints, and the same constraints apply throughout the task. It therefore does not specify which part of the robot may touch which part of the target, and it does not restrict the direction of motion while the target is pressed and released. We address these two points with two extensions. Part-region permissions allow contact only between selected parts of the robot and the target, and phase-direction constraints change the allowed motion as the task moves between phases. We study both extensions in simulation, on a dedicated task and filter. Fig. 5 (a) shows the simulated task. ## 5. Discussion 

5.1. Influence of the standing position on filter activity and target reach 

In practical switching operations, a humanoid cannot always stand at the exact same place in front of the equipment every time, and its position relative to the cabinets changes the geometry that the filter works with. The two configurations show this effect. The filter changes the operator’s command in 26.78% of eligible cycles in C2, compared with 8.45% in C1. A possible reason is the distance between the working region and the non-target box. At the return pose, the tool is 28.49 cm from the non-target box in C1 but only 12.30 cm in C2, and C2 places the robot closer to the wall, about 41 cm from toe to wall compared with about 48.5 cm. The tool therefore starts each motion closer to the boundary in C2 and spends more time where the rows are active. The timing of the corrections is consistent with this explanation. 5.2. Causes of constraint holds and computational cost 

A filter that runs inside teleoperation must not stall the robot or delay its commands, so the duration of constraint holds and the cost of computation determine whether the method is usable during live operation. Most holds are short: 148 of 158 last at most two cycles. A likely reason is that a short hold occurs when a candidate narrowly fails the next-pose check, and the following cycle, with a slightly different state, yields an acceptable candidate. The two long holds have different causes. The 61.38 s hold in C2 round 1 occurs after the tool has moved past the edge of the non-target box. There, the closest separating direction no longer supports both complete boxes, so the geometry check rejects the state for 3,069 cycles and no rows can be built. Because the arm does not move while held, the state does not change, and the hold persists until the operator requests a manual return. This also explains why C2 round 1 is the only round without an automatic return. The 8.74 s hold in C1 round 4 occurs when the nonlinear check rejects all four step scales for 361 cycles, which suggests that the linearized rows of Eq. (6) permit steps that the exact barrier does not. Neither long hold involves a running QP, and solver time is small throughout. All 180 background QP calls succeed, with a mean solve time of about 0.5 ms, a small fraction of the 20 ms period. The QP is rarely needed: 71.4% of the cycles that produce a candidate use the nominal command, which is expected when the tool is far from every constrained box for most of the task, and the remaining cycles are served by the active-set solution (18.6%) and the native-bound projection (10.1%). In summary, the non-blocking schedule keeps solver time from limiting the loop, and the main weakness of the current implementation is recovery from unusual geometric states rather than solver speed. 

5.3. Difference between commanded and measured model gaps 

The filter acts on the command sent to the robot, whereas safety depends on where the arm actually is. We therefore compare two model gaps to the non-target: the reference gap, computed at the commanded pose, and the encoder gap, computed at the pose measured by the joint encoders. Over all rounds, the reference gap never falls below 50.00 mm, whereas the encoder gap reaches a minimum of 49.04 mm in C1 and 49.55 mm in C2. Each configuration has 10 encoder samples below 50 mm, and all 20 occur at the onset of a return. Fig. 11 shows how these values arise. Fig. 11(a) follows the last 3 s before the return in C1 round 3, the return with the smallest encoder gap. The two gaps have nearly the same shape, but the encoder gap follows the reference gap with a short delay. In several intervals, the reference gap stays flat at 50 mm, because the filter holds the command at the boundary. Just before the trigger, the command stops outside the boundary, while the measured arm, which lags behind, continues briefly towards the non-target and reaches 49.04 mm. The automatic return is triggered at this moment. Fig. 11(b) shows both gaps at the trigger cycle of all 17 automatic returns. The reference gap is at or above 50.00 mm in every case, whereas the encoder gap is 49.04-49.97 mm, so the measured arm reaches the boundary at every trigger but crosses it by less than 1 mm. In most returns, the two gaps nearly coincide. In the first two returns of C1 round 6, however, the reference gap is still about 61 mm when the encoder gap reaches the boundary, so these returns are triggered by the measured pose. This confirms that the return logic of Section 3.5 checks both poses, so a lag between them cannot delay the return. One possible reason for the brief crossing is therefore tracking lag between the command and the arm, rather than a failure of the constraint. In summary, the constraint holds exactly on the command and approximately on the measured pose, and the margin 𝑑 must cover tracking lag as well as model error. With 𝑑 = 5 cm, the observed crossing of less than 1 mm uses a small part of the margin. 

5.4. Effects of part-region permission and phase-direction constraints 

Switch operation requires contact with the target, so a filter must decide which contact is allowed and how the hand may move while in contact. Our simulation study separates the contributions of the two extensions that address these questions. Under I1, a finger pivot brings the middle finger towards the target. M1 removes every pair between the robot and the target assembly once permission is active, so no row prevents the middle finger pad from touching the guard, which it does with peak summed forces of 8.00-9.35 N for 5.18-5.45 s per run. M3 keeps a 5 mm clearance for every right-hand part except the index pad, so the same input is corrected before contact. Because the index pad pair is permitted in both methods, the press itself is unaffected, and both methods complete 3/3 runs. Object-level permission therefore admits contact by any part of the hand once the target is excluded, which is the permission that the hardware filter uses. 5.5. Relation between the simulation and real-world results 

Table 5 compares the two studies. They share the robot model and the filter formulation, but differ in task, end effector and source of geometry, so they complement each other rather than repeat the same measurement. Three findings are consistent across the two studies. First, the QP does not limit the control loop, as its solve time stays well below 1 ms in both. Second, no unintended contact occurs when the filter’s geometry matches the scene. Third, the position of the equipment relative to the robot can change the filter’s behavior: a different standing position more than triples the hardware intervention ratio, and an unreflected 5 ◦ rotation prevents the press in simulation.

The studies differ in constraint holds, which occur only on hardware. A possible reason is that the simulation uses ground-truth geometry and joint states, whereas on hardware the boxes are estimated, the measured pose lags the reference and operator motion varies between rounds, creating states near the boundary that the simulation runs do not reach. The simulation thus isolates the effect of each constraint, while the hardware study shows how the complete system behaves under real perception and operator input. 

5.6. Implications for real-world electrical switch operations 

Switching operations follow procedures that identify the target device, operate it and then verify the result. The results suggest three implications for introducing a teleoperated humanoid into this workflow. First, the filter addresses the physical operation step, while target identification and verification remain with the operator and the procedure. The operator selects the target before each trial, and the filter then keeps that target reachable and constrains its neighbor. Across the 12 attempts, the operator reports no non-target actuation, and approaching the non-target triggers an automatic return in 11 rounds. A safety layer of this kind can therefore reduce the reliance on the operator’s precision near a neighboring device, but it does not replace the procedural checks around the operation. Second, the method fits sites that are designed for human workers. The scene model is built from the robot’s own head camera at its current position without entering a cabinet pose for each scene or modifying the equipment. This matters in electrical rooms, where devices sit close together and drawings may not match the installed layout. The simulation adds that the model should be rebuilt when the robot or the equipment moves, since an outdated map causes a missed operation after a 5 ◦ rotation. Third, the method keeps authority with the operator and fails towards stopping. Safe commands pass through unchanged, a stop at the boundary can only be lifted through recalibration and an explicit command, and when the rows cannot be built, the arm holds instead of moving. For a site procedure, this means that the operator must be prepared to resolve a hold, for example by requesting a manual return, until a fallback is implemented. The simulation further suggests that, for tasks in which the hand itself presses a device, permissions should be limited to the intended contact and motion should be restricted by task phase. 

5.7. Limitations 

Several limitations remain. First, the hardware and simulation studies use different end effectors, so the fine contact control examined in simulation is not tested on the robot. In simulation, the robot presses the button with the index finger of a three-finger hand, which makes it possible to permit contact for one finger pad and to protect the other fingers. On our physical robot, the switch is operated with a rigid 3D-printed tool on the right wrist, which the filter treats as a single box. The hardware study therefore tests only object-level permission, with the whole target assembly excluded from the constraints, and cannot show whether part-region permission and phase-direction constraints would work with a real hand. The tool is also mounted on one arm only, which limits the reach of the robot and causes both incomplete operations. Second, our physical test equipment differs from that of real electrical substations. The robot operates two general-duty enclosed safety switches mounted on a wall, of the kind used in residential and light commercial installations. Substation switchgear, such as disconnect switches, circuit breakers and racking mechanisms, can differ in size, handle design, operating force and travel, and is usually housed in large cabinets or arranged in rows of panels. We do not test such cabinets, so the scene model, the declared size bound and the standing positions used here may not transfer directly, and larger or more closely packed equipment may require other box sizes, more constrained objects and other positions. Third, the switches are not energized. Energizing them is not feasible in our laboratory, and testing an unproven filter on live equipment would be unsafe. As a result, we cannot measure the electrical consequences of an operation, such as arcing during switching or the effect of an incomplete or repeated actuation, and the study provides no evidence for live work. The results describe the motion of the robot relative to de-energized equipment only. For the same reason, this study does not assess how an arc or the electromagnetic field near live equipment would affect the robot. An arc flash releases intense heat, pressure and light, and the cameras, encoders, motor drivers and onboard computers of a humanoid can be damaged or disturbed, which can in turn degrade the scene model or the control loop on which the filter depends. Damage to a robot is less serious than injury to a human worker, but a robot that fails next to electrical equipment can still create a hazard. Before industrial deployment, the robot would therefore need its own protection, such as insulated tools, arc-rated covers, an adequate standoff distance and verified electromagnetic compatibility. We leave this part to future work.

## 6. Conclusion 

This study proposes a runtime safety filter for VR teleoperation of a humanoid robot on neighboring safety switches. Humanoid robots can perform switching operations at sites designed for human workers, but in a tight workspace an imperfect operator command can bring the tool into a neighboring device, and existing humanoid teleoperation systems do not constrain motion against the scene. A safety layer for this task must keep the selected target reachable while stopping motion towards its neighbor, work from the robot’s own sensing at an unmodified site, and leave safe commands unchanged. To address these challenges, a digital model of the scene is built from the robot’s head RGB-D camera at its current standing position, using fitted cabinet planes and a declared size bound. Based on the operator-selected target, a CBF-QP filter keeps the tool at least 5 cm from the non-target assembly and the walls while leaving the target assembly unconstrained. A non-blocking schedule passes safe commands unchanged without delaying the control loop, and a latched return, which only the operator can lift, stops continued inward motion at the boundary. On a Unitree G1, in 12 attempts at two standing positions, there are 10 completed target operations and no non-target actuation or accidental contact. Approaching the non-target triggers an automatic return in 11 rounds, the reference model gap never falls below 50 mm, and all 180 background QP calls succeed with a mean solve time of about 0.5 ms. In our simulation study, part-region permissions and phase-direction constraints together achieve 9/9 safe completions, compared with 5/9 for raw teleoperation: the former removes non-permitted contact by other parts of the hand, and the latter keeps the finger centered and prevents it from pushing back in during withdrawal. This work demonstrates the feasibility of adding a target-aware safety layer to live humanoid control next to neighboring electrical switches, without modifying the site or the existing software stack, and provides a technical foundation for using humanoid robots in electrical switching operations. Although this study demonstrates the approach on de-energized equipment in a laboratory, several challenges remain before it can be applied to real switching operations. First, the robot operates the switches with a rigid tool on one arm, whereas the fine contact control is examined only in simulation with a three-finger hand, so part-region permission and phase-direction constraints have not been tested on the robot, and the single tool also limits reach. Second, the test equipment consists of wall-mounted general-duty safety switches, which may differ from substation switchgear in size, handle design, operating force and cabinet arrangement. Third, the switches are not energized, so the electrical consequences of an operation and the effect of arcing on the robot itself cannot be assessed. Future research will therefore focus on bringing the part-region and phase logic to a dexterous hand on the robot, testing on substation-type switchgear and large cabinets, and evaluating energized operation under appropriate electrical safety arrangements.
