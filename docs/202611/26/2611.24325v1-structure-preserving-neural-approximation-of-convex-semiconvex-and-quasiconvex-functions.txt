# Structure-Preserving Neural Approximation of Convex, Semiconvex, and Quasiconvex Functions 

## Yeachan Park ∗ Yeoneung Kim †‡ 

Abstract 

Input convex neural networks provide a natural way to enforce con-vexity globally through architectural constraints. We study how this structure-preserving approximation framework extends to semiconvex, qua-siconvex, and convex transformable functions. For C1,1 convex functions on compact convex domains, we establish the sharp W 1,p minimax rate 

m−1/d under max-affine approximation with m affine pieces, and show that every max-affine function admits an exact bounded-width ICNN representation with complexity linear in m. The same W 1,p approxi-mation rate extends to semiconvex functions through quadratic lifting and to convex transformable functions through monotone composition. For general Lipschitz quasiconvex functions, we develop a quantitative finite-probe approximation theory based on monotone responses to affine projections and obtain a structure-preserving IqCNN with an L∞ ap-proximation guarantee. We further show that these approximation re-sults extend to ReLU-transferable smooth activations, including Softplus and ELU. Numerical experiments on PDE-based approximation problems compare the proposed architectures with parameter-matched MLPs and validation-tuned shape penalties, demonstrating global structural validity without penalty tuning while maintaining competitive approximation ac-curacy. Overall, the results identify recoverable convex latent structure as the key mechanism enabling gradient-level structure-preserving approxi-mation beyond convexity. 

# 1 Introduction 

Convexity is a useful structural prior in neural approximation because it can be enforced globally through the network architecture. Input convex neural net-works [ICNNs; 3], for example, guarantee convexity of the input–output map by constraining hidden-to-hidden weights to be nonnegative. Such guarantees are attractive in optimization, control, inverse problems, and related applica-tions where violations of the prescribed geometry may matter independently of prediction error [13, 23, 30].

and convex −→ convex transformable −→ quasiconvex .

The first direction relaxes convexity through an explicit quadratic correction, while the second passes from a single convex latent function to progressively more general quasiconvex representations. We also extend the theory beyond ReLU networks. For a class of convex, nondecreasing ReLU-transferable activations, including Softplus and ELU, the structure-preserving approximation results carry over with the same approxi-mation orders. Taken together, the results identify recoverable convex latent structure as the mechanism behind gradient-level approximation. Convex functions possess this structure directly, semiconvex functions recover it through quadratic lifting, and convex transformable functions retain it through monotone composition. General quasiconvex functions instead admit a representation through a family of affine probes, leading to a different approximation regime. Numerical experiments in dimensions up to 32 compare the proposed archi-tectures with parameter-matched multilayer perceptrons and validation-tuned shape penalties. The penalized models can substantially reduce sampled struc-tural violations and can sometimes attain lower approximation error. The architecture-based models, however, enforce the prescribed geometry globally 3without tuning a shape-penalty parameter and achieve competitive accuracy together with greater stability in the high-dimensional convex transformable experiments. The main contributions are as follows. 

• We establish a sharp W 1,p approximation theory for C1,1 convex func-tions, proving the minimax rate m−1/d under max-affine approximation and showing that every max-affine function admits an exact bounded-width ICNN realization with complexity linear in the number of affine pieces. 

• We extend the convex W 1,p approximation theory to broader classes with recoverable convex latent structure: semiconvex functions through quadratic lifting and convex transformable functions through monotone composition, leading to the corresponding IsCNN and IctNN architectures.

• We extend the approximation results to ReLU-transferable smooth activa-tions, including Softplus and ELU, and provide PDE-based numerical ex-periments comparing the proposed structure-preserving architectures with parameter-matched MLPs and validation-tuned shape penalties. # 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Input Convex Neural Networks and Max-Affine Ap-proximation 

Input convex neural networks impose convexity of the input–output map through architectural constraints, most notably the nonnegativity of hidden-to-hidden weights [3]. They have been applied to optimization-aware learning and control [13], optimal transport and generative modeling [23, 30], structured prediction, and energy-based modeling. Their approximation properties have also been 4studied, including the approximation of convex target functions by ICNNs [23]. ICNNs have further been used in a variety of control applications [37, 9, 27]. Closely related are max-affine models, which form a canonical class for convex regression and piecewise-linear convex approximation [29, 6]. 

## 2.2 Structure-Preserving Neural Architectures 

More broadly, neural architectures have been designed to preserve geometric and analytic properties beyond ordinary convexity. Examples include parameterized convex models [26], polyconvex architectures for mechanics [19], and networks constrained to be Lipschitz continuous [4, 20] or monotone [34, 16]. These works encode structural priors directly in the network architecture, complementing classical universal approximation results for unconstrained neural networks [15, 22, 28, 32, 38]. 

## 2.3 Convexity and Generalized Convexity in Optimiza-tion, Control and Engineering 

Convexity and related generalized convexity conditions are widely used in opti-mization, variational analysis, optimal control, dynamic programming, robotics, and computer vision. Semiconvex functions arise in nonsmooth and nonconvex constrained op-timization, where their generalized differential properties support optimality analysis and numerical methods [31]. In optimal control and Hamilton–Jacobi theory, the closely related notion of semiconcavity is a fundamental regularity property of value functions and viscosity solutions [7, 10]. Semiconvexity and semiconcavity have also been used in optimal control [8, 18] and robotics [24]. Quasiconvexity, understood through convexity of sublevel sets, provides an-other classical relaxation of convexity in mathematical programming. Qua-siconvex optimization problems retain convex sublevel sets and can often be solved through sequences of convex feasibility problems or bisection procedures. This structure underlies both classical optimality theory for quasiconvex pro-grams and modern disciplined quasiconvex programming frameworks [35, 17, 2]. Quasiconvex objectives and constraints have been used in multiview geometry [1, 21], control and remote estimation [11], and inverse optimization for appli-cations such as production planning [12].

# 8 Approximation of quasiconvex functions 

We now turn to the unrestricted quasiconvex class. At this level, the latent con-vex structure used in the convex and semiconvex analyses is no longer available a priori. Instead, the correct structural description is the affine-probe supremal representation of Lemma 4.8: a continuous quasiconvex function is recovered as the supremum of monotone one-dimensional responses applied to affine projec-tions. This representation is exact but inherently infinite-dimensional. Conse-quently, the natural finite-complexity approximation class is obtained by trun-cating the infinite supremum to finitely many affine probes. The results of this section should therefore be interpreted as a baseline approximation theory for general quasiconvexity: they quantify what can be obtained at the fully gen-eral level, in terms of function values only, before any additional latent convex structure is imposed.

In this section, we evaluate structure-preserving neural architectures for PDE-based approximation of convex, semiconvex, quasiconvex, and convex-transformable functions. We consider four problem classes: (A) convex, (B) semiconvex, (C) quasiconvex, and (D) convex-transformable functions. Each experiment is formulated through a PDE whose solution belongs to the corresponding function class. We compare structure-preserving architec-tures, which enforce the relevant geometry by construction, with standard MLPs equipped with sampled shape penalties. The main objective is to assess whether architectural constraints provide more reliable preservation of the target struc-ture than penalties imposed only at finitely many collocation points. We con-sider problems in three and higher dimensions. Note that quasiconcave functions can be obtained by negation. 

## 11.1 Setup 

We use ICNN-family architectures and MLPs with Softplus activations so that the required first- and second-order derivatives are well defined. Since Soft-plus is ReLU-transferable, the corresponding Softplus-ICNN family retains the structure-preserving and approximation properties established in Theorem 5.3. The models are trained using physics-informed objectives that combine PDE residuals with the appropriate boundary or auxiliary conditions. The specific PDE and loss terms depend on the function class and are described in the corresponding experiment below. In addition to the PDE loss, penalized MLP baselines include sampled shape constraints tailored to each class. All results are reported as the test-set mean and standard deviation over three independently initialized runs, and figure error bars indicate one stan-dard deviation. Training collocation points are resampled from the specified domain at every optimization step. We use Adam with cosine learning-rate de-cay, batch size 768, and 6 ,000 fixed test points per run. The semiconvex and convex-transformable experiments are trained for 5 ,000 steps, while the quasi-convex experiment is trained for 4 ,000 steps. Architectures are chosen to have comparable numbers of trainable parameters. For the penalized MLP baselines, the shape penalty is chosen according to the target structure. In part A, we penalize negative values of the minimum Hessian eigenvalue. In part B, we apply the same penalty to the shifted Hessian 

D2F + ρtI. In part C, we use the second-order quasiconvexity condition and penalize negative curvature along eight random directions orthogonal to ∇F ,corresponding to tangent directions of the level set. In part D, we provide the oracle transform g = exp( f ) and penalize negative eigenvalues of D2 exp( FMLP ).

Each shape penalty is evaluated on additional unlabeled collocation points. For parts C and D, these points are sampled from the larger evaluation cubes of radii 1 .5 and 2, respectively. We tune the penalty parameter on inde-pendent validation samples, using λ ∈ { 0, 0.1, 1, 10 , 100 } for part B and λ ∈{0, 1, 10 , 100 , 1000 } for parts C and D. Each value of λ is evaluated using three 40 independently trained models. Figure 5 reports the full sweep. Based on vali-dation performance, we fix λ = 100 for parts B and C and λ = 1000 for part D before test evaluation. These penalized MLPs provide strong finite-sample baselines, but their struc-tural constraints are enforced only at sampled points. In contrast, ICNN, Is-CNN, IqCNN, and IctNN satisfy their respective structural constraints globally for every admissible parameter value.

# 12 Conclusion 

We developed a structure-preserving approximation theory for convex, semi-convex, quasiconvex, and convex transformable functions using ICNN-based architectures. The results reveal a common principle: the strength of the ap-proximation guarantee is determined by the form of the underlying convex rep-resentation. For C1,1 convex functions, we established the sharp W 1,p minimax rate 

m−1/d under max-affine approximation and showed that every max-affine func-tion can be represented exactly by a bounded-width sequential ICNN with com-plexity linear in the number of affine pieces. This connects classical piecewise-affine convex approximation directly to neural-network approximation. The same W 1,p approximation order extends to semiconvex functions through quadratic lifting and to convex transformable functions through monotone composition with a convex latent function. For general Lipschitz quasiconvex functions, the relevant structure is differ-ent. Using a supremal representation by monotone responses to affine probes, we derived a quantitative finite-probe approximation theorem and introduced a structure-preserving IqCNN architecture. This yields a value-level L∞ ap-proximation guarantee based on a finite family of one-dimensional monotone components. Together, these results distinguish function classes admitting a single recoverable convex latent representation from those described through a family of convexity-compatible probes. We also showed that the approximation results extend beyond ReLU net-works to ReLU-transferable convex activations, including Softplus and ELU, without changing the corresponding approximation orders. This allows the structure-preserving theory to be combined with smooth activations when dif-ferentiability of the approximating network is desirable. The numerical experiments support the theoretical picture. Compared with parameter-matched MLPs and validation-tuned shape penalties, the proposed architectures enforce the prescribed geometry globally by construction, while the penalty-based methods provide only sampled control and require an addi-tional tuning parameter. At the same time, the structure-preserving models retain competitive approximation accuracy, illustrating that exact geometric constraints need not come at the cost of practical approximation quality. 51 References
