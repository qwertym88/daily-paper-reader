Abstract 

Model-based offline-to-online reinforcement learning (RL) enables sample-efficient adaptation by leveraging offline pre-training for online fine-tuning. However, the distribution shifts between offline and online stages often hinder fine-tuning performance. Many existing methods encode the optimism-pessimism trade-off through a single-objective formulation. Selecting its trade-off coefficient through online fine-tuning can require costly bi-level optimization. We identify the balance established during offline training as a key challenge: in our motivating experiments, optimistic policies adapt better to novel tasks, whereas pessimistic policies perform better on the same online tasks. To address this challenge, we propose a bi-objective formulation that captures this trade-off, yielding a Pareto policy pool during offline training. This policy pool enables adaptive selection during online fine-tuning across diverse online tasks. To generate the pool, we introduce Multi-Objective Soft Actor-critIC (MOSAIC), which solves bi-objective problems and constructs diverse Pareto policies. After offline training, a contextual bandit algorithm hierarchically selects the most suitable policy for fine-tuning at each online interaction step. Empirically, our pipeline, Hi erarchical Pareto Policy 

Pool ( HiP3 ), achieves the strongest aggregate performance across both same and novel online tasks and maintains its advantage under stronger distribution shifts. Comprehensive ablations further clarify the roles of the policy pool, MOSAIC-based pool construction, and the online selection mechanism. 

1 Introduction 

A major obstacle to deploying reinforcement learning (RL) in real-world settings is its high sample complexity. Offline RL mitigates this issue by learning from pre-collected data [1]. However, distribution shifts between offline data and online interaction can hinder the deployment of offline-trained policies. This motivates offline-to-online RL (O2O RL), which adapts pretrained policies using limited online interaction [2, 3]. Model-based O2O RL further improves sample efficiency by learning environment models for offline pre-training and online fine-tuning [4, 5]. Despite this progress, distribution shifts continue to limit fine-tuning performance in model-based O2O RL. We argue that a key factor is the optimism-pessimism trade-off established during offline training. Following prior works [6–8], we use model-predicted return and uncertainty as proxies for optimism and pessimism, respectively. Figure 1(a) illustrates offline policies with different trade-offs

Policies P2 and P3 represent intermediate trade-offs. Appendix D.1 further reproduces this reversal across four controlled settings spanning different tasks and offline-data qualities. These observations suggest that the preferred optimism-pessimism balance depends on the task encountered during online fine-tuning. Existing model-based approaches commonly encode this trade-off through a single scalarized ob-jective, such as ˆr − λu , thereby committing each offline policy to a particular balance. Adapting this balance to an online task by selecting λ according to fine-tuning performance requires an outer search over coefficients around the inner policy-optimization procedure, making task-specific tuning costly. Training multiple policies with different fixed coefficients is a natural alternative, but linear scalarization does not in general recover all Pareto-optimal trade-offs when the attainable objective set is non-convex. Appendix B.5 provides a detailed discussion and empirical comparison. In this paper, we treat optimism and pessimism as two distinct objectives and introduce Hierarchical Pareto Policy Pool (HiP3), which constructs a diverse offline policy pool for adaptive online selection. During online adaptation, the agent encounters changing states and updates its candidate policies, making policy selection an ongoing decision. This design raises two algorithmic challenges. First, how can we generate policies that cover diverse optimism-pessimism trade-offs across the Pareto front? Second, how can we dynamically select a suitable policy for the current state during online fine-tuning, rather than committing to a single candidate throughout adaptation? To address the first challenge, we introduce Multi-Objective Soft Actor-critIC (MOSAIC), which solves constrained bi-objective policy optimization problems defined by reference vectors targeting different regions of the Pareto front. It then uses warm-started neighborhood search to discover additional trade-off policies around the obtained ones. To address the second challenge, HiP3 uses a contextual bandit as a high-level controller over a candidate subset of the offline policy pool. At each online interaction step, the controller selects a policy conditioned on the current state, and the selected policy is fine-tuned with model-based updates. We evaluate HiP3 on the standard D4RL continuous-control benchmark [9] against representative online and O2O RL baselines across both same and novel online tasks. HiP3 achieves the strongest average performance in both settings, with particularly clear advantages on novel tasks, and remains effective under stronger distribution shifts. Ablation studies examine the roles of the policy pool, MOSAIC-based pool construction, state-dependent policy selection, and candidate-set initialization. Additional analyses characterize policy-selection behavior during online adaptation. Our code is available at https://github.com/GuochenZhou/HiP3 

2 Related Work 

Offline RL and Model-based Variants. Offline RL trains policies on static datasets to avoid the high sample complexity of online methods [1]. A major challenge is poor extrapolation to out-of-distribution (OOD) actions, often causing overestimation. Most approaches rely on pessimism 2to counter this, via policy constraints [10, 11], regularization [12], or uncertainty penalties [13, 14]. Model-based methods improve efficiency with learned dynamics for planning or simulated rollouts [15–18]. Notably, works like MOPO [6] and MOReL [7] incorporate uncertainty to penalize OOD regions (e.g., subtracting it from rewards). Critically, these fix the optimism-pessimism trade-off via a single penalty, limiting flexibility for online fine-tuning. 

Offline-to-Online (O2O) RL. Distribution shifts hinder direct online deployment of offline poli-cies [19], necessitating fine-tuning. However, fine-tuning often underperforms [20] due to (1) training instability from the shift [21], and (2) restricted exploration inherited from offline pessimism. Instabil-ity is mitigated via balanced replay [21], priority sampling [22], policy expansion [3], state-adaptive policy families [23], and more recently warm-start recalibration without retaining offline data [24]. Exploration or policy improvement is enhanced via critic guidance [25], guide policies [26], perturbed value updates and more frequent critic updates [27], optimistic critic reconstruction with constrained fine-tuning [28], energy-guided diffusion sampling that distills offline knowledge for online data generation [29], and expressive flow-based policies [30]. Beyond O2O-specific methods, scalable world-model agents such as TD-MPC2 [31] provide strong sample-efficient model-based baselines, but do not explicitly address the optimism-pessimism trade-off induced by offline pretraining. Most of these methods optimize a single policy or otherwise commit to a fixed adaptation strategy, rather than preserving multiple optimism-pessimism trade-offs for later selection. HiP3 instead treats the trade-off itself as an adaptation decision: it preserves diverse trade-off policies offline and performs state-dependent policy selection among them during online adaptation.

5 Experimental Results 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate HiP3 against O2O RL and online RL baselines on two task categories derived from the D4RL benchmark [9]: same online tasks and novel online tasks (see Appendix C.1 for a brief overview). Our primary evaluation target is adaptation under changes in dynamics and rewards. Fine-tuning on the same online tasks provides a complementary evaluation of the same framework. We compare HiP3 with representative O2O RL baselines, including Cal-QL [2], PEX [3], AWAC [20], IQL [40], CQL [11], and FamO2O [23], together with the online RL baseline MBPO [15]. CQL, Cal-QL, AWAC, and IQL are run using the publicly available CORL [41] codebase with default hyperparameters unless otherwise specified, while PEX and MBPO use their official implementations. After 1M offline steps, all O2O methods except FamO2O are fine-tuned for 100K online steps, and MBPO is trained from scratch for 100K steps. For HiP3, the online candidate set is initialized by the one-time ranking described in Section 4 before the 100K-step fine-tuning stage. Implementation details are provided in Appendix C.2. The offline policy-optimization budget is distributed across the complete policy pool rather than assigned independently to each policy, while each online update optimizes only the selected policy. 5.2 Main Results 

As shown in Table 1, HiP3 achieves the strongest average performance across both the same-task and novel-task settings, with the best final performance on 7 of 12 same-task datasets and 9 of 12 novel-task datasets. Consistent with these aggregate results, Figure 3 shows that several representative O2O baselines struggle on novel tasks and, in some cases, also underperform on same-task settings initialized from lower-quality offline data such as random or medium-replay datasets. For readability, the main table reports representative baselines, while complete per-task comparisons, including IQL, CQL, and the available FamO2O results, are given in Appendix Table 21. Together, these results indicate that HiP3 improves adaptation across varied online tasks by combining a diverse offline policy pool with state-dependent online selection. Because different states favor different optimism-pessimism trade-offs, HiP3 can switch among candidate policies during fine-tuning, whereas fixed-trade-off baselines remain tied to the bias of a single offline policy. To test whether HiP3 generalizes beyond MuJoCo locomotion, we also evaluate it on Adroit and Maze2D. HiP3 remains competitive on the Adroit tasks and achieves the best performance on Maze2D among the compared baselines, suggesting that the benefits of HiP3 may extend to additional continuous-control and navigation settings. Detailed results are provided in Appendix D.2.

6 Conclusion 

This paper studies the optimism-pessimism trade-off in model-based O2O RL and introduces HiP3, which preserves diverse trade-offs offline and performs state-dependent policy selection online rather than committing to a single balance in advance. HiP3 formulates optimism and pessimism as two explicit objectives, uses MOSAIC to construct a Pareto policy pool, and uses a contextual bandit to select candidate policies conditioned on the current state throughout model-based fine-tuning. Across both same and novel online tasks, HiP3 achieves strong aggregate performance and maintains its advantage under stronger distribution shifts. These results support a simple design principle for model-based O2O RL: preserve trade-off diversity offline and defer the choice among candidate policies to online adaptation. 

Limitation: While HiP3 performs strongly on the evaluated O2O RL benchmarks, we identify three key areas for further investigation. First, similar to prior methods, HiP3 still exhibits an initial performance degradation at the early stage of online fine-tuning. We provide an empirical analysis of this behavior in Appendix D.3 and believe that mitigating such transient instability remains a promising avenue for future work. Second, while our evaluation primarily focuses on the widely-adopted D4RL MuJoCo suite to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison, extending the proposed framework to higher-dimensional environments with more pronounced distribution shifts represents a valuable future direction. Third, because reward and uncertainty are model-derived surrogates, offline Pareto trade-offs may not exactly match real-environment trade-offs under severe model bias.

A Societal Impacts 

This work contributes to offline-to-online reinforcement learning by explicitly modeling the trade-off between optimistic exploration and pessimistic conservatism, a central challenge when adapting policies under distribution shift. By enabling flexible policy selection during online interaction, the proposed approach has the potential to reduce unnecessary exploration and improve sample efficiency, which may benefit real-world learning systems where online interaction is costly or risky. At the same time, as with many offline-to-online RL methods, transient performance degradation dur-ing early online fine-tuning highlights the need for cautious deployment, particularly in safety-critical settings or under severe distribution shifts. While our experiments focus on standard continuous control benchmarks, applying the proposed framework in real-world systems would require careful validation, monitoring, and safeguards to ensure stable behavior. We hope this work encourages further research into principled uncertainty-aware adaptation mechanisms that balance performance and safety in online learning.

B.2 Uncertainty Estimation u in HiP3 

The predicted uncertainty u of the environment model M is estimated as the maximum standard devi-ation among the ensemble, i.e., u(st, a t) = max Ni=1 || σi(st, a t)|| . This ensemble-based uncertainty estimation strategy follows established practices in model-based offline RL such as MOPO [6] and MOReL [7]. This method is favored for its scalability, ease of implementation, and effectiveness in representing epistemic uncertainty in deep neural networks. Although alternative techniques such as Bayesian methods [45] and unsupervised representation-based approaches [46] are available, recent studies [47, 48] have demonstrated that deep ensembles tend to provide more accurate and better-calibrated uncertainty estimates. These results indicate that ensemble methods strike an effective balance between predictive performance and computational cost, making them a reliable and practical choice for uncertainty estimation in HiP3. A limitation of using predicted return and uncertainty directly is that they may not match the true dynamics in out-of-distribution regions, especially when the offline dataset has limited coverage. Conservative approaches [5, 6] address this by penalizing high-uncertainty predictions. While effective in reducing overestimation, this requires careful tuning of penalty coefficients and may restrict exploration during online fine-tuning. HiP3 avoids the use of fixed penalties by formulating optimism and pessimism as two separate objectives in a bi-objective optimization problem. This formulation produces a Pareto set of offline policies with different optimism-pessimism levels. During online fine-tuning, HiP3 performs state-dependent policy selection over these policies. 

B.3 Environment Model M

The environment model used by HiP3 consists of a bootstrap ensemble of N environment mod-els: M = {Mi}Ni=1 . Each member i of the ensemble is a probabilistic neural network, tak-ing state-action pairs (st, a t) as input and outputting a Gaussian distribution over the next state 16 and reward: ˆPi(st+1 , r |st, a t) = N (μi(st, a t), σ i(st, a t)) . The predicted reward is the ensemble mean of the reward components ˆr(st, a t) = 1

> N

PNi=1 μi,r (st, a t), where μi,r (st, a t) denotes the reward component of the joint predicted mean μi(st, a t). The predicted uncertainty u of the en-vironment model M is estimated as the maximum standard deviation among the ensemble, i.e., 

u(st, a t) = max Ni=1 || σi(st, a t)|| .This environment model is trained according to the design of MOPO [6]. Specifically, we trained an ensemble of 7 models, from which the 5 best models ( N = 5 ) in terms of the prediction errors are selected. Each model of the ensemble is independently trained using the maximum likelihood estimation with mini-batch stochastic gradient descent [49]. During the inference stage, the policy interacts with a randomly selected model from the N ensemble models, generating state-reward pairs from the corresponding distributions. This approach introduces variability by sampling different transitions from different models within a single episode, leading to a highly stochastic environment model. Previous works [6, 15] have shown that this environment model effectively mitigates the model exploitation problem in offline RL scenarios. The hyper-parameters of the environment model M are provided in Table 5. For the MuJoCo tasks, we adopt the standard MBPO/MOPO world-model configuration [6, 15] given their proven effectiveness in similar continuous control environments.

B.4 Multi-objective Optimization 

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) considers the simultaneous optimization of two or more potentially conflicting objectives [50]. Formally, given a decision space X and d objective functions 

fi : X → R for i ∈ { 1, . . . , d }, we consider the following vector optimization problem: 

max  

> x∈X

f (x) ≜  f1(x), f 2(x), . . . , f d(x).

The trade-offs among objectives arise because improving one objective may degrade another, so a single solution that simultaneously maximizes all objectives need not exist. MOO may therefore admit multiple Pareto-optimal solutions representing different trade-offs. We use the following standard definitions. 

Dominance. Given two solutions x1, x 2 ∈ X , we say that x1 dominates x2 (denoted x1 ≻ x2) if 

fi(x1) ≥ fi(x2), ∀i ∈ { 1, . . . , d },

and 

fj (x1) > f j (x2)

for at least one objective j.

Pareto optimality. A solution x⋆ ∈ X is Pareto-optimal if there does not exist another solution 

x′ ∈ X such that x′ ≻ x⋆.

Pareto set and Pareto front. The Pareto set is the set of Pareto-optimal solutions in the decision space: 

P⋆ = {x ∈ X | ∄ x′ ∈ X such that x′ ≻ x}.

17 The corresponding Pareto front is the image of the Pareto set in the objective space: 

F⋆ = {f (x) | x ∈ P ⋆}.

By providing Pareto-optimal solutions spanning different regions of the Pareto front, MOO enables different trade-offs to be preserved for subsequent selection according to task requirements. 

B.5 Comparison with linear scalarization 

Linear scalarization (LS) provides a natural baseline for multi-objective RL by combining multiple objectives through a fixed scalarization weight. In the context of model-based O2O RL, fixed linear scalarization introduces two structural limitations that are particularly relevant to optimism-pessimism trade-offs: First, linear scalarization can recover supported Pareto-optimal solutions by varying the scalarization weight, but it cannot in general recover unsupported Pareto-optimal solutions when the attainable objective set is non-convex [51]. HiP3 instead uses reference-vector-guided constrained optimization together with MGDA, and is therefore not restricted to the supported solutions induced by fixed linear scalarization weights. This allows MOSAIC to target trade-off regions that linear scalarization may miss. Second, a single LS objective fixes a global weight λ and therefore a single optimism-pessimism balance. This can be extended by training multiple LS policies with different weights and selecting among them online, as in our HiP3-LS baseline. However, each candidate remains tied to a predefined scalarization weight, and different weights can map to the same or similar supported solutions, which can produce redundant candidates and limit the diversity available for online selection. HiP3 instead directly targets distinct trade-off regions through reference vectors and then performs state-dependent policy selection over the resulting pool. For a controlled comparison, we construct HiP3-LS by replacing MOSAIC with fixed linear scalar-ization objectives. HiP3-LS trains five policies by maximizing

C Experimental Setup 

C.1 MuJoCo Simulation Environment 

We conduct the experiments on continuous control tasks using the MuJoCo [53] simulation envi-ronment. For offline pre-training, we use the standard D4RL Gym benchmark [9], which provides datasets with diverse qualities. Our HiP3 and all the baseline methods are tested on three tasks: Walker2d, Hopper, and HalfCheetah. For each task, these algorithms are evaluated on datasets with four different quality levels: random-v2, medium-v2, medium-replay-v2, and medium-expert-v2. These datasets are generated as follows. "Random" is generated by collecting 1 million steps of rollouts from a randomly initialized policy. "Medium" is generated by collecting 1 million steps of rollouts from a partially trained SAC. "Medium-replay" is generated by recording all samples in the replay buffer observed during training until the policy reaches a medium level of performance. "Medium-expert" is generated by mixing 1 million steps of expert-level data with 1 million steps of sub-optimal data. In the online stage, we categorize the experiments into two types of online tasks: the same online tasks and novel online tasks. For the former, the same offline task environments are used for the online tasks, while for the latter we introduce changes in both dynamics and reward structure, following common practices for inducing distribution shifts in continuous control benchmarks. Specifically, we modify the environments as follows: • Gravity increased from 9.8 → 11 .5

• Friction coefficients scaled by 1.2×

• Forward-reward weight scaled by 0.85 

• Control-cost weight increased to 0.2

• Noise scales are set to 0.2, 0.1, and 0.05 for HalfCheetah, Hopper, and Walker2d, respec-tively. These modifications introduce noticeable distribution shifts and increase task difficulty compared to the standard settings. 19 C.2 Implementation Details 

We first describe the baseline implementations. Cal-QL [2], AWAC [20], IQL [40] and CQL [11] are evaluated in the offline-to-online settings provided by CORL [41], using the official CORL implementations. For MBPO [15], PEX [3], WSRL [24], SO2 [27], FQL [30] and EDIS [29], we use the official implementations released by their authors. When conducting the comparison for PEX, we employed the same O2O setting provided by the official code. For MBPO, we learned the policies from scratch with the hyper-parameters provided by the official implementation. For FamO2O [23], we have referenced their published results, which involved running 1M steps for online fine-tuning. All O2O methods except FamO2O are pretrained for 1M offline steps and then fine-tuned for 100K online steps. MBPO is trained from scratch for 100K steps. For HiP3, candidate initialization evaluates each offline policy with one target-task rollout and selects the top-K candidates, using a rollout horizon of at most 500 steps per policy. The ranking trajectories are used only for candidate initialization and are not added to the online replay buffer or used for policy or model updates. The resulting candidate set is then fine-tuned for 100K online steps following the procedure described in Section 4. The reported 100K-step budget counts only environment transitions used for online fine-tuning. The one-time rollout-based candidate ranking uses additional target-environment interactions outside this fine-tuning budget.

C.4 Computational Cost 

HiP3 constructs multiple policies in the offline stage, but this does not multiply the total offline optimization budget by the number of policies. All three matched variants use the same total 1M-step offline policy-optimization budget and 100K-step online fine-tuning budget. For HiP3 and HiP3-LS, the offline budget is distributed across the policy pool rather than assigned independently to each policy. This matched protocol allows us to separate differences in wall-clock cost from differences in the total amount of policy optimization. Table 7 reports the end-to-end wall-clock cost of the three variants under the same model-based backbone, rollout configuration, hardware, and optimization budgets. The single-policy variant requires 5.8h for offline training and 8.4h for online fine-tuning, for a total of 14.2h. HiP3-LS increases the offline time to 6.3h while keeping the online time unchanged at 8.4h. Since the total offline optimization budget is fixed, this increase reflects the additional computation associated with maintaining and optimizing multiple candidate policies rather than additional policy-optimization steps. Full HiP3 requires 7.1h offline and 8.5h online, for a total of 15.6h. Relative to HiP3-LS, the additional offline computation is associated with MOSAIC: HiP3-LS uses fixed linear scalarization, whereas MOSAIC additionally evaluates reference alignment and combines multiple objective gradients through MGDA during optimization. MOSAIC further expands the policy pool through warm-started neighborhood search rather than training every additional candidate from scratch. After optimizing a policy for a reference vector, HiP3 reuses the resulting policy as the initialization for nearby reference vectors. This allows additional trade-off regions to be populated without linearly increasing the optimization budget with the number of candidate policies. The total offline policy-optimization budget therefore remains fixed at 1M steps. At the online stage, HiP3 fine-tunes only the policy selected at the current interaction step rather than updating all candidate policies. The policy pool therefore does not multiply the online policy-optimization cost. LinUCB is computationally lightweight, maintaining a matrix A ∈ Rn×d×d

and a vector b ∈ Rn×d, where n < 10 is the number of candidate policies and d < 50 is the observation dimension. Consistent with this design, the online wall-clock times of the three matched variants are nearly identical in Table 7. The reported online time for HiP3 also includes the one-time 20 rollout ranking used to initialize the candidate set, which takes less than 10 minutes in our MuJoCo experiments on the same NVIDIA A6000 setup.

D.5.1 Model and Training Sensitivity 

We first examine the sensitivity of HiP3 to world-model training. Table 17 varies the hyper-parameter max-model-epoch-since-update controlling model update epochs ( mmepoch ∈ { 1, 5, 10 }).
