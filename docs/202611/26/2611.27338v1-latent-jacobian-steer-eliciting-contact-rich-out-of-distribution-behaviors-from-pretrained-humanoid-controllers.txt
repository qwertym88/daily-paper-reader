## ABSTRACT 

Given a pretrained general latent-conditioned whole-body humanoid controller, say a behavioral foundation model (BFM), how can we elicit complex and contact-rich out-of-distribution behaviors? Raw joint-action exploration enables direct, task-specific adjustments but can disrupt coordination learned by the pretrained controller, whereas latent exploration retains that coordination but is difficult to steer toward desired physical effects. To combine the complementary strengths of these exploration spaces, we propose Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS), which cou-ples sampling in direct joint-action space with the semantics of the learned frozen controller’s input latent space. For a task specified through desired physical ef-fects, we sample task-relevant joint-action perturbations and map them through a damped local inverse of the policy Jacobian to obtain candidate latent ( z) com-mands. These commands are executed through the frozen pretrained policy, while exploration is directed toward the physical outcomes required by the new task. Candidate outcomes are evaluated in simulation and composed into latent-command sequences that accomplish the task. Ablations show that neither action-space nor latent-space exploration alone provides the same capability as their cou-pling. Across diverse out-of-distribution tasks, simulation-based searching using this method produces contact-rich behaviors, including hurdling, manipulating ar-ticulated objects, and opening doors through non-prehensile contact. Over ten seeds, LJS completes more of each humanoid task than when searching in either space alone. Beyond humanoids, it also steers a goal-conditioned dexterous hand policy through four assembly tasks better than searching in goal space directly. CEM-LJS (ours) 5/5 effects

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

For humanoid robots to become useful, they must be able to physically interact with objects and environments in diverse and often contact-rich ways. Recent advances in motion capture and general motion-tracking have enabled humanoids to reproduce an increasingly broad repertoire of dynamic whole-body behaviors (Chen et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026b). Yet obtaining behaviors for a new contact-rich loco-manipulation task remains considerably more task-specific. Such behaviors often require dedicated human demonstrations or trajectories reconstructed from video through Real2Sim pipelines that recover the relevant object and environment interactions (Dan et al., 2025; Ranawaka et al., 2026). High-fidelity simulation provides a natural way to generate such behaviors. Existing approaches using simulation either learn new behaviors from task-specific rewards (Sferrazza et al., 2024; Mu et al., 2024) or explicitly generate demonstrations and trajectories for them (Jing et al., 2025; Taouil et al., 2026). More recently, Behavioral Foundation Models such as Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero have instead pretrained general latent-conditioned whole-body controllers that can be prompted to-wards different motions, goals, and rewards (Tirinzoni et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b). These ap-proaches inspire our question: can we steer such a frozen controller to produce complex contact-rich behaviors that lie outside its training distribution, without collecting a task-specific motion demon-stration or training a new policy? We hypothesize that much of the whole-body coordination required for these behaviors is already present in a sufficiently general pretrained controller, even if it was never trained on such object-environment interaction data. This is fairly intuitive in hindsight. A human who has learned to move in free space can use these motion priors to play around with objects and the environment. The challenge is how to steer it towards a new physical interaction. Direct exploration in joint-action space makes task-specific changes easy to express, for example, moving a hand towards a desired contact, but it can disrupt the coordination learned by the controller. Exploration in its latent space preserves this coordination, but it is difficult to know which latent perturbation will produce a desired physical effect. Our key observation is that these two spaces are locally connected by the frozen policy itself: its Jacobian describes how perturbations in latent commands change the resulting joint actions. Based on this observation, we introduce Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS) (Figure 2), which couples precise exploration in joint-action space with coherent execution through the latent-conditioned pre-trained controller. Given a task-relevant joint-action perturbation, LJS uses a damped local inverse of the policy Jacobian to obtain candidate latent commands, which are then executed and evaluated in simulation. Complex tasks can then be described through simple sequences of physical effects: opening a door, for example, can be specified as (1) reaching and grasping the handle, (2) manip-ulating it to release the latch, and (3) pushing the door about its hinge. In this way, LJS provides a means of eliciting and collecting new contact-rich behaviors from a frozen whole-body controller without requiring a task-specific motion demonstration, a complete reference trajectory, or training a new task policy. We evaluate LJS over ten seeds on three contact-rich humanoid tasks and, beyond humanoids, on four precise assembly tasks with a frozen goal-conditioned dexterous hand policy. 

## 2 RELATED WORKS 

From motion tracking to contact-rich behavior discovery. General trackers such as TWIST (Ze et al., 2025) and SONIC (Luo et al., 2026) provide broad whole-body motion repertoires, while PGMT (Li et al., 2026a) and GigaBrain-WBC-0.5 (Cheng et al., 2026) incorporate terrain percep-tion and environment interaction. Motion generators such as OMOMO (Li et al., 2023) and CHOIS (Li et al., 2024) produce human–object interactions from object trajectories or language and spatial guidance, using captured interactions as training data. For a new contact-rich task, however, the movement that realizes the required interaction must still be obtained. Real2Sim pipelines recon-struct it from demonstrations (Dan et al., 2025; Ranawaka et al., 2026); contact-implicit trajectory optimization (Posa et al., 2014) and logic-geometric programming (Toussaint, 2015) optimize mo-tions and manipulation decisions. More recently, MotionDisco (Taouil et al., 2026) combines mo-tion search with trajectory optimization, while OmniContact (Yu et al., 2026b) chains skills through contact-flow representations. We investigate whether these interactions can instead be discovered by 2Preprint steering the existing coordination of a frozen whole-body controller, without supplying a reference motion or training a new task policy. 

Reusing and steering pretrained behaviors. Latent skill methods such as SPiRL (Pertsch et al., 2020), OPAL (Ajay et al., 2021), SkiMo (Shi et al., 2023), and ASE (Peng et al., 2022) provide reusable primitives for downstream learning or planning. METRA (Park et al., 2024b) and HILP (Park et al., 2024a) learn behavioral spaces before downstream tasks are specified, while forward-backward representations (Touati & Ollivier, 2021) support reward-based policy inference without retraining. This direction underlies whole-body behavioral models including Meta Motivo (Tirin-zoni et al., 2025), BFM-Zero (Li et al., 2026b), and FB-MEBE (Hu et al., 2026). Beyond whole-body control, Play2Perfect (Lum et al., 2026) pretrains a goal-conditioned dexterous hand policy through play and then fine-tunes it for each precise assembly task. Their behavioral coverage provides a useful starting point, but finding commands for precise new interactions remains difficult. Adapta-tion methods address this through parameter updates, skill-based learning, or action corrections, as in OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2025), SUPE (Wilcoxson et al., 2025), and residual RL (Johannink et al., 2019). Closer to our setting, FlowDAgger (Murray et al., 2026), UniSteer (Lu et al., 2026), and Flow Reversal Steering (Tang et al., 2026) map human or VLM corrections into the noise space of frozen generative policies. These works connect action-space guidance to latent execution. LJS instead discovers useful corrections from simulator-evaluated physical outcomes, without expert corrective actions or a learned steering policy. 

Searching through the policy’s local inverse. CEM (Rubinstein, 1999), iCEM (Pinneri et al., 2020), MPPI (Williams et al., 2017), and DIAL-MPC (Xue et al., 2025) search through simulated outcomes; SkiMo (Shi et al., 2023) and BFM-Zero (Li et al., 2026b) also demonstrate planning over learned behavioral spaces. Our question concerns how to construct useful proposals within a frozen controller. Structured exploration methods such as gSDE (Raffin et al., 2022), LATTICE (Chiappa et al., 2023), and DEP-RL (Schumacher et al., 2023) organize perturbations for coordinated control, while Safe Mutations (Lehman et al., 2018) and Policy Manifold Search (Rakicevic et al., 2021) use output sensitivities to guide exploration. Drawing on local inverse mappings used in damped inverse kinematics (Wampler, 1986) and operational-space control (Khatib, 1987), LJS couples joint-action proposals to latent execution through the policy Jacobian. CEM searches joint-command changes, a damped inverse maps them into latent perturbations, and simulator rollouts evaluate their physi-cal effects. The Jacobian describes the frozen actor’s local response, not contact dynamics. This coupling lets joint-action coordinates structure the search while the pretrained controller generates every executed command. 

## 3 LATENT JACOBIAN STEER 

We propose Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS), which couples the direct joint-action and latent search spaces. We search for task-relevant changes in the joint-action space, map them to changes in the latent command via the local Jacobian of the frozen policy, and evaluate the resulting behavior using physics simulation. Figure 2 gives an overview. 

Problem Formulation. We consider humanoid control in a simulator as a finite-horizon Markov de-cision process M = ( S, U, P, ρ 0, T ), where S is the state space, U is the joint-command space, P is the transition dynamics including the low-level controller, ρ0 is the initial-state distribution, and T is the task horizon. We specify the task objective separately via a physical-effect sequence T , defined below, whose scores guide the search and whose acceptance conditions determine task completion. The state st includes the robot and environment configurations and velocities, together with the con-troller memory needed to make the transitions Markovian. For the 29-DOF humanoid considered here, ut ∈ U ⊆ R29 specifies joint-position targets, realized through a joint-space PD controller. We have a frozen latent-conditioned pretrained policy ut = πθ (ht, z t), where ht contains the robot’s proprioceptive observations and action history, and zt ∈ Z is its latent command. For our particular purpose of demonstrating the adaptation of the pretrained policy over interesting out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks, we begin by concretizing how to define a task itself . It is worth noting that we chose this as our task definition for ease of expressing various tasks, and we are not proposing it as a canonical way to define loco-manipulation tasks. We define a task as an ordered sequence of physi-cal effects T = ( E1, . . . , E K ). Its forward-backward (FB) representation relates latent commands to the future states the policy visits, allowing motions, goals, and rewards to prompt the same controller without task-specific retraining. We defer the FB formulation and its prompting mechanisms to Appendix A.1. 3.2 DEFINING THE PHYSICAL EFFECTS FOR A CONTACT -RICH OOD TASK 

As described earlier, we define a task through a small sequence of measurable physical outcomes. To illustrate: for shutter traversal, this involves placing the palms beneath the shutter, lifting it, moving the head and torso through the opening, clearing it with the entire body, and standing upright on the far side. Each effect specifies what must happen while leaving the movements that realize it to the search. For example, lifting can require a minimum shutter height and sufficient upward support, with a score penalizing shortfalls and a separate acceptance condition determining completion. It is to be noted that defining these effects are not equivalent to dense reward engineering, as the underlying motion is not engineered and depends heavily on the frozen controller. We construct these effects stage-wise, using an LLM as a coding assistant to translate intuitive task descriptions into simulator measurements and scores. We inspect rollouts and refine definitions that admit unintended outcomes. We defer the mathematical score definitions, task-specific sequences, and acceptance conditions to Appendix L. 3.3 COUPLING OF THE LATENT AND DIRECT ACTION SPACE

How do we score and execute a candidate? We simulate each candidate for H control steps from the same saved simulator state and controller history, holding its latent command fixed while the frozen actor receives updated observations. We rank candidates by their mean physical-effect score over the latter half of the rollout, allowing time for the response to develop. Candidates violating full-segment constraints are rejected and excluded from the elite set. After all CEM rounds, we execute the lowest-scoring admissible candidate for L ≤ H steps. Its la-tent command becomes the new anchor; we recompute the Jacobian and restart CEM with zero-mean perturbations. We advance only when the executed segment satisfies the current effect’s acceptance conditions. Search ends when all effects are accepted, the budget is exhausted, or no admissible candidate is found. The exact rollout objective and search parameters are provided in the appendix. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate whether LJS can elicit contact-rich behaviors from a frozen whole-body controller, whether coupling joint-action proposals to latent execution improves task completion, and whether the same holds for a different pretrained controller. We run two sets of experiments: three contact-rich whole-body tasks with the frozen BFM-Zero G1 humanoid controller in MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012), and four precise assembly tasks with the frozen pretrained policy of Play2Perfect (Lum et al., 2026). Neither uses task-specific training or reference motions. Beyond task completion, we also examine search cost, motion quality, robustness to new starts and randomized physics, whether a search result can be used to train a closed-loop policy, a sanity check with minimal effect definitions, and search ablations. 4.1 CONTACT -RICH TASKS 

Humanoid tasks with BFM-Zero. We consider three tasks that require different combinations of contact establishment, whole-body support, object manipulation, and recovery (Figure 3). 

Hurdle traversal. The humanoid must cross a rigid hurdle using its body for support. It establishes palm and leg contacts, moves into a supported straddling configuration, transfers its body across the obstacle, places both feet on the far-side floor, and returns to standing. The ten effects require alternating support configurations, leg clearance, and recovery after crossing. 

Shutter opening and traversal. The humanoid must place both palms beneath a vertically sliding shutter, lift it, move its head and torso through the opening, clear the panel with its entire body, and stand upright on the far side. These five effects combine precise palm placement and orientation with upward support, crouched whole-body movement, and recovery. 6Preprint 

Door opening while carrying a cube. The humanoid approaches a cube on a stand, grasps its oppos-ing side faces, lifts it clear, and carries it to a closed door. It then uses the grasped cube to depress the handle and push the door open. The six effects require retaining the cube while manipulating another object. 

Assembly tasks with Play2Perfect. Our second set of experiments takes LJS beyond humanoids, to the pretrained play policy of Play2Perfect (Lum et al., 2026). This policy controls a Sharpa hand on a Franka arm through 29 joint targets and is conditioned on a 6-dimensional object goal. Play2Perfect fine-tunes the policy separately for each of its assembly tasks. We instead keep the pretrained policy frozen and only search over its goal input, on the same four tasks: placing the first and the second part of a beam assembly, screwing a leg onto its thread, and a tight peg insertion. Observe that the goal can be thought of loosely as a search variable that sequences and steers the policy over the whole episode, much like the latent command of BFM-Zero. Each task has three or four effects, for example picking up the leg, engaging the thread, turning it in, and releasing it for screwing. Here, LJS maps joint-action proposals through the Jacobian of the policy’s joint targets with respect to its goal. 

Other controllers and simulators. Since LJS only needs a frozen latent- or goal-conditioned policy and a simulator, we also show how it can be integrated with and used to steer the ASE (Peng et al., 2022) character controller to open a door and jump across a gap, the FB-MEBE (Hu et al., 2026) policy of a Go2 quadruped to push a box to a target, and the SPiRL (Pertsch et al., 2020) skill decoder of a robot arm to take a cube out of a cabinet. Finally, we also run BFM-Zero with LJS in a second simulator, Isaac Sim (Mittal et al., 2025), to carry a breadbox (Figure 3, Appendix I). 4.2 DEFINING THE PHYSICAL -EFFECT SCORES 

We construct effects stage-wise, beginning with an intuitive description of the required outcome. An LLM coding agent helps translate this description into simulator measurements, such as body-object distances, object orientation, contact forces, clearance, and velocity. These measurements define a score for ranking candidates and a separate acceptance condition for advancing to the next effect. They specify physical outcomes without prescribing the complete movement that realizes them. We inspect the resulting rollouts on development seeds and revise definitions that admit unintended solutions. Every definition is human-reviewed and held fixed during evaluation; the LLM does not observe the search online or select commands. The evaluation seeds are never used while designing the effects. This back-and-forth required less than five hours to finalize a task’s effects. All methods are evaluated with the same final task definitions. The mathematical definitions and an example Human–AI interaction card (Feng et al., 2026) are provided in Appendix L; practical guidance is given in Appendix M. 4.3 BASELINES AND EVALUATION 

Our main comparisons keep CEM as the optimizer and change where proposals are made and how they are executed. We additionally ablate CEM while retaining the LJS coupling. 

CEM-LJS. Our method searches over joint-action perturbations and maps each proposal into a latent command through the damped policy-Jacobian inverse. CEM fits its proposal distribution to the best simulated candidates, and the selected command is executed through the frozen actor. 

CEM direct-z. We apply CEM directly to the 256-dimensional latent command, following the latent-optimization approach demonstrated by BFM-Zero (Li et al., 2026b). This retains the opti-mizer and frozen-policy execution while removing the joint-action proposal space and Jacobian pull-back. Note that, by itself, BFM-Zero’s reward prompting cannot express our tasks, as its backward map sees only the robot’s body, not the objects and contacts the effects measure (Appendix A.1). 

CEM residual-BFM. CEM searches for additive corrections to the frozen actor’s joint-position targets. This retains joint-action proposals but removes the requirement that they be realized through latent commands, giving the search direct authority to override the actor’s output. 

Mixed-Gaussian LJS. This optimizer ablation retains the Jacobian pull-back and replaces CEM with Gaussian proposals. Below a physical-effect score threshold, finite-difference simulator probes 7Preprint estimate a targeted correction. The candidate population combines proposals around that correction and around zero, together with directional trials and the unchanged command. 

CEM direct-goal. For Play2Perfect, CEM searches the 6-dimensional goal with the same budgets. 

Evaluation. We evaluate every method on ten seeds that were never used while defining the tasks. For the humanoid tasks, all methods share one search setting: each candidate is simulated for 40 control steps, the selected command is executed for 20 steps, each decision evaluates 1,024 can-didates, and each effect allows at most 30 decisions. These settings are fixed across methods and seeds, to avoid any method-specific tuning. For Play2Perfect, candidates are simulated for 20 steps and executed for 10, with 1,024 candidates per decision. We report the mean number of accepted effects, the number of fully completed tasks, and the wall-clock search time on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. We ask the following questions to analyze whether LJS works as intended and what makes it robust. 

Does coupling the two spaces complete more of each task? CEM-LJS completes the most effects on every humanoid task (Table 1), significantly so on the shutter and door-cube (Appendix C), and the gap grows with how precise the required contacts are. The shutter is the clearest example. Supporting and lifting the panel needs both hands placed beneath its edge and pushing upward, and CEM-LJS is the only method that gets through it. Direct latent search keeps the controller’s coordination but cannot place the hands precisely enough to support the panel, so it almost never completes even the first effect. Residual search can move the hands to the right place, but overriding the joint targets also overrides the balance the controller provides. Its best run crawls under the panel with its torso on the floor and never stands back up (Figure 1). On door-cube, CEM-LJS completes the task on every seed, while residual search never gets the cube to the door. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented Latent Jacobian Steer (LJS), a method for eliciting contact-rich OOD behaviors from frozen latent-conditioned humanoid controllers. LJS proposes physically meaningful changes in joint-action space, pulls them back into the latent space through a damped local inverse of the pol-icy Jacobian, and evaluates the resulting latent commands through simulation. LJS can therefore be interpreted as an interface that makes behavioral foundation models precise . Importantly, LJS produces a sequence of latent commands (z1, . . . , z M ) rather than an open-loop trajectory of joint actions. While each command is held fixed for a short execution window, the frozen policy contin-ues to receive updated observations and generate new joint targets. These latent sequences can also serve as contact-rich demonstrations for behavioral cloning, reinforcement learning, or policy distil-lation; initialize subsequent task-specific optimization; and help probe which behaviors are present in a pretrained controller but difficult to access through direct prompting. The current method is an offline behavior-generation procedure. It requires privileged simulator measurements, saved-state resets, and many parallel candidate rollouts, making it unsuitable for direct real-time deployment. Its success is also limited by the behavioral support of the frozen controller, while the decomposition into physical effects remains human-specified and can admit unintended solutions. We show that a single LJS-generated behavior can already be distilled into a closed-loop policy that carries over to a second simulator, and a natural next step is the sim-to-real evaluation of such policies. We hope this simple and elegant method can give rise to new data-generation pipelines as well as learning paradigms that make use of pretrained controllers while not losing the authority of exploring in direct action space. LJS in-stead searches latent commands through the frozen actor’s Jacobian and simulator-evaluated physi-cal effects; it does not use F or B during search. Reward prompting also cannot express our tasks. The backward map B of the G1 controller takes only the robot’s own state, namely its joint positions and velocities and the positions, orientations, and velocities of its bodies, so a reward on the shutter, the cube, the door, or contact forces is not a function of its input. A prompted latent is also a single command for the whole task, while our tasks are ordered sequences of effects. A.2 CEM UPDATES FOR JOINT -ACTION SEARCH

## B WHY DOES LJS WORK , MATHEMATICALLY ?

A small change in the latent command can move some joints strongly and others barely at all. LJS accounts for this unequal sensitivity when constructing its proposals. Intuitively, if we choose some random (including 29-D) space over the action space itself, some degrees of freedom might be unfairly disadvantaged. Appendix B explains why LJS works. The policy responds very strongly to some latent directions and barely to others, so equally sized random changes in the latent command mostly move the joints along a few directions. The damped inverse of LJS corrects for this, so that equally sized joint-action proposals produce comparably sized changes in the joints. Two simpler explanations could also account for the results of Table 10, and we test both on all three tasks with the protocol of Appendix C. First, CEM adapts its search width separately along each axis it samples in, so LJS might help simply because the joint axes happen to be convenient axes for CEM. To test this, we rotate the joint-action proposals of CEM-LJS by one fixed random rotation. This keeps the initial proposals the same and only changes the axes along which CEM adapts. Second, LJS might help only because it searches the 29 latent directions that the policy actually responds to. To test this, we let CEM search along exactly these directions, the right-singular vectors of the policy Jacobian (∆z = V c ), but without the rescaling of LJS. With rotated axes, CEM-LJS completes as many shutter and door-cube tasks as before and a similar number of hurdle effects, although none of its hurdle runs reaches the final effects (Table 6). Over the three tasks, it completes 17 of 30 runs against 20 for CEM-LJS (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0 .60 ). Searching along the Jacobian’s latent directions without the rescaling behaves very differently. It completes only 9 of 30 runs ( p = 0 .009 ) and fewer effects on every task, though still far more than a random 29-dimensional latent subspace, which completes none. A natural suspicion is that its proposals are simply too small. Scaling them up until they move the joints as much as CEM-LJS proposals, however, still leaves it at 3 of 10 shutters. Together, these tests point to the two parts of LJS described above: the Jacobian selects the latent directions the policy responds to, and the damped inverse balances how strongly each of them moves the joints.

Retries and randomized physics. A CEM search can settle in a poor local optimum, for example when a hurdle effect is accepted in a pose that makes the next effect hard to reach. The retries largely remove the factor of luck from a single search, and the remaining trade-off is between robustness and wall-clock time.

## J A CLOSED -LOOP POLICY FROM ONE CEM-LJS ROLLOUT 

To see whether the behaviors LJS discovers can be used downstream, we trained a closed-loop policy from a single CEM-LJS rollout on a new task. Writing the effects, searching and training took three to four hours in total. This section describes the task, the search, the policy, and how we evaluated it. Videos of the policy, and a version that runs live in the browser, are on the project page. 

Task. The table is 0.75 m tall, 1.45 m long and 1.0 m deep, hollow underneath, with its long side against a wall. The G1 starts standing 0.55 m in front of the near edge. The eight effects ask for (1) both palms on the table top, (2) the right knee on the top, (3) the rest of the right leg on the top, (4) the chest down onto the table, (5) the whole body on the table, (6) a crouch on both feet on the top, (7) a rise onto both feet, and (8) standing up and settling. Throughout the climb, no body part may reach in under the table top, and nothing but the feet may touch the floor. As for the other tasks, an LLM coding agent helped translate these descriptions into scores and acceptance conditions (Section 4.2). 

Search. CEM-LJS found the climb with the frozen BFM-Zero controller, retrying failed effects and scoring every candidate under four randomized physics settings of friction, link masses, PD gains and control delay (Appendix F). The robot model applies the G1’s motor torque limits. The climb was searched in three parts, each continuing from the saved end of the previous one. The resulting climb lasts 34.4 s. 

Policy. SuperMimic (Liao et al., 2026a) learns a world model of the robot from simulator data, on 8-step windows, and improves the policy by back-propagating through 32-step rollouts imagined in that world model. We used its official code on mjlab with MuJoCo Warp and added the table. Each simulated world has its own randomized table, and the table’s near-edge point is part of the world-model state, so that imagined rollouts know where that world’s table is. The reference is the searched climb followed by a 1 s hold, at 50 Hz. It is worth noting that the search used BFM-Zero’s G1 model, while the policy runs on mjlab’s G1 model, which has the same kinematics but different 28 Preprint collision geometry. The reward is SuperMimic’s tracking reward with action-rate and joint-limit penalties. The actor sees five frames of on-robot measurements, each with uniform noise: the gravity direction in the pelvis frame ( ±0.05), the pelvis IMU angular velocity ( ±0.2 rad/s), joint positions with en-coder bias ( ±0.01 rad), joint velocities ( ±0.5 rad/s), its last action, and the table’s near-edge point in the pelvis frame with a visibility flag ( ±3 cm). The edge point is given only while the edge is more than 0.10 m ahead of the pelvis, as the head camera would see it. The actor also sees the reference joint targets at 10 future frames over 0.9 s and the reference pelvis orientation relative to its own. It is given no base linear velocity, no root position, and no state estimate. The table is redrawn every episode, with its near edge moved by up to ±4 cm toward the robot, 

±8 cm sideways, its height changed by up to ±3 cm, and its friction drawn from 0.5 to 1.1. Each simulated world also draws its own robot: PD gains scaled by 0.85–1.15 ( kp) and 0.8–1.2 ( kd) per actuator, torso mass scaled by 0.9–1.15, a torso centre-of-mass offset of up to ±2.5 cm forward and 

±5 cm sideways and vertically, foot friction from 0.3 to 1.2, and joint encoder bias. Training used 4,096 parallel worlds on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU and took 45 minutes, about 80 million environment steps. 

Evaluation. Each trial starts with the robot standing in front of the table and runs the whole climb in closed loop. A trial succeeds if the climb runs to the end without termination and the pelvis ends at least 0.6 m above the table top, inside its footprint, and within 20 ◦ of upright. An episode terminates if the pelvis drifts more than 0.5 m from the reference or its orientation drifts too far. For the second simulator, we export the actor and run it in standard MuJoCo on the CPU, with the same robot and table model, a 5 ms physics step and four physics steps per control step. Its observations are built from the simulator’s IMU and joint readings with the same rules as in training, and each trial draws the table’s placement, height and friction from the training ranges. Table 19 gives the results. All 21 failures on randomized tables in the training simulator were terminations for drifting from the reference. ## M TIPS FOR PRACTITIONERS 

Begin with an intuitive score. Highly constrained scores are not necessarily better scores. We first encode the obvious physical outcome and inspect the behavior produced by the search. We add terms only to address failure modes observed in the resulting rollouts, rather than trying to anticipate every possible failure in advance. 

Define the task one physical effect at a time. The terminal rollout of one accepted effect often reveals how the next effect should be specified. It may expose the relevant contact configuration, show which movement is currently blocked, or indicate that an intermediate physical effect is re-quired. We therefore found it more practical to construct long tasks stage-wise than to define every effect in advance. 

Inspect the motion, not only the scalar score. A lower physical-effect score does not always indicate useful progress, and a temporary score increase does not always indicate failure. The robot may need to make a short adjustment before further progress becomes possible, while a search can also exploit a missing condition and reduce the score through an unintended movement. Video replay remains the most useful diagnostic for distinguishing these cases. 

Avoid ending a stage in uncontrolled motion. Dynamic or unexpected terminal states are diffi-cult to recover from during later effects. When this occurs, the next score should not merely force the robot forward from an unsuitable state. It is usually better to revise the preceding effect to require settling, support, or bounded velocity before acceptance. 

Match the horizon to the physical response. Large body movements and heavy-object displace-ment may not develop within the same horizon as a small contact adjustment. Increasing the pre-diction horizon gives the simulator time to reveal the outcome of a candidate, while increasing the execution horizon can reduce the number of replanning decisions. Both can also make an incorrect candidate more costly. We therefore treat prediction and execution horizons as task-stage parame-ters. 36
