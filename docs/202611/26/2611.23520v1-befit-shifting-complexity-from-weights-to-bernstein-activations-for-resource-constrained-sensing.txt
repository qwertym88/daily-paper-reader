# BeFit: Shifting Complexity from Weights to Bernstein Activations for Resource-Constrained Sensing 

# Malak Gamal El-Din, Yasser Shoukry, Salma Elmalaki 

## Abstract 

Time-series sensing increasingly relies on neural networks, yet de-ployment on resource-constrained microcontrollers remains chal-lenging. Aggressive model compression can reduce accuracy, while restricting weights to multiplication-free representations intro-duces further losses. We present BeFit , an end-to-end framework that shifts model capacity from weights to learnable Bernstein polynomial activations. BeFit (i) finds compact Bernstein-activated convolutional neural networks (BeCNNs) through hardware-aware neural architecture search and trains them via knowledge distilla-tion; (ii) projects their weights to multiplication-free codes, freezes the codes, and recovers the resulting accuracy loss through the learnable activations; and (iii) generates model-specific kernels that exploit the projected weights for multiplication-free convolutions. On human activity recognition, BeCNNs are 2.75 percentage points (pp) more accurate on average than ReLU CNNs of identical archi-tecture. On a TI CC3220SF (Cortex-M4), weight projection speeds BeCNNs up by 2.2 × on average while losing 0.8 pp, compared with 2.4 pp for ReLU CNNs. Against the MLPerf Tiny DS-CNN refer-ence model running on TensorFlow Lite Micro with CMSIS-NN, the unprojected BeCNN achieves 1.0 pp higher accuracy, 2.2 × lower latency, and 6.4 × lower flash, while the projected model remains within 1.0 pp of the reference accuracy and runs 5.9 × faster. In emulation on a RISC-V core without a hardware multiplier, the instruction-count reduction relative to the DS-CNN increases from 5.2 × on the Cortex-M4 to 147 ×. BeFit also extends to transformers and MLPs, and across all tasks the final models stay within 1.56 pp of their float32 teachers on average.

## 1 Introduction 

Sensing applications such as human activity recognition (HAR) on wearables and keyword spotting (KWS) run neural networks continuously and close to the sensor, often on low-power micro-controllers (MCUs) with a few hundred kilobytes of memory and limited compute. Fitting these budgets requires shrinking the model and reducing costly multiplications, both of which lower accuracy. Recovering that accuracy within MCU budgets remains a challenge. Existing work addresses parts of this problem. Runtimes and ker-nel libraries [11, 25], hardware-aware architecture searches [6, 29] and memory co-design [55] fit models to the MCU, but keep fixed activations and weights that must be multiplied. Ternary and power-of-two quantization [27, 56] removes the multiplications but keeps

fixed activations, so the restricted weights must recover the lost accuracy on their own, which is hard in compact models. Existing MCU work on learnable activations, such as TinyKAN-HAR [26], keeps weights that still multiply. None of these approaches com-bines frozen multiplication-free weights with learnable polynomial activations to recover the resulting accuracy loss. We present BeFit , an end-to-end framework that turns a large pretrained network into a compact MCU model with multiplication-free convolutions. BeFit replaces a CNN’s conventional activations with learnable Bernstein polynomials [9, 21], forming a BeCNN. Next, it finds a compact BeCNN with a hardware-aware architecture search and trains it via knowledge distillation (KD). It then projects the convolutional weights onto a multiplication-free set, freezes them and recovers the lost accuracy through the activations, and finally generates kernels that exploit the projected weights for deployment. To our knowledge, BeFit is the first to use learnable Bernstein activations for sensing on MCUs, and the first to use them to recover the accuracy lost when weights are projected onto a multiplication-free set. The contributions of this work are as follows: 

• An end-to-end framework for MCU deployment. BeFit , built around learnable Bernstein activations, turns a large pretrained network into an integer MCU image through hardware-aware architecture search, knowledge distillation, weight projection with recovery, and kernel generation with lookup-table activations. 

• BeCNNs: Bernstein activations for sensing on MCUs. 

We bring learnable Bernstein activations to compact CNNs for sensing on MCUs, replacing their conventional activa-tions to form Bernstein-activated CNNs (BeCNNs). They re-store the expressiveness compact models lose under strong compression, at almost no additional inference cost. 

• Weight projection with activation recovery. We project the convolutional weights to multiplication-free codes and freeze them, then exploit the learnable Bernstein activations to recover the model’s lost accuracy. 

• Projection-aware kernel generation. BeFit generates architecture-specific kernels that exploit the projected weights for multiplication-free convolutions, and compiles each activation into a lookup table for efficient inference. 

• Evaluation on six tasks and a real MCU. We show that BeCNNs are more accurate than ReLU CNNs under strong compression and lose little accuracy after projection. We evaluate BeFit end to end on the TI CC3220SF [45] against TensorFlow Lite Micro [11] with CMSIS-NN [25] and TinyEngine [29]. We also test it in emulation on RISC-V with no hardware multiplier, and extend it to transformers and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs).

## 2 Background and Related Work 

MCU frameworks. TensorFlow Lite Micro (TFLM) [11] is an open-source MCU runtime used by MLPerf Tiny’s reference implementations [5]. Its interpreter executes a quantized model graph and calls a kernel per operator, taken from CMSIS-NN [25] on Arm Cortex-M. While TFLM runs a given model, hardware-aware architecture searches design models that fit the MCU: MCUNet [29] co-designs the search with its own inference engine, and MicroNAS [23] targets time-series classifiers under memory and latency limits. However, these works keep standard layers with fixed activations and multiplied weights. BeFit instead changes the building blocks themselves, with learnable Bernstein activations and multiplication-free weights, and builds the search, training and kernels around them. 

Learnable activations. Padé Activation Units [33] learn ra-tional activations end to end, over an unbounded input range. Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) [31] replace each weight of a multilayer perceptron with a learnable one-dimensional function on its edge, and these functions can be compiled into lookup tables on MCUs [24]. TinyKAN-HAR [26] applies one learnable spline per neuron after each linear layer of a fully connected network, stored as a lookup table, and keeps int8 weights; it targets zero-shot recog-nition and explainability. Faggi et al. [14] pair a CNN backbone with a KAN classifier head, but the convolutions keep fixed activations. 

BeFit instead places one learnable Bernstein curve per filter or layer inside a standard CNN, compiles each into a lookup table, and uses these curves to recover the accuracy lost to compression and multiplication-free weights. 

Bernstein polynomial networks . Khedr and Shoukry [21] in-troduce DeepBern-Nets, feed-forward networks in which standard activations are replaced by learnable Bernstein polynomials, and use their coefficient bounds to certify image classifiers. Albool et al. [2] prove that their approximation error decays exponentially in the network depth, against a polynomial rate for ReLU networks. Bern2Edge [12] later distills feed-forward networks into Bernstein networks for FPGA deployment, either as lookup tables or as sym-bolic rules. BeFit instead brings Bernstein-activated CNNs to time-series sensing on resource-constrained MCUs, and exploits them to recover the accuracy lost to multiplication-free convolutions. 

Multiplication-free weights. Ternary ( {0, ±1}) and power-of-two ( {0, ±2𝑘 }) weights need no multiplier. Ternary Weight Net-works (TWN) [27] approximate each filter by a scale times ternary codes and train latent full-precision weights through the quantizer, and INQ [56] quantizes weights to powers of two or zero in steps, retraining the remaining weights after each step. DeepShift [13] trains power-of-two weights so that each multiply becomes a shift and a sign flip, and ShiftCNN [18] converts pretrained CNNs to power-of-two weights for custom accelerators. All keep fixed acti-vations and recover accuracy, when needed, through the weights. ReActNet [30] binarizes weights and activations and adds learnable shifts and slopes to the sign and PReLU functions, but still trains the weights. BeFit instead freezes the projected codes and recovers accuracy through the learnable Bernstein curves. To our knowledge, it is the first to do so with learnable polynomial activations. Sensing systems have also cut multiplications by leaving stan-dard networks behind: Bakar et al. [4] run Tsetlin machines on batteryless MCUs, whose inference evaluates Boolean clauses and sums their votes without any multiplication, and Fast-Inf [10] re-duces the multiply count with binary tree-based networks whose cost grows logarithmically with network size. Both, however, give up the CNN. BeFit instead keeps the convolutional backbone with multiplication-free weights. 

Kernel generation. Generated kernels are usually specialized to the model’s structure. TinyEngine in MCUNet [29] generates kernels and memory schedules for each layer’s shape, and Lupe [53] converts network descriptions into code for ultra-low-power MCUs, but both still load and multiply the weights. Other work specializes the code to the weight values. On FPGAs, unrolled ternary net-works [48] store the weights in the routing of pruned adder trees. On MCUs, ATAMAN [3] writes int8 weights into generated Cortex-M7 kernels, but still multiplies, and Neuro-C [40] fixes ternary connections on a Cortex-M0, but has no convolutions. In contrast, 

BeFit generates multiplication-free convolution kernels for MCUs directly from its projected weights (Section 3.5). 

## 3 BeFit Framework 

BeFit turns a large pretrained teacher into a compact integer model for MCUs. Its central idea is to shift model capacity from the weights to the activations: the weights are made fewer and then multiplication-free, while learnable Bernstein activations carry the expressiveness the weights give up. This trade suits MCUs because the activations are cheap where the weights are not. Each activation curve compiles into a fixed-size lookup table, so its inference cost is small and independent of its degree. The building block is the BeCNN (Section 3.1), a standard CNN whose fixed activations are replaced by learnable Bernstein curves (Fig. 2). Around it, BeFit 

runs four modules (Fig. 1). (1) A hardware-aware NAS (Section 3.2) finds a compact BeCNN under the MCU’s memory limits, trading accuracy against instructions measured on the target’s own build. Because the polynomial degree does not change these costs, the search treats it as a free dimension and can raise the curves’ expressiveness at no additional deployed inference cost, although training cost grows (Section 4.1). (2) Knowledge distillation (Section 3.3) then trains the selected BeCNN in full from the teacher. (3) Projection and recovery (Section 3.4) remove the convolutions’ multiplications: the convolutional weights are projected onto multiplication-free codes and frozen, and the accuracy lost to projection is recovered by retraining the Bernstein curves rather than the weights. (4) 

Projection-aware kernel generation (Section 3.5) specializes the inference kernels to the multiplication-free codes. Each Bernstein activation becomes a single table load, and requantization a shift. The result is an integer model whose convolutions need no multiplier. Section 3.6 extends the same pipeline to transformers and MLPs.

Why Bernstein activations. Two properties suit Bernstein activations to resource-constrained MCUs. ## 3.2 Hardware-Aware Neural Architecture Search (NAS) 

The first step of BeFit (Fig. 1) is a hardware-aware search for the right BeCNN. In a conventional CNN with fixed activations, added capacity costs instructions. In contrast, a BeCNN can increase its representational capacity by raising the Bernstein polynomial de-gree without increasing deployed inference cost. The search there-fore chooses the architecture and the polynomial degree together, trading accuracy against computation under the target MCU’s mem-ory limits.

## 3.3 BeCNN Training via Knowledge Distillation 

The second step of BeFit (Fig. 1) trains the student BeCNN found by the NAS. Since the search only trains each candidate briefly and on labels alone, knowledge distillation (KD) from the pretrained teacher now trains the student in full, before any weight projection.

## 3.4 Projection and Recovery via Learnable Activations 

The third step of BeFit (Fig. 1) projects the trained BeCNN’s dense convolutional weights to multiplication-free codes and recovers the lost accuracy through the learnable Bernstein activations (Fig. 3). 

3.4.1 Projection to multiplication-free codes. Projection re-stricts each filter’s weights to a small set of values that need no multiplication. We first write the weights of filter 𝑗 as a vector w𝑗 

and replace it with 𝛾 𝑗 p𝑗 : one positive scale 𝛾 𝑗 for the whole filter times a vector of integer codes p𝑗 from a code set P. We choose between two code sets, ternary (P = {0, ±1}) and powers of two 

(P = {0, ±1, ±2, ±4}) . The scale 𝛾 𝑗 adds no run-time multiply: re-quantization uses only a right shift, and the remaining scale is folded into the activation’s lookup table (Section 3.5.2). 3.4.2 Recovery through learnable Bernstein activations . Pro-jection lowers the model’s accuracy, and conventional methods recover it by retraining the weights [27, 56]. BeFit instead freezes the projected codes and trains the Bernstein coefficients, along with the convolutional biases, per-filter scales and classifier, in one training run. Because the coefficients directly control each activa-tion’s shape (Eq. (1)), training them reshapes each filter’s response without changing its frozen weights, which recovers the accuracy lost to projection. Section 4.5 shows how much of the recovery the polynomial coefficients carry alone. Recovery trains on the deployed int8 model through fake-quantized forward passes with straight-through gradients (Sec-tion 3.5.2). It uses self-distillation with the loss of Eq. (4), with the unprojected BeCNN as the teacher, and keeps the epoch with the best validation accuracy. The result is a network with multiplication-free convolutions: only the classifier stays int8 and multiplies. Section 3.5 generates a kernel that exploits these weights for MCU deployment, and Section 4.5 gives the ReLU baseline, the ablations and the results. 

## 3.5 Projection-Aware Kernel Generation and Inference 

The last step of BeFit (Fig. 1) turns the trained BeCNN into integer code for the MCU. The projection-aware generated kernel (the gen-erated kernel for short) runs projected models ( BeFit projected ), the 

dense kernel runs unprojected ones ( BeFit dense ), and every Bern-stein activation becomes a lookup table. 

3.5.1 Kernel generation . Projection alone does not make in-ference cheaper: a generic kernel still loads and multiplies every weight, zeros included. The savings come from how the kernel stores and applies the fixed codes. BeFit supports three options: weights as code for speed, weights as data for flash savings, and a mix of the two (Fig. 4). 

Weights as code. The codes are known at compile time, so a generator writes them directly into the program (Fig. 4, panel 3a). Each weight multiplies one tap, so the generator applies one rule per code in P (panel 2): a zero emits no instruction, ±1 adds or subtracts the input, and ±2𝑘 adds or subtracts it shifted by 𝑘 . Each filter becomes straight-line code with no weight loads and no loop over weights. This is the fastest option, at the cost of more flash, since every nonzero code becomes instructions. 

Weights as data. To save flash, the nonzero codes can instead be stored as index lists read by one shared loop (Fig. 4, panel 3b). Each power of two has an add list and a subtract list: for every nonzero code, the loop loads its index, loads that input and adds or subtracts it, doubling the sum between powers. Zeros take no space and all filters share one loop, so this option needs the least flash, at the cost of higher latency from one extra index load per code. It suits large models where flash is the limit. 

Mix. The mix sits between these two ends of the trade-off: weights are stored as code for the parts of the model that save 

> 5Gamal El-Din et al.

the most time per byte, and the rest are stored as data. Section 4.6 compares the three options. 

Architecture-aware. The generated kernel also exploits the Cortex-M4’s architecture in three ways. (1) One register, two posi-tions. An int16 input buffer places the inputs of two consecutive out-put positions in the two halves of one 32-bit register, so each SADD16 

or SSUB16 updates both. (2) Reuse across filters. With weights as code, one input load serves a chunk of up to four filters, instead of a separate load for each. (3) Power groups. SADD16 has no built-in shift, so shifting every input would cost extra instructions. Instead, each filter adds its inputs into one accumulator per power of two, then combines them once per output by doubling and adding (Fig. 4, panel 4). For example, weights (1, 2, −4, 1) on inputs (𝑥 0, 𝑥 1, 𝑥 2, 𝑥 3)

give 𝑔 1 = 𝑥 0 + 𝑥 3, 𝑔 2 = 𝑥 1 and 𝑔 4 = −𝑥 2, and output 𝑔 1 + 2(𝑔 2 + 2𝑔 4).

3.5.2 Lookup-table activations . The kernel’s sum 𝐴 𝑗 then passes through the filter’s Bernstein activation. A polynomial is too costly to evaluate at run time on an MCU, but a Bernstein curve’s input lies in the fixed range [0, 1] (Eq. (1)), so it can be precomputed into a lookup table. Bern2Edge [12] introduced such tables for FPGAs, with linear interpolation that costs one multiply per activation. BeFit removes this multiply: each input maps directly to one table entry, so an activation is a single table load.

Scale-aware tables. A shift divides only by powers of two, so it cannot match the filter’s scale 𝑠 𝑗 exactly. Correcting this at run time would take a multiply, so each table instead stores the curve at the inputs its entries actually receive, adapting scale-aware activation tables and shift-based scaling [43, 49]. The remaining constants also need no run-time work: the table’s output scale and offset fold into the classifier, and BatchNorm and the pooling average fold into adjacent layers. 

Same cost at any degree. A curve of any degree compiles to the same 𝐸 -entry table, so the degree changes only the stored values, not the flash ( 𝐸 bytes per curve), RAM or instructions (one load per output). High-degree polynomials therefore add expressive-ness under compression and capacity for recovery at no additional deployed inference cost. The resulting convolution needs no multiplications: weights become additions, requantization a shift and the activation a table read. Only the int8 classifier still multiplies.

## 3.6 Extensions to Other Architectures 

BeFit is not specific to convolutions: any linear layer followed by a scalar activation can use the same pipeline. In a transformer, per-unit Bernstein curves replace the feed-forward network’s ac-tivation (GELU or Swish), and projection makes the linear layers multiplication-free; only the attention products, which multiply two activations, keep their multiplications. In an MLP, each hid-den neuron gets one curve, and the hidden rows are projected to multiplication-free codes. Section 4.7 evaluates both.

## 4.5 Projection and Recovery 

Projecting the distilled students. Table 10 projects each distilled student to powers of two, recovers it (Section 3.4; recipe in Appen-dix A) and runs it on the generated kernel (Section 3.5). ## 4.7 Beyond CNNs: Transformers and MLPs

4.7.1 Keyword transformers . We replace the GELU feed-forward (FFN) activation of a KWT-1-style transformer [7] with one degree-15 Bernstein curve per hidden unit. GELU becomes one 256-entry table shared by all units of a layer, and each Bernstein unit its own 64-entry table (Section 3.5.2); both run on BeFit ’s kernels.

## 5 Limitations and Future Work 

Remaining multiplications. The int8 classifier still multiplies. In the keyword transformers, the attention products 𝑄𝐾 ⊤ and softmax ·𝑉 multiply two activations, so there are no weights to project. These products are 20–34% of the unprojected model’s multiply-accumulates, which limits the speedup. Multiplication-free or lookup-based attention is a natural next step. 

On-device adaptation. The projected codes are frozen, and training only the Bernstein coefficients recovers most of the pro-jection loss (Section 4.5). This suggests updating only the curves on the device, for example to adapt to a new user or to sensor drift, while the weight code stays fixed in flash. 

Energy and hardware coverage. We report latency, flash and RAM on a single Cortex-M4 board. The RISC-V results without a hardware multiplier come from emulation. Future work will mea-sure energy per inference on the board. It will also run BeFit on multiplier-less RISC-V silicon and on Cortex-M cores with vec-tor extensions, where the trade-off between projected and dense weights may change. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented BeFit , an end-to-end framework that shifts model capacity from the weights to learnable Bernstein activations, so that compact MCU models keep their accuracy while their convolutions become multiplication-free. Across six sensing tasks, BeCNNs out-perform ReLU CNNs of identical architecture, lose little accuracy after weight projection, and run faster than strong MCU baselines, with gains that grow on cores without a hardware multiplier. These results suggest that learnable activations are an effective place to put model capacity when weights must be small and cheap.

## A Dataset and Build Details 

Dataset preprocessing. HAR windows are 128 samples at 50 Hz (2.56 s, 50% overlap), lie within single-label runs, and are standard-ized per channel on training statistics. UCI-HAR keeps total accel-eration and body gyroscope. HHAR uses the accelerometer and gyroscope of the six phones that record both, resampled to 50 Hz, without the null label. PAMAP2 uses the ±16 g accelerometer and gyroscope of its three IMUs, with missing samples interpolated, resampled from 100 Hz, without the transient label 0. The DS-CNNs’ 12 Speech Commands classes are ten words, silence and unknown, with 30 ms frames, 20 ms hop, 40 mel bins and 10 coefficients [5]. UJIIndoorLoc splits its official training file 70:30 [22] and tests on its official validation file. 

Teacher models. Table 17 lists every teacher. ResNet, DeepSense and DeepConvLSTM are trained on our splits with their published recipes: Adam at 10 −3, halved on plateau, batch 64 (ResNet [20, 50]); Adam at 10 −4, 𝛽 = (0.5, 0.9), L2 5 × 10 −4, batch 64 (DeepSense [54]); RMSProp at 10 −3, dropout 0.5, batch 100 (DeepConvLSTM [36]). On top of these, they use the students’ augmentation and train for at most 500 epochs, with early stopping on validation accuracy (patience 100). DeepSense splits each 128-sample window into 8 in-tervals of 16 samples, because our windows are shorter than the pub-lished 5 s. The DS-CNN is the released MLPerf Tiny model [5, 32], and its int8 network is the released TFLite file. AST is the released checkpoint MIT/ast-finetuned-speech-commands-v2 [17], which we convert to int8 with the TFLite Converter on 500 training

## D Neural Architecture Search Details 

The HAR and Speech Commands searches took 11.1 and 33.0 GPU-hours, including pruned trials. Candidates of non-dominated rank 

≤ 2 are evaluated over three seeds: 163 BeCNN and 166 ReLU models. Figure 7 shows the resulting fronts. On all three HAR datasets, the BeCNN envelope lies above the ReLU envelope at most instruc-tion counts, with the largest gaps at the smallest models. It peaks at 94.93% against 93.29% on UCI-HAR, 93.32% against 91.44% on HHAR and 87.18% against 85.85% on PAMAP2. On HHAR and PAMAP2 the BeCNN peak needs fewer instructions. On Speech Commands, the BeCNN leads almost everywhere below 0.9M instructions, by up to 6.13 pp. Between 0.9M and 3M the two alternate, and above 3M the BeCNN leads by at most 1.09 pp.
