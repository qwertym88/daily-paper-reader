BARE: Body-Aware Reflexive Evasion for Humanoid Robots
Zihao Li, Tong Chen, Hua Song, Zitong Ding, Zihao Xu, Ce Hao, and Weibang Bai∗
Abstract— Dynamic obstacle avoidance is essential for safely
deploying humanoid robots in real-world environments, where
rapidly approaching objects require immediate evasive responses.
Recent advances in reflexive evasion have focused primarily
on quadrupeds, which typically rely on large-scale whole-body
maneuvers. In contrast, humanoids can exploit their rich body
articulation to evade through small, localized body adjustments.
By moving threatened body regions without repositioning the
entire body, these responses may require less execution time
and less surrounding free space. However, local adjustments
can alter mass distribution or support conditions, making it
challenging to achieve rapid evasion while maintaining whole-
body stability. To this end, we propose BARE (Body-Aware
Reflexive Evasion), a humanoid reflexive evasion framework
guided by human motion priors that enables rapid local evasion
while maintaining whole-body stability. A risk-guided motion
adapter is designed to select reference motions from a retargeted
human evasive motion library based on the threatened body
region and to adjust their temporal phases based on collision
urgency, allowing the robot to reach evasive postures before
potential contact. Conditioned on the adapted reference and
link-level collision risk, a reflexive evasion policy learns through
reinforcement learning to coordinate local body adjustments
with whole-body stabilization. We validate BARE in simulation
and real-world experiments, demonstrating its ability to perform
targeted local evasion while maintaining whole-body stability.
Videos are available at https://mayberobolab.github.
io/BARE-DOA/.
I. INTRODUCTION
Avoiding collisions with surrounding objects is a fun-
damental requirement for deploying humanoid robots in
real-world environments [1], [2]. Unexpected contact can
interrupt task execution and compromise balance, potentially
causing falls and damage to the robot or its surroundings [3].
Existing approaches address this challenge through visual
path planning [4], [5], collision-free footstep generation [6],
and online gait adjustment [7], [8], enabling humanoid robots
to navigate around clutter and moving obstacles.
However, walking-based avoidance may be insufficient
when dynamic obstacles approach rapidly at close range.
Planning delays and the time required to execute movements
within actuator limits can leave the robot unable to avoid

create clearance. Recent work has advanced this capability on
quadrupeds. REBot learns evasion and recovery policies for
rapid obstacle avoidance, while APREBot and UEREBot
extend these capabilities through active perception and
navigation–evasion coordination in complex environments

to evade obstacles through large-scale whole-body maneuvers,
such as jumping, lateral stepping, or retreating. In contrast,
humanoid robots have more degrees of freedom and can
exploit local body adjustments, such as retracting an arm
or lifting a leg, to create clearance without repositioning
the entire body. Such small-scale, targeted responses can
reduce both the execution time and the free space required
for evasion, which is especially advantageous when obstacles
approach rapidly at close range. Nevertheless, local evasive
motions can alter the robot’s mass distribution or support
configuration, making rapid local evasion while maintaining
whole-body stability a key challenge.
To address this challenge, we propose BARE (Body-Aware
Reflexive Evasion), a framework for humanoid reflexive
evasion guided by human motion priors. It adjusts coordinated
retargeted human evasive motions based on collision risk
across body regions, enabling targeted local responses within
a short response window while maintaining whole-body
stability. The framework comprises two components. A Risk-
Guided Motion Adapter evaluates link-level collision risk
from the robot state and relative obstacle motion. When risk
exceeds a threshold, the adapter selects a reference from a
library of retargeted human evasive motions according to the
threatened body region and adjusts its phase according to
collision urgency so that key evasive poses are reached in time.
A Reflexive Evasion Policy generates joint-position targets
from the adapted reference, link-level collision risk, and robot
and obstacle states. Through reference-guided reinforcement
learning, the policy learns to move threatened body regions
out of the obstacle’s path while maintaining whole-body
stability.
We evaluate BARE in simulation and on a physical Unitree
G1 to assess evasion performance within a short response
window across obstacle arrival times, approach directions,
and target heights. Ablation studies evaluate the contributions
of risk-conditioned motion selection, phase adaptation, and
the reward design.

II. RELATED WORK
Human motion tracking and learned motion priors provide
structured guidance for coordinated skills in physically
simulated characters, as demonstrated by DeepMimic and
AMP [12], [13]. H2O and OmniH2O extend human-motion
learning to real humanoid whole-body teleoperation, while
ASAP addresses dynamics mismatch between simulation and
real robots for agile skills [14]–[16]. BeyondMimic further
develops versatile control through motion tracking and guided
diffusion [17]; parkour and table-tennis systems demonstrate
terrain traversal and rapid whole-body interaction [18], [19].
Alongside these advances in humanoid motion capabilities,
progress has also been made in safe navigation. iWalker
learns visual path and footstep planning, FootstepNet gener-
ates collision-free footstep sequences, and model predictive
contouring control enables Digit to overtake moving obstacles
[4], [6], [7]. These approaches avoid obstacles by adjusting
walking paths and gait. Even with rapid planning updates,
executing the required motion may exceed the remaining
response window against close-range, rapidly approaching
threats.
To handle rapidly approaching dynamic obstacles, re-
searchers have explored avoidance methods that connect envi-
ronmental perception with immediate motion responses. For
UAVs, event-camera-based perception and end-to-end learning
policies have demonstrated rapid responses to dynamic
obstacles [20]–[22]. For legged robots, REBot combines
reinforcement learning-based evasion and recovery policies,
enabling quadrupeds to avoid incoming obstacles within a
short response window and regain stability [9]. APREBot
introduces active perception combining LiDAR and cameras
to improve awareness of dynamic threats from different
directions [10]. UEREBot further coordinates navigation and
reflexive evasion with safety constraints, supporting both goal
progress and high-speed obstacle avoidance in unstructured
environments [11]. These quadrupedal methods predominantly
evade threats through whole-body maneuvers such as jumping,
lateral displacement, or retreating. The richer articulation of
humanoids offers opportunities for localized responses, but
these motions must remain coordinated with whole-body
balance. Exploiting this capability for timely, stable, and
targeted evasion requires further investigation.

IV. METHOD
This section presents BARE, a framework for humanoid
reflexive evasion. We first outline the framework and its
operation (Sec. IV-A), then introduce the risk-guided motion
adapter, which selects reference motions and adjusts their
timing according to link-level collision risk (Sec. IV-B).
Finally, we describe how the reflexive evasion policy learns
local evasion and whole-body coordination from the adapted
references, together with its reward design and training
procedure (Sec. IV-C).
A. Framework Overview
BARE comprises a risk-guided motion adapter and a
reflexive evasion policy (Fig. 3). The adapter integrates the
Body-Link Risk Estimator, Ref. Motion Selection, and Ref.
Phase Adaptation modules shown in the figure. Offline, human
evasive motions are recovered from videos and retargeted
to build a library containing local evasive motions and
whole-body displacement maneuvers. Online, the adapter
estimates link-level collision risks from relative robot–obstacle
motion. Once risk reaches the activation threshold, it selects
a reference suited to the threat and adapts its phase to the
obstacle motion. Conditioned on the adapted reference, the
link-level collision-risk vector, and robot and obstacle states,
the reflexive evasion policy produces joint-position targets to
execute evasion and recovery.

C. Reflexive Evasion Policy
The adapted motion provides a coordinated reference, but
successful evasion also depends on robot dynamics, execution
errors, and immediate obstacle motion. The reflexive evasion
policy conditions on proprioception, relative obstacle state,
link-level collision risk, and reference state to convert the
reference into executable evasive behavior. Denoting its input
by zt, following residual learning that combines a nominal
signal with a learned correction [25], the policy produces
a joint residual δqt = πθ(zt) and forms the control target
around the reference joint position ¯qt as:
ut = ¯qt + β wt ⊙δqt,
(5)
where β is the residual scale, wt is a joint-wise binary
residual mask associated with the selected reference motion,
and ⊙denotes element-wise multiplication. This mask con-
centrates corrections on joints relevant to the current response,
while the reference itself retains motion needed for whole-
body coordination. Local evasion therefore does not require
the remaining body regions to stay still: lifting a leg, for
example, may involve coordinated adjustments of the support
leg and torso.
The residual mask is determined by the selected reference
motion. Let Ak,i(ϕt) denote the activity weight of joint i
in reference motion k. During evasion and recovery, we set
wi,t = 1[Ak,i(ϕt) > 0], and disable residual corrections upon
completion. The mask restricts residual corrections, while
the reference motion retains whole-body coordination for
balance.

execution, and motion stability. To clarify their roles, we
group the reward terms by function:

The evasion terms penalize insufficient clearance at threat-
ened links and actual robot–obstacle contact, encouraging
timely clearance creation. Contact detection covers the entire
body so that moving the initially threatened region does
not hide a collision involving another link. Reference terms
guide position and velocity tracking for relevant joints,
organizing high-dimensional exploration around coordinated
human evasive motions [17]. For lateral movement and retreat,
reference base displacement is also tracked to preserve the
motion required for whole-body displacement maneuvers.
The locality term accounts for the number of joints actually
moved, horizontal base displacement, and joint power. It
is enabled when the collision-free clearance condition is
satisfied, encouraging local and efficient execution among safe
responses. Regularization terms penalize action changes, joint-
limit violations, and self-collisions, with additional penalties
for falls or instability terminations. Together, these objectives
encourage threatened-region evasion while retaining whole-
body balance and recovery. Reward trade-offs do not provide
formal collision-avoidance or stability guarantees; both are
evaluated using the criteria in the problem formulation.
We optimize the learned policies with proximal policy
optimization (PPO) [26]. Training combines staged learning
with joint optimization to coordinate reference execution
and risk-conditioned motion adaptation. Staged learning
establishes the capabilities required by each component, while
joint optimization further coordinates phase adaptation and
executed evasion, with reference selection rules held fixed.

A. Experimental Setup
Task. We use a Unitree G1 humanoid with 29 actuated
joints in mjlab with the MuJoCo backend [27]. Each trial
starts from the same nominal standing posture. Threatening
projectiles are randomly generated within predefined body-
target regions, with horizontal bearings spanning [0◦, 360◦).
Projectile size is held consistent across methods, and initial
positions and velocities are constructed consistently from
target positions, directions, and arrival times. Arrival time
Tarr denotes the interval from attack onset to predicted first
contact if the robot maintains its nominal standing posture.
We use three groups: short [0.10, 0.50) s, medium [0.50, 1.50)
s, and long [1.50, 3.00] s. Trials are randomly sampled within
each interval with seeds independent of training. We retain
attacks predicted to hit the nominal posture. Main comparisons
and ablations share the attack distribution; all methods use
the same robot model, attacks, obstacle-state information, and
success criteria.
Metrics. We report avoidance success rate (ASR), peak
total power (PTP), and average movement distance (AMD).
ASR is defined as ASR = Nsuccess/Ntotal, where the
numerator counts successful trials and the denominator counts
all trials. Success requires collision-free obstacle passage, no
instability during evasion and recovery. ASR is reported as a
proportion from 0 to 1; all tabulated values use two decimal
places. PTP is PTP = maxt
P
i |τi(t) ˙qi(t)|. Joint mechanical
power is evaluated at each 5-ms physics step, and PTP is the
maximum summed absolute joint power over the evaluation
interval, reported in kW. AMD measures the mean horizontal
base displacement from the activation position during evasion
and recovery, in metres. ASR includes all valid attacks; PTP
and AMD are averaged over successful trials.
Baselines. REBot (G1, adapted) follows the reinforcement-
learning evasion and recovery approach of REBot, using
a reference-free G1 policy trained on the common attack
distribution [9]. FootstepNet (G1) serves as a navigation-
based avoidance baseline based on footstep planning; its
original method uses reinforcement learning to generate
feasible collision-free footstep sequences [6]. Each method
retains its own response decision mechanism.

VI. REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
We deploy BARE on Unitree G1. Onboard estimation
provides proprioception, while OptiTrack tracks obstacles
equipped with motion-capture markers; velocities are esti-
mated from successive positions and transformed into the
robot frame. An operator attacks from the front, sides, and
rear using ball-on-rod thrusts and sweeps, kicks, disc throws,
and horizontal fist sweeps.
B. Real-Robot Results
The robot starts from a stable standing posture, performs
an evasive maneuver when an obstacle approaches, and then
returns to stable standing. Figure 6 presents representative
responses to attacks targeting the upper, middle, and lower
body regions, demonstrating reference-guided evasion and
subsequent recovery on hardware. BARE achieves a 57%


avoidance success rate in real-world experiments on Unitree
G1. Success follows the simulation criteria: no robot–obstacle
contact during obstacle passage, no instability during evasion
and recovery, and a return to stability. The performance
gap between simulation and real-world experiments may
stem from sensing and control latency, imperfect actuator
response, and differences in ground contact dynamics, which
can affect timely evasion and stable recovery. Differences
between physical attacks and simulated projectiles may also
contribute to this gap.
VII. CONCLUSION
Humanoid articulation offers the potential to evade incom-
ing obstacles through local body adjustments, but coordinating
these responses with whole-body stability within a short
response window remains challenging. We presented BARE,
a humanoid reflexive evasion framework guided by human
motion priors. By selecting reference motions according to
link-level collision risk and adapting their execution phase to
threat urgency, BARE guides a learned policy to coordinate
targeted evasion with balance and recovery. Simulation results
demonstrate more effective evasion than the baselines across
tested arrival-time groups, with less whole-body movement
and lower peak power demands during successful responses.
Ablations support the contributions of risk-conditioned motion
selection, phase adaptation, and the locality reward. Real-
world experiments on Unitree G1 demonstrate evasion and
recovery against diverse threats, highlighting the potential of
human motion priors and risk-guided reference adaptation
for humanoid reflexive evasion.
Despite its effectiveness, our method has several limitations.
The current system relies on external motion capture for
obstacle states, and reliable evasion under the tightest response
constraints remains challenging. Evasion on uneven terrain
and under constrained support conditions also remains to
be evaluated. Future work will integrate onboard sensing
and active observation for autonomous threat detection and
tracking, and incorporate terrain and contact states into
reference adaptation to extend stable evasion to more complex
environments.
