ABSTRACT
Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control is central to online planning for
model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL), but its iterative refinement incurs
planning latency. Reducing refinement iterations often degrades performance,
motivating stronger policy guidance learned from the planner. However, existing
methods typically learn from the planner through action-level alignment, which
does not directly capture the complete H-step planner proposal. Moreover, plan-
ner imitation leaves further target improvement to online refinement. To address
these limitations, we propose FaSt World PlANner (SWAN), an advantage-aware
MPPI-based MBRL framework that explicitly represents and improves the com-
plete H-step planner proposal to improve policy guidance for efficient online
planning. For proposal representation, SWAN combines k-MPPI, a multimodal
MPPI extension, with a mixture of factor analyzers (MFA) action-chunk policy to
capture multiple modes and correlations across time and action dimensions. For
proposal optimization, we introduce Advantage-Guided Proposal Optimization
(APO), which reuses MPPI evaluations to construct an improved target using ad-
vantage estimates and trains the policy toward this target while preserving planner
alignment. Experiments across 31 high-dimensional continuous-control tasks from
DMControl, HumanoidBench, and MyoSuite show that SWAN substantially im-
proves control performance and sample efficiency while using 50% fewer MPPI
refinement iterations than state-of-the-art planning-based MBRL methods. Finally,
SWAN demonstrates zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a physical Unitree Go2,
achieving stable velocity-tracking locomotion with high-frequency control. (see
our demo at https://swanfastworldplanner.github.io/).

INTRODUCTION
Recent model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) methods such as TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al.,
2024) and BMPC (Wang et al., 2025a) combine learned world models and policies with Model
Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, a sampling-based model predictive control (MPC) algorithm,
to optimize action sequences online (Williams et al., 2017; Hansen et al., 2022; 2024; Wang et al.,
2025a; Zhan et al., 2026). In these MPPI-based methods, the policy guides candidate generation
and MPPI improves action sequences through iterative refinement. However, repeated trajectory
evaluation introduces planning latency, while reducing refinement iterations leaves less opportunity
to correct poor candidates (Williams et al., 2017; Pinneri et al., 2021). Prior work (Anthony et al.,
2017; Silver et al., 2017) on expert iteration shows that policies learned from a planner can improve
subsequent search. Thus, we seek better policy guidance through learning from the planner to improve
control performance with fewer MPPI refinement iterations (Sacks & Boots, 2023).

A central obstacle to improving policy guidance
is policy-planner mismatch between learned
policies and planner-derived action distributions.
Specifically, in prior critic-guided methods, the
critic learns from planner-generated data but
bootstraps with actions from the learned pol-
icy, while the resulting value estimates guide
policy updates and provide terminal values for
MPPI. Thus, policy-planner mismatch can lead
to inaccurate value estimates, affecting both pol-
icy improvement and planning (Wang et al.,
2025a; Serra-Gomez et al., 2026). To reduce the
mismatch, recent MPPI-based methods either
learn the policy through planner imitation (Wang
et al., 2025a) or retain critic-guided updates
with planner-derived regularization (Zhan et al.,
2026; Serra-Gomez et al., 2026). However, two
gaps remain in representing and improving the
complete planner proposal. First, the proposal representation gap. Existing alignment objectives
operate on individual actions or the planner’s first-action distribution, rather than the complete H-step
action-chunk distribution. Consequently, the learned policy does not directly learn the temporal
correlations and multiple modes represented by the weighted action chunks. To examine the gap,
we extend BMPC with our action-chunk proposal representation while retaining planner imitation,
yielding BMPC-Chunk. As shown in Fig. 1 (left), BMPC-Chunk outperforms BMPC under the
same MPPI refinement budget on Dog-Run, motivating direct representation of the complete planner
proposal. Second, the proposal optimization gap. Even with an action-chunk representation, direct
critic-guided policy updates remain susceptible to policy-planner mismatch. To reduce the mismatch,
planner-derived regularization encourages planner alignment in critic-guided updates, but the trade-
off with value maximization can leave residual mismatch. In contrast, planner imitation directly
uses the current planner proposal as the learning target, leaving further target improvement to MPPI
refinement. As shown in Fig. 1 (right), BMPC-Chunk remains sensitive to the number of refinement
iterations, motivating additional proposal improvement in policy learning while preserving planner
alignment.
Overcoming both gaps poses two technical challenges: i) efficiently represent the multimodal action-
chunk planner proposal, and ii) optimize the proposal using advantage estimates while preserving
planner alignment during policy learning. To address both challenges, we introduce FaSt World
PlANner (SWAN), an advantage-aware MPPI-based MBRL framework that improves policy guidance
through explicit representation and improvement of the complete H-step planner proposal. For
proposal representation, SWAN proposes k-MPPI, a multimodal extension of MPPI, with a Mixture
of Factor Analyzers (MFA) action-chunk policy. k-MPPI maintains multiple search modes through
standard MPPI refinement within each mode, while the MFA policy models correlations across
time and action dimensions and supports direct action-chunk sampling. For proposal optimization,
SWAN introduces Advantage-Guided Proposal Optimization (APO). APO uses advantage estimates
from MPPI trajectory evaluations to construct an improved target under a KL constraint relative to
the current planner proposal. We then train the policy toward the improved target under a separate
constraint that preserves planner alignment. Advantage information therefore enters policy learning
through target construction rather than direct critic gradients through policy actions. Experiments on
31 high-dimensional continuous-control tasks across DMControl (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020),
HumanoidBench (Sferrazza et al., 2024), and MyoSuite (Caggiano et al., 2022) show that SWAN
improves control performance and sample efficiency with 50% fewer MPPI refinement iterations than
the MPPI-based baselines. We further confirm the gains in Unitree G1 and Go2 simulations (Zakka
et al., 2026), then demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to a physical Go2 with stable velocity
tracking at an average 308.16 Hz.
Our contributions are as follows. 1). We propose SWAN, an advantage-aware MPPI-based MBRL
framework that improves online planning efficiency through better policy guidance. 2). We propose a
novel approach that jointly introduces k-MPPI and an MFA policy to explicitly represent multiple
proposal modes and correlations across time and action dimensions. 3). We introduce APO to improve
the planner proposal using advantage estimates while preserving planner alignment. Theoretically,

RELATED WORK
Model-based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL). Recent MBRL learns world models to predict the
consequences of actions for policy learning or planning (M. Moerland et al., 2023). Based on how the
learned world model is used, existing methods can be grouped into two categories: 1) imagination-
based methods optimize policies from model-generated trajectories (Hafner et al., 2019a; 2020; 2023;
Janner et al., 2019), 2) planning-based methods optimize action sequences online (Hafner et al.,
2019b; Sikchi et al., 2022; Hansen et al., 2022; 2024). LOOP (Sikchi et al., 2022) learns dynamics
in the environment state space and uses an actor-derived prior to constrain trajectory optimization
and reduce actor divergence. For planning in learned latent world models, TD-MPC and TD-MPC2
use MPPI as an online planner with learned policies guiding candidate generation (Hansen et al.,
2022; 2024). To improve policy learning, subsequent MPPI-based methods reduce the mismatch
between learned policies and planner-derived action distributions. BMPC (Wang et al., 2025a)
learns a policy through imitation of the planner’s first-action distribution. BOOM (Zhan et al., 2026)
combines critic-guided policy updates with soft value-weighted alignment to planner actions stored
in replay. PO-MPC (Serra-Gomez et al., 2026) learns an adaptive prior from the planner’s first-
action distribution to regularize policy optimization. However, these methods incorporate planner
information through one-step action policy learning. In contrast, SWAN directly represents the
complete H-step planner proposal and uses advantage estimates from MPPI trajectory evaluations to
construct an improved target while preserving planner alignment.
Multimodal Action-Chunk Policies. Recent robot policies use diffusion and flow matching to
implicitly model high-dimensional, multimodal action-chunk distributions (Chi et al., 2025; Chisari
et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025). D-MPC (Zhou et al., 2025) also learns diffusion action proposals
from offline trajectories for online MPC. Furthermore, for MPPI-based MBRL, nonlinear dynam-
ics and non-convex control objectives can produce separated high-value regions in action-chunk
space (Lambert et al., 2021; Honda et al., 2024). Return-weighted MPPI candidates can there-
fore contain multimodal structure, motivating diffusion or flow matching for learning the planner
proposal. However, online proposal learning requires fast sampling and adaptation to changing
planner proposals. Iterative generation introduces control latency (Wang et al., 2025b), while direct
policy-gradient backpropagation through diffusion sampling can be computationally expensive and
unstable (Ma et al., 2025). We thus combine k-MPPI with an MFA action-chunk policy to explicitly
model the planner proposal, supporting direct sampling and capturing correlations across time and
action dimensions.
Advantage-Guided Policy Learning. Prior model-free RL methods optimize policies using esti-
mated advantages (Schulman et al., 2015; 2016; 2017; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018). Subsequent work
incorporates estimated advantages into policy learning by weighting actions from historical, replay,
or offline data (Wang et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019; Kostrikov et al., 2022). Recent SPO (Macfarlane
et al., 2024) uses advantage-weighted SMC search to construct first-action targets with exact environ-
ment models for policy fitting. In this paper, we propose APO, which applies advantage estimates
from MPPI trajectory evaluations to improve the current complete planner proposal, and then trains
the policy toward the improved target under a separate planner-alignment constraint.

PROPOSED SWAN
Motivation. Although the methods such as BMPC and PO-MPC described in Sec. 3 reduce policy-
planner mismatch, two gaps remain in learning the complete planner proposal πP as follows.
4


1) Proposal representation gap. Existing alignment objectives operate at the action level, whereas the
complete planner proposal factorizes as
πP (τ | zt) = πP,0(at | zt)πP (at+1:t+H−1 | at, zt).
(3)
Matching πP,0 therefore does not determine the conditional distribution of the remaining actions.
2) Proposal optimization gap. Even with complete action chunks, critic-guided updates remain
susceptible to mismatch since the temporal difference (TD) bootstrapping follows πθa, which need
not match πP,0. To reduce the mismatch, planner-derived regularization balances planner alignment
against value maximization. In contrast, planner imitation uses πG
P,0 as the learning target. However,
extending imitation to complete chunks still uses the current πP as the learning target, leaving further
target improvement to MPPI refinement.
Proposed Method. To address both gaps, we introduce FaSt World PlANner (SWAN), an advantage-
aware MPPI-based MBRL framework shown in Fig. 2. The proposed method combines k-MPPI and
an MFA action-chunk policy for complete proposal representation with APO to improve the learning
target while preserving planner alignment, thereby improving policy guidance for efficient online
planning. We detail the two novel components below.

EXPERIMENTS
We first evaluate SWAN in terms of (i) control performance, sample efficiency, and online planning
efficiency, (ii) the benefit of complete H-step planner proposal representation with k-MPPI and MFA
under matched planning budgets, and (iii) whether APO improves control performance over planner
imitation under reduced MPPI refinement budgets. We further evaluate SWAN on Unitree G1 and
Go2 in simulation, then deploy it on a physical Go2 robot for real-world validation.
Benchmarks. We evaluate 31 tasks comprising 7 from DMControl (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020),
14 from HumanoidBench (Sferrazza et al., 2024), and 10 from MyoSuite (Caggiano et al., 2022),
spanning locomotion and manipulation with maximum action dimensions of 38, 61, 39, respec-
tively. Additional Unitree G1 and Go2 tasks (Zakka et al., 2026) have 29 and 12 action dimensions.
Appendix D lists all tasks and descriptions.
Baselines. We compare against planning-based BMPC (Wang et al., 2025a), BOOM (Zhan et al.,
2026), and PO-MPC (Serra-Gomez et al., 2026), imagination-based DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023),
and model-free FPO (McAllister et al., 2026) and SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018). For action-chunk
comparison, BMPC-DP replaces BMPC’s one-step policy with a diffusion policy (Chi et al., 2025),
retaining MPPI and learning the complete H-step planner proposal through planner imitation.
Implementation Details. We match environment steps across methods. In the full-budget com-
parison, MPPI-based baselines use M = 8 refinement iterations, while SWAN uses M = 4. In the
half-budget comparison, all MPPI-based methods use M = 4. We provide detailed implementation
settings and hyperparameters in Appendix E.
5.1
MAIN RESULTS
Control performance and sample efficiency. As shown in Figs. 3, 4, and 5, we compare SWAN’s
control performance and sample efficiency with baselines under full- and half-budget settings. Each

5.2
SIM-TO-REAL TRANSFER
We further train and evaluate SWAN on simulated Unitree G1 and Go2 velocity-tracking tasks (Zakka
et al., 2026), where it outperforms BMPC in final return and sample efficiency with half as many MPPI
refinement iterations per task. We then demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to a physical
Go2. The robot tracks forward, backward, and lateral velocity commands, turns in both directions,
and follows figure-eight trajectories. Motion capture measurements over selected figure-eight and
straight-line segments give planar velocity mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 0.126 and 0.149 m/s, with
yaw angular velocity MAEs of 0.112 and 0.068 rad/s, respectively. We further assess locomotion
stability with external pushes during motion, observing stable locomotion without falling. With only
9


M = 3 MPPI refinement iterations per control step, the controller operates at average 308.16 Hz on
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090. We detail simulation and real-world results in Appendix H. Overall,
our SWAN achieve reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer ability in stable high-frequency locomotion
control. Demonstrations are available at https://swanfastworldplanner.github.io/

CONCLUSION AND LIMITATION
We proposed SWAN, an advantage-aware MPPI-based MBRL framework that improves online plan-
ning efficiency through better policy guidance. SWAN combines k-MPPI with an MFA action-chunk
policy to capture multimodal proposal structure and introduces APO, which uses advantage estimates
to construct an improved target from the planner proposal while preserving planner alignment during
policy learning. Extensive experiments across multiple continuous-control benchmarks demonstrated
strong control performance and sample efficiency while requiring fewer MPPI refinement iterations.
We further demonstrated SWAN’s zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to a physical Unitree Go2 for locomo-
tion. One limitation is SWAN’s current focus on state-based, task-specific continuous-control settings.
Future work will extend SWAN to multimodal observations and multi-task learning, enabling shared
proposal learning across diverse tasks and sensory modalities.

DETAILED EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
This section provides additional experimental details for the results reported in Sec. 5. We first
describe the common training and evaluation protocol, followed by the implementation settings
of the baselines. We then provide the hyperparameters specific to SWAN and finally describe the
benchmark rewards.
E.1
TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Training details.
All benchmark experiments are conducted in an online reinforcement learning
setting, where agents continuously collect environment transitions during training. For fair com-
parison, we match the total number of environment steps across methods on each task. We define
environment steps as the number of agent decision steps multiplied by the environment action repeat.
The action repeat is 2 for DMControl and 1 for HumanoidBench, MyoSuite, Unitree G1, and Unitree
Go2. We train agents for 4M environment steps on DMControl, 2M steps on HumanoidBench and
MyoSuite, 2M steps on Unitree G1, and 1M steps on Unitree Go2. All simulation experiments are
conducted with three independent random seeds on single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU.
23


For MPPI-based methods, we consider two planning-budget settings. In the full-budget comparison,
BMPC, BOOM, PO-MPC, and BMPC-DP use M = 8 MPPI refinement iterations, whereas SWAN
uses M = 4. In the half-budget comparison, all MPPI-based methods use M = 4. The number of
candidate trajectories per refinement iteration is kept unchanged when varying M, such that reducing
the number of refinement iterations directly reduces the number of model-rollout evaluations during
planning.
Evaluation protocol.
We evaluate the current controller every 50,000 agent steps, corresponding
to 100,000 environment steps on DMControl, and using 10 evaluation episodes per task. MPPI-based
methods retain online planning during evaluation using the refinement budget associated with the
corresponding experimental setting. For DMControl, HumanoidBench, Unitree Go2, and Unitree G1,
we report the mean episode return, whereas for MyoSuite we report the task success rate.
E.2
BASELINE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
We compare SWAN with the planning-based MBRL methods BMPC (Wang et al., 2025a),
BOOM (Zhan et al., 2026), and PO-MPC (Serra-Gomez et al., 2026), the imagination-based MBRL
method DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023), and the model-free methods FPO (McAllister et al., 2026)
and SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018). For BMPC, BOOM, and PO-MPC, we follow algorithm-specific
hyperparameters reported in their original implementations, and use the same model architectures
and optimization settings across these methods for fair comparison. We detail specific and common
hyperparameters of these planning-based methods in E.3. The only modification in the half-budget
setting is reducing the number of MPPI refinement iterations to M = 4, while all other settings
are kept unchanged. For DreamerV3, FPO, and SAC, we use their default configurations for all
tasks across all benchmark suites and match the environment-interaction budget to that of the other
methods.
We additionally construct BMPC-DP by replacing BMPC’s one-step policy with a diffusion policy
that generates complete H-step action chunks, while retaining the original MPPI planner and planner-
imitation objective. The diffusion policy is parameterized by a conditional 1D residual CNN operating
over the action sequence, with four residual blocks, a hidden width of 512, and kernel size 3. During
training, we use a DDPMScheduler with 1,000 diffusion timesteps. During inference, action
chunks are generated using a DDIMScheduler with 20 denoising steps.

HYPERPARAMETER SELECTION FOR SWAN
Further, we show the hyperparameter selection of proposal modes K, the MFA rank parameter r, and
the APO target temperature λ through comparisons on Dog-Run. As shown in Fig. 11 (left), based
on BMPC-MFA, increasing r from 0 to 1 provides consistent significant return gain for both K = 1
and K = 2, supporting the importance of modeling correlations across time and action dimensions.
However, increasing r from 1 to 2 provides no consistent return gain for either K = 1 or K = 2,
supporting r = 1 with fewer covariance parameters. The middle panel further shows that K = 2
improves returns over K = 1, while K = 4 provides no further gain. For APO, λ = 0.1 achieves the
highest final return among the five tested values in the right panel. We therefore use K = 2, r = 1,
and λ = 0.1 as the default SWAN settings.

FINAL PERFORMANCE AND SAMPLE EFFICIENCY
We provide complete final-performance and sample-efficiency statistics for Sec. 5.1, reporting full-
and half-budget comparisons separately. SWAN uses M = 4 MPPI refinement iterations in both
settings, while the MPPI-based baselines use M = 8 and M = 4, respectively.
For each method, task-level evaluation curves are averaged over three random seeds. Suite-average
curves use evaluation steps shared by all included tasks and seeds, averaging first across tasks within
each seed and then across seeds. The HumanoidBench suite average includes 13 tasks, excluding
Reach. For each task or suite average, Final is the last observed value of the corresponding mean
evaluation curve, without smoothing or averaging over a terminal window. Because individual task
curves may extend beyond the last shared evaluation step, suite-level Final need not equal the average
of task-level Final values.
We measure sample efficiency by T80, the first observed environment step at which a method’s mean
evaluation curve reaches 80% of SWAN’s Final for the corresponding task or suite average. All
methods therefore share the same reference threshold. We report T80 in millions of environment steps,
with lower values indicating better sample efficiency. The symbol “–” indicates that the threshold is
not reached within the recorded evaluations.
Analysis. Under the full-budget comparison in Tables 6, 8, and 10, SWAN achieves the highest suite-
average performance across all three benchmarks despite using only M = 4 refinement iterations.
On DMControl, SWAN obtains an average return of 898.8 with T80 = 0.60M, compared with 883.7
and 1.00M for PO-MPC with M = 8. On Dog-Run, SWAN improves the final return over BOOM by
67.2, while reducing T80 from 2.80M to 1.30M; on Humanoid-Run, it improves over BMPC by 25.8
with T80 reduced from 2.10M to 1.40M. On HumanoidBench, SWAN reaches an average return of
688.0 with T80 = 0.90M, compared with 604.0 and 1.25M for PO-MPC. The gains are particularly
pronounced on Run and Sit Hard, where SWAN improves the final return over PO-MPC by 112.2
and 92.0, while reducing T80 by 37.9% and 62.5%, respectively. On MyoSuite, SWAN achieves
88.0% average success with T80 = 0.55M, compared with 81.0% and 1.05M for BMPC. On Pen
Twirl, SWAN reaches 100% final success with T80 = 0.25M, whereas the MPPI-based baselines
reach at most 66.7% and do not reach the shared threshold.
Under the matched M = 4 half-budget setting in Tables 7, 9, and 11, the gaps generally increase.
SWAN improves the suite-average return over PO-MPC by 25.3 on DMControl and 110.5 on Hu-
manoidBench, while reducing T80 by 50.0% and 37.9%, respectively. On MyoSuite, SWAN improves
the average success rate over BMPC by 13.7 percentage points and reduces T80 by 63.3%. The wider
gaps therefore indicate that the competing MPPI-based methods benefit more from additional refine-
ment, whereas SWAN maintains strong control performance and sample efficiency under the reduced
planning budget. Moreover, BMPC and BOOM no longer reach the HumanoidBench suite-average
threshold with four iterations, while PO-MPC and BOOM no longer reach the MyoSuite threshold.

UNITREE SIM-TO-REAL TRANSFER
Unitree G1 and Go2 Locomotion. In this section, we evaluate on velocity tracking tasks for
Unitree G1 and Go2 in MJLab (Zakka et al., 2026) to assess whether SWAN’s performance and
sample-efficiency gains extend to robot locomotion. As shown in Fig. 18, SWAN outperforms
BMPC in final return and sample efficiency while using half as many MPPI refinement iterations
on both tasks. Specifically, BMPC uses M = 8 and M = 6 on G1 and Go2, respectively, whereas
SWAN uses M = 4 and M = 3. The sample-efficiency advantage is particularly pronounced on
G1, demonstrating effective learning on the higher-dimensional task despite the smaller refinement
budget.

To quantify tracking during figure-eight and
straight-line walking, we compare velocity com-
mands with body-frame velocities from motion capture (mocap), as shown in Fig. 19. Using aligned
measurements before visualization smoothing, we compute the planar velocity error at each sample
as the Euclidean norm of the forward and lateral velocity errors. For yaw angular velocity, we use the
absolute difference between measured and commanded values. We then summarize tracking over
each plotted interval using mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE), computed
as the mean error magnitude and the square root of the mean squared magnitude, respectively, with
equal weight per sample.
For figure-eight and straight-line walking, the planar velocity MAEs are 0.126 and 0.149 m/s,
respectively, with RMSEs of 0.144 and 0.161 m/s. The corresponding yaw angular velocity MAEs
are 0.112 and 0.068 rad/s, with RMSEs of 0.177 and 0.084 rad/s.
We further assess locomotion stability by applying external pushes during motion. The robot
maintains stable locomotion without falling. With only M = 3 MPPI refinement iterations per
control step, the controller operates at an average frequency of 308.16 Hz, demonstrating high-
frequency control under a limited online planning budget. Real-world demonstrations are available at
https://swanfastworldplanner.github.io/.
