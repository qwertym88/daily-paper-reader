Abstract
Gradient-based actor optimization is central to deep reinforcement learning. How-
ever, conventional first-order optimizers do not explicitly exploit the local curvature
of neural actor losses. We propose EQUIP (Curvature-Equilibrated Gradients for
Policy Search), a novel approach that seeks to balance local contraction and expan-
sion of the actor parameter space by equilibrating positive and negative curvature.
EQUIP constructs a trajectory-informed search subspace and uses the projected
Hessian of actor loss to build a bounded local metric for curvature equilibration.
First-order loss matching preserves the approximated first-order decrease in actor
loss. Meanwhile, gradient mixing and curvature-based gradient selection adjust
the gradient applied for actor optimization. We establish the geometric properties
of curvature equilibration, derive bounds on gradient alignment and magnitude,
and identify conditions for improved actor loss reduction and convergence toward
stationarity under direct gradient updates. We integrate EQUIP into Soft Actor–
Critic (SAC) without modifying its actor loss, critic learning, or replay mechanism.
Experiments on 6 MuJoCo continuous control benchmarks show that the resulting
EQUIP-SAC achieves higher mean sample efficiency than SAC and two second-
order policy optimization baselines. Ablation studies further highlight the benefits
of curvature equilibration and trajectory-informed geometry for policy training.

Introduction
Gradient-based policy optimization is fundamental to deep reinforcement learning (DRL). Actor–
critic algorithms, such as DDPG, TD3, and SAC, optimize neural policies using actor-loss gradients
guided by learned action-value functions (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Fujimoto et al., 2018; Haarnoja
et al., 2018a). Despite their success, actor optimization remains challenging because neural actor
losses are highly nonconvex and evolve as critics and training data change. Common first-order actor
optimizers do not explicitly exploit the local curvature of the actor loss. Yet, curvature information can
help identify more promising actor update directions (Maniyar et al., 2024; Khorasani et al., 2025),
offering valuable opportunities for greater loss reduction and more effective policy optimization.
Geometry-aware and second-order methods seek to exploit such information. For example, natural
policy gradient uses the Fisher information matrix to adjust policy updates (Kakade, 2001), while
Hessian-based methods use local curvature to construct actor optimization directions (Maniyar et al.,
2024; Kordabad et al., 2025). However, curvature computations can be expensive for neural actors
with a large number of trainable parameters. Moreover, negative or near-zero Hessian eigenvalues
may cause inverse-curvature updates to produce ascent directions or excessively large actor parameter
changes (Dauphin et al., 2014). These limitations demand for alternative approaches that exploit
curvature information efficiently and reliably.

A promising direction arises from the local geometry of gradient-based actor optimization. For
sufficiently small gradient updates, positive and negative curvature contribute respectively to local
contraction and expansion of the actor parameter space (Dauphin et al., 2014; Chen and Huang, 2023).
While both effects can benefit optimization, excessive contraction or expansion may undermine
optimization performance and stability.
We take a new geometric perspective by considering the balance between these competing effects as
a way to guide actor optimization. Rather than suppressing curvature or approximating its inverse, we
investigate how equilibrating positive and negative curvature can lead to more effective and reliable
actor updates.
Motivated by this geometric insight, we propose EQUIP (Curvature-Equilibrated Gradients for
Policy Search), a general mechanism for curvature-aware actor optimization. EQUIP constructs a
low-dimensional policy search subspace from recent actor gradients. It then estimates the projected
Hessian using Hessian–vector products and constructs a bounded local metric to equilibrate positive
and negative curvature, enabling curvature-aware gradient adjustments without computing or inverting
the full Hessian. Periodically refreshing and reusing the local geometry further reduces computational
overhead.
Since curvature equilibration alone does not guarantee improved actor updates, EQUIP further intro-
duces first-order loss matching, gradient mixing, and curvature-based gradient selection techniques.
First-order loss matching preserves the approximated first-order decrease in actor loss achievable
by the raw gradient, while gradient mixing controls the adjustment strength and bounds the mixed
gradient norm. Gradient selection then favors the mixed gradient when the local curvature suggests
a greater decrease in actor loss than the raw gradient. Together, these mechanisms exploit local
curvature while maintaining controlled actor updates.
We integrate EQUIP into SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018a), resulting in EQUIP-SAC. EQUIP modifies
only the gradient supplied to the actor optimizer, while preserving SAC’s actor loss, critic learning
procedure, and replay mechanism. Experiments on 6 MuJoCo continuous-control benchmarks
demonstrate that EQUIP-SAC achieves higher mean sample efficiency and evaluation returns than
SAC and two second-order policy optimization baselines under the same environment-interaction
budget. Ablation studies further support the effectiveness of the key algorithmic components, while
parameter sensitivity analyses provide additional evidence for the integrated design of EQUIP.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
• We propose EQUIP as a curvature-equilibration mechanism for gradient-based policy opti-
mization. It constructs a bounded local metric from the projected actor-loss Hessian within
a search subspace formed from recent actor gradients, enabling efficient use of positive and
negative curvature without computing or inverting the full Hessian.
• We develop a gradient adjustment mechanism combining first-order loss matching, gradient
mixing, and curvature-based selection. The resulting policy updates preserve the predicted
first-order decrease in actor loss, control gradient magnitude, and can achieve greater actor
loss reduction than raw-gradient updates under suitable conditions.
• We establish theoretical properties of EQUIP, including the geometric interpretation and
feasibility of curvature equilibration, gradient alignment and norm bounds, and conditions
for improved actor optimization. We further derive stationarity guarantees for direct actor
updates under fixed and changing actor losses, and relate actor-loss stationarity to the
entropy-regularized return under bounded approximation errors.

Related Work
We review related research in four areas: actor–critic policy optimization, policy geometry and
second-order methods, efficient curvature estimation and gradient subspaces, and geometry-aware
policy optimization approaches. We then summarize the key differences between EQUIP and existing
methods.
Actor–critic policy optimization.
Policy-gradient methods optimize policies using gradients of
the expected return (Sutton et al., 1999; Silver et al., 2014). Actor–critic methods employ learned
2


value functions to guide actor optimization. DDPG and TD3 optimize deterministic policies through
critic-based action gradients (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Fujimoto et al., 2018), while SAC learns stochastic
policies through an entropy-regularized actor loss (Haarnoja et al., 2018a).
Recent advances in off-policy reinforcement learning focus on improving value estimation, training
efficiency, and neural network architectures. REDQ combines critic ensembles with frequent updates
(Chen et al., 2021). CrossQ uses batch normalization to simplify critic learning without target
networks (Bhatt et al., 2024), while SimBa employs normalization and residual connections to
support larger networks (Lee et al., 2025).
Policy geometry and second-order optimization.
Natural policy gradient adjusts policy updates
using the inverse Fisher information matrix (Kakade, 2001). TRPO constrains policy updates
through KL divergence, while PPO uses a clipped probability-ratio objective to limit policy changes
(Schulman et al., 2015, 2017). ACKTR approximates natural-gradient updates using Kronecker-
factored curvature information (Wu et al., 2017). These methods primarily exploit the geometry of
policy distributions or constrain policy changes.
Another line of research uses Hessian information to construct second-order policy updates. Maniyar
et al. (2024) estimate policy gradients and Hessians from sampled trajectories and employ cubic-
regularized Newton updates to escape saddle points. Khorasani et al. (2025) propose VR-SCP, which
combines Hessian–vector products, variance reduction, and cubic-regularized optimization to improve
the sample efficiency of reaching second-order stationary points. Sun et al. (2025) further develop
a variance-reduced cubic-regularized Newton method based on Hessian estimation of the expected
return. Recent studies also investigate second-order optimization for actor–critic learning (Kordabad
et al., 2025; Manivannan and Venkataraman, 2026).
Among these methods, VR-SCP provides a recent second-order policy optimization approach with a
publicly available implementation. We therefore include it in our experimental comparisons.
Efficient curvature estimation and gradient subspaces.
Several optimization methods use cur-
vature estimates or structured gradient statistics to improve training efficiency. Sophia periodically
estimates diagonal curvature and clips its updates (Liu et al., 2024), while SOAP applies Adam
(Kingma and Ba, 2015) in a rotated coordinate system derived from Shampoo preconditioning
(Vyas et al., 2025). In deep reinforcement learning, Creus Castanyer et al. (2025) investigate gradi-
ent degradation in large networks and improve learning using residual connections and structured
preconditioning.
Low-dimensional curvature approximations offer another way to reduce computational costs.
SketchySGD uses randomized curvature estimates to construct preconditioners (Frangella et al.,
2024), while CAO periodically updates a low-rank Hessian approximation for damped inverse-
curvature scaling (Du, 2025). For policy optimization, Schneider et al. (2024) find that PPO and SAC
gradients often concentrate in low-dimensional subspaces spanned by leading Hessian eigenvectors,
which can remain similar across multiple policy updates.
Geometry-aware policy optimization approaches.
Sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) seeks
parameters with low loss under nearby parameter perturbations (Foret et al., 2021). In reinforce-
ment learning, Q-SAM extends sharpness-aware optimization to offline policy learning to improve
generalization of trained policies (Wang et al., 2026).
Another approach uses a local metric to control policy-search geometry. Specifically, Chen and
Huang (2023) propose a method that trains a dedicated metric tensor network to reduce the absolute
divergence of the policy-gradient field. They demonstrate its effectiveness with SAC and TD3.
Key distinctions and contributions of EQUIP.
Existing studies provide several ways to exploit
policy geometry and curvature information, including natural-gradient updates, Newton-type opti-
mization, curvature-based preconditioning, and sharpness-aware learning. Compared to these works,
EQUIP introduces a different approach that explicitly equilibrates positive and negative curvature
within a trajectory-informed search subspace.
Unlike Newton-type methods that use curvature to construct second-order update directions, EQUIP
directly constructs a bounded local metric from the projected actor-loss Hessian to adjust actor
gradients. Unlike metric-based approaches that train an additional network, metric is computed
3


directly in EQUIP from local curvature information. EQUIP further integrates first-order actor loss
matching, gradient mixing, and curvature-based gradient selection to preserve the predicted first-order
decrease in actor loss and control gradient adjustments. These components can be incorporated into
many existing actor–critic algorithms without modifying their policy optimization objectives or critic
learning procedures.
Furthermore, our theoretical analysis establishes the feasibility of curvature equilibration under
bounded metric weights and provides bounds on gradient alignment and magnitude. It also identifies
conditions under which mixed gradients achieve greater actor loss reduction than raw gradients. We
further derive convergence guarantees toward stationarity under direct actor updates for fixed actor
losses, along with conditional stationarity bounds for changing actor losses.

This provides the foundation for gradient-based policy optimization. In actor–critic methods, a
learned critic estimates action values or advantages to guide actor updates. Different algorithms
construct their actor losses differently. For example, DDPG and TD3 optimize deterministic policies

a clipped policy-gradient objective (Schulman et al., 2017). SAC combines critic-based policy

Despite these differences, gradient-based actor–critic methods share a common optimization process:
they update actor parameters using gradients of an actor loss. EQUIP builds on this process
by incorporating local curvature information to adjust the actor gradient without changing the
underlying actor loss or critic learning procedure. This makes EQUIP potentially applicable to
different actor–critic algorithms, provided that their actor losses support the required gradient and
curvature computations. In this study, we focus on SAC as a representative application.

EQUIP: Curvature-Equilibrated Gradients for Policy Search
4.1
Algorithm overview
We introduce EQUIP, (Curvature-Equilibrated Gradients for Policy Search), a general mechanism
for curvature-aware actor optimization. The central idea is to construct a bounded local metric
that equilibrates positive and negative curvature within a search subspace, followed by gradient
rescaling and mixing to approximately preserve the predicted first-order decrease in actor loss. This
curvature-equilibration approach is applicable to a broad range of gradient-based policy optimization
algorithms, with its integration into SAC detailed in Section 6.
For any base actor-critic algorithm such as SAC, let the current actor update be t. The base algorithm
provides a differentiable actor loss ℓt. Its raw gradient gt and Hessian Ht are

To make curvature calculations tractable for neural actors, EQUIP constructs a low-dimensional
policy search subspace from the current raw gradient gt as well as recent gradients utilized for actor
optimization (Subsection 4.2). Within this subspace, it constructs a bounded local metric to reduce
the imbalance between positive and negative curvature contributions (Subsection 4.3; Equation (12)).
The metric-adjusted gradient is then rescaled to match the approximated first-order decrease in actor
loss achieved by a direct raw-gradient update (Subsection 4.4; Equation (14)) and mixed with gt.
A gradient-selection mechanism (Subsection 4.5) chooses between the mixed and raw gradients,
yielding the final gradient ut as input to the actor optimizer O. We use Adam (Kingma and Ba,
2015) as O in our experiments, while the theoretical analysis in Section 5 considers the direct update
θt+1 = θt −ηtut for simplicity. Algorithm 1 summarizes the complete procedure. Subsection 4.6
further analyzes EQUIP’s computational and memory complexity.
4.2
Forming a search subspace from recent gradient directions
Forming the full p × p Hessian of the actor loss is computationally prohibitive. Instead, EQUIP
estimates curvature within a low-dimensional search subspace constructed from recent actor gradients.
At actor update t, we retain the gradient vectors used in the previous h completed actor updates and
combine them with the current raw gradient gt. Including gt ensures that the search subspace captures
the current gradient direction, which may not be adequately represented by historical gradients. We
normalize these vectors and apply Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization to remove linearly dependent
directions, followed by a reduced QR decomposition to improve numerical orthonormality. The
resulting rt orthonormal vectors qj (1 ≤j ≤rt) form the basis Qt = [q1, . . . , qrt] ∈Rp×rt of the
search subspace. If rt < rmin, the subspace is not sufficiently ranked, and the actor is updated using
the raw gradient gt.

requiring rt HVPs in total. All HVPs are evaluated using the same actor loss, with the same batch Bt,
actor noise ξt, and auxiliary quantities ζt held fixed. The HVPs and the resulting projected Hessian

Computational and memory complexity
EQUIP refreshes the search subspace and local metric every K actor updates, reusing them between
refreshes. The mixed gradient is still computed from the current raw gradient at each actor update.
Reusing the local geometry avoids repeated HVPs, although the cached curvature may become
outdated. Proposition 5.4 analyzes the effect of such changes, and detailed refresh procedures are
explained in Appendix B.3.
Let Cbase denote the cost of a standard actor update, including gradient computation and parameter
optimization, and let CHVP denote the cost of one HVP. For a search subspace of dimension

5
Theoretical Analysis
In this section, we theoretically analyze EQUIP. We first examine how the local metric in EQUIP
equilibrates positive and negative curvature and establish the conditions for achieving exact curvature
equilibration (Subsection 5.1). We then establish first-order actor loss matching and gradient norm
9


bounds (Subsection 5.2), followed by an analysis of the projected curvature comparison and its
implications for local actor loss (Subsection 5.3). Finally, we derive conditional stationarity bounds
for direct actor updates θt+1 = θt −ηut under fixed (Subsection 5.4) and changing actor losses
(Subsection 5.5), and relate these results to entropy-regularized policy optimization. Complete proofs
are provided in Appendix A.
In a nutshell, our theoretical results show that EQUIP can equilibrate positive and negative curvature
within the search subspace under bounded metric weights (Propositions 5.1 and 5.2). Through
first-order loss matching and gradient mixing, EQUIP can effectively reduce the actor loss, while
bounding the norm of the mixed gradient relative to the raw gradient (Theorem 5.3). Under suitable
conditions, the mixed gradient can also achieve a greater decrease in actor loss than the raw gradient
(Corollary 5.5). Furthermore, we establish convergence toward first-order stationarity for direct
actor updates under standard smoothness assumptions for fixed actor losses (Theorem 5.7), together
with conditional stationarity bounds for changing actor losses (Theorem 5.10). We finally show that
convergence toward stationarity of the expected actor loss implies approximate stationarity of the
entropy-regularized return, subject to suitable assumptions (Proposition 5.11).

Pusher (Todorov et al., 2012). SAC, EQUIP-SAC, and projected saddle-free Newton (PN) share the
same PyTorch SAC implementation, adapted from OpenAI Spinning Up (Achiam, 2018)3.
We also follow the default hyper-parameter settings provided by OpenAI Spinning Up. Specifically,
the actor and twin critics each use two 256-unit ReLU hidden layers, with a tanh-squashed Gaussian
policy for the actor. We use Adam with a learning rate of 10−3 for both actor and critic optimization,
a discount factor of γ = 0.99, a fixed entropy temperature of α = 0.2, and Polyak retention of
0.995. Actor and critic updates use minibatches of 100 transitions randomly sampled from the replay
buffer. Actor–critic training begins after 1,000 environment steps, with 50 gradient updates performed
after every 50 steps of environmental interactions. Furthermore, SAC, EQUIP-SAC, and PN takes
random actions during the first 10,000 environmental steps before switching to the learned policy for
subsequent environmental explorations.
EQUIP configuration.
While SAC passes the raw actor gradient to Adam, EQUIP-SAC adjusts
it using Algorithm 1. We follow a gradient mixing weight of λ = 0.3 and request an adjustment at
every actor update. Only adjustments satisfying the metric eligibility conditions in Subsection 4.3
(Equation (13)) and the gradient-selection criterion in Subsection 4.5 (Equation (16)) are accepted.
Otherwise, the raw gradient is used.
VR-SCP baseline and implementation.
We experimentally compare EQUIP-SAC with VR-SCP,
a recent second-order policy optimization method proposed by Khorasani et al. (2025). Unlike PN,
VR-SCP is an independent policy optimization algorithm rather than an adaptation of SAC. This
comparison allows us to evaluate EQUIP-SAC against a recent method that also exploits curvature
information to improve policy optimization and sample efficiency, but adopts a different design
strategy. We adapt the authors’ released implementation to our PyTorch/Gymnasium MuJoCo-v5
environment (Paszke et al., 2019; Towers et al., 2025) while preserving its optimization procedure
and key algorithmic components.
We use the hyperparameter settings in the authors’ released code4, as summarized in Table 1. All
training interactions are counted toward the same environment-step budget as EQUIP-SAC. We have

Evaluation settings.
We evaluate each learned policy every 4,000 environment interactions using
10 deterministic episodes. All results are reported over the first 300,000 environment interactions.
Our primary measure of sample efficiency is the area under the evaluation-return curve (AUC),
averaged over the 300,000-step training window. We also compare the evaluation returns achieved
by all competing algorithms at 300,000 environment steps.
Each algorithm uses 10 training seeds per benchmark task. For paired comparisons, we use matching
seed IDs. Evaluation returns are averaged across episodes within each training seed. We report the
mean performance together with 95% Student-t confidence intervals across training seeds. The ±
values in the experimental result tables represent confidence intervals, rather than standard deviations.
For learning curves, we apply a five-checkpoint moving average to each seed after truncating the data
to 300,000 steps. The curves show mean evaluation returns with point-wise 95% confidence intervals.
All reported AUC and final-return values are computed from raw performance data obtained through
our experiments.

7.5
Empirical use of curvature adjusted gradients
We investigate how frequently EQUIP-SAC accepts curvature-adjusted gradients during actor training
and how this relates to policy performance. According to Algorithm 2, EQUIP-SAC requests a
gradient adjustment at every actor update. An adjustment is accepted only when the metric satisfies
the curvature-equilibration conditions in Equation (13) and the mixed gradient passes the curvature-
based selection criterion in Equation (16), together with the additional numerical safeguards described
in Appendix B.2. Otherwise, the raw actor gradient is used. We define the acceptance fraction as the
number of accepted adjustments divided by the total number of requests.
Over 300k environment interactions, the acceptance fractions are 56.6% on HalfCheetah, 82.6% on
Walker2d, 94.2% on Swimmer, and 100.0% on Pusher. Interestingly, higher acceptance does not
necessarily lead to greater performance improvements. Pusher accepts nearly all adjusted gradients
but achieves only a 1.9% mean AUC improvement over SAC. In contrast, Walker2d achieves a much
larger improvement of 33.5% despite its lower acceptance fraction.
These findings suggest that the effectiveness of curvature-based gradient adjustments depends not
only on their acceptance frequency, but also on the magnitude and direction of the adjustments.
Both are influenced by the bounded spectral weights and gradient mixing. In particular, clipping
the spectral weights may prevent exact equilibration of positive and negative curvature. The metric
eligibility conditions in Equation (13) therefore require a sufficient reduction in curvature imbalance.
Consequently, accepting more adjusted gradients does not necessarily imply stronger curvature
equilibration or greater improvement in actor optimization.
7.6
Exploratory extensions to EQUIP
We also investigated several extensions to EQUIP to examine whether alternative gradient-adjustment
schedules and curvature estimation strategies could further improve actor optimization. These
experiments are detailed in Appendix C.
First, we investigate whether the frequency and timing of curvature-based gradient adjustments
affect policy optimization. Our experiment on HalfCheetah compares two settings. In the first,
curvature-based gradient adjustments are requested at every actor update. In the second, such
adjustments are requested only during several short, predefined periods of training. Our experiments
show that continuous requests can improve sample efficiency, but require substantially more gradient
adjustments and computational effort.
Second, we investigate whether combining different spectral weighting strategies could further
improve learning performance. We specifically develop a hybrid approach that uses EQUIP’s curvature-
equilibration weights when both positive and negative curvature are present, and PN’s inverse-
absolute-curvature weights when the projected Hessian is positive definite. Although this hybrid
design achieves higher mean AUC on Walker2d, it does not consistently outperform the standard
EQUIP across all tested benchmark tasks.
Finally, we examine whether reusing historical curvature information could improve the estimation of
the projected Hessian. Specifically, we investigate whether averaging projected Hessians computed
at different stages of actor training can improve curvature estimation. Since the search subspace
evolves during training, historical curvature estimates can only be reused when their subspaces are
sufficiently compatible with the current one. Our experiments show that this compatibility condition
is rarely satisfied. Hence, temporal averaging is seldom applicable and offers little opportunity to
improve learning performance.
Overall, these experiments suggest that performing curvature-based gradient adjustments throughout
training can improve sample efficiency. In contrast, hybrid spectral weighting and temporal averaging
23


of projected Hessians may not consistently provide additional performance gains. We therefore retain
the simpler EQUIP design in our main experiments.
7.7
Summary
Our empirical findings in this section indicate that curvature equilibration can improve the sample
efficiency of SAC actor training. Comparisons with PN as well as controlled component ablations
provide further evidence for the proposed design of EQUIP. Moreover, increasing the search subspace
rank, refreshing the geometry information more frequently, or averaging more curvature minibatches
does not consistently improve learning performance. These results support a relatively simple and
computationally efficient approach to curvature estimation for actor optimization.
In addition to all the reported experimental results, it is worthwhile to mention that the baseline
comparisons have some limitations. In particular, PN is an adaptation of saddle-free Newton
for reinforcement learning and may not fully reflect the potential of the original method, which
was evaluated on various other machine learning problems (Dauphin et al., 2014). Furthermore,
VR-SCP uses a different policy optimization framework and was originally tuned for MuJoCo-v2
benchmarks (Khorasani et al., 2025). Although these benchmarks are similar to the MuJoCo-v5 tasks
used in our experiments, we did not fine-tune VR-SCP’s hyper-parameters specifically for the v5
tasks. These limitations should be considered upon interpreting the experimental results.
8
Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced EQUIP, a novel curvature-based approach to actor optimization in
deep reinforcement learning. Unlike conventional second-order methods that primarily rescale
gradients according to curvature magnitude, EQUIP explicitly seeks to equilibrate positive and
negative curvature within a trajectory-informed search subspace. Its key innovations include a
bounded local metric for curvature equilibration, first-order loss matching to preserve the predicted
decrease in actor loss, and curvature-based gradient selection to determine when gradient adjustments
should be applied. Together, these components provide a principled and practical way to exploit local
curvature information without modifying the underlying policy optimization objective.
We integrated EQUIP into SAC and demonstrated its effectiveness on 6 MuJoCo-v5 benchmarks.
EQUIP-SAC consistently achieved higher mean AUC than SAC, PN, and VR-SCP across all 6
benchmark tasks under the same environment interaction budget. Further ablation and parameter
sensitivity analyses provided additional evidence for the proposed design and demonstrated that more
extensive curvature computation does not necessarily improve learning performance.
The significance of this work extends beyond the performance improvements achieved by EQUIP-
SAC. Our findings demonstrate that the complex geometry of the actor loss landscape provides
valuable information for improving policy learning. A promising direction for future research is to
make EQUIP more adaptive as actor training progresses. This could be realized by dynamically se-
lecting the search subspace, scheduling curvature updates, and controlling the strength and frequency
of gradient adjustments. Further research could also explore more efficient curvature estimation and
reuse strategies, as well as the integration of curvature equilibration techniques into other policy
optimization algorithms. Together, these directions offer exciting opportunities for advancing the
effectiveness, efficiency, and applicability of geometry-aware reinforcement learning.

Implementation Details
This appendix describes the numerical checks and geometry-refresh rules used to implement EQUIP
in SAC. EQUIP-SAC keeps the original SAC actor loss, critic updates, replay buffer, and Adam
optimizer. It changes only the gradient supplied to the actor optimizer, as defined in Equation (17).
The search subspace is constructed from recent gradients supplied to the actor optimizer, and the
projected Hessian is computed using the same actor loss and fixed actor noise as the current gradient,
with the critics held fixed. The implementation uses double precision for the small projected-Hessian
eigendecomposition, while the actor and its optimizer retain their usual precision.

B.3
Geometry refresh and gradient history
EQUIP-SAC requests a gradient adjustment at every actor update. The search basis, projected Hessian,
and local metric are computed at the first request and refreshed every K requests thereafter. At each
refresh, the search subspace is constructed from the current raw gradient and at most rmax −1 recent
gradients stored in a history buffer of capacity h. Numerically dependent directions are removed
during orthogonalization. HVPs are computed only if the resulting rank satisfies rt ≥rmin. If the
rank is too small, another refresh is attempted at the next actor update. Other invalid geometry results
leave the stored geometry unusable until the next scheduled refresh.
Between refreshes, the search basis Q, curvature eigenvectors U, eigenvalues µ, and metric weights b
remain fixed. However, the raw gradient, matching scale, mixed gradient, and selection checks are
recomputed at every actor update. Rejecting a mixed gradient does not trigger an extra refresh. After
a successful actor update, the history stores a detached copy of the gradient ut supplied to the actor
optimizer.

B.4
EQUIP-SAC implementation settings
We implement EQUIP-SAC in Python by extending the PyTorch implementation of Soft Actor–Critic
(SAC) from OpenAI Spinning Up (https://github.com/openai/spinningup). Our implemen-
tation retains the original SAC training framework, including the actor and critic networks, experience
replay, and Adam optimizers. The proposed EQUIP components are implemented separately and
integrated into actor training without modifying SAC’s objectives for critic and actor update. The
SAC and PN baselines use the same underlying SAC codebase.
Our experiments use Python 3.10.20 and PyTorch 2.13.0 for neural network training, automatic
differentiation, and HVPs. We use Gymnasium 1.3.0 to provide the MuJoCo-v5 continuous-control
environments. The software environment also includes NumPy 2.2.6 and SciPy 1.15.3. Neural
network computations use single-precision floating-point arithmetic, while the eigendecomposition
of the projected Hessian is performed in double precision to improve numerical stability.
Our experiments were conducted on CPU nodes of the university’s computing grid, with jobs managed
through the Sun Grid Engine. Python dependencies were managed using Micromamba. The grid
contains different CPU models, and the computing resources were not identical across all runs.
Consequently, we evaluate learning performance primarily in terms of environment interactions rather
than wall-clock training time.
Table 7 summarizes the common SACand EQUIP settings used in our experiments. We evaluate the
learned policies every 4,000 environment interactions using 10 deterministic evaluation episodes.
The maximum episode length is 1,000 steps, subject to the termination conditions of each benchmark
task. No additional observation or reward normalization is applied. Separate random seeds are used
for training environments, evaluation environments, and replay sampling.

300k environment interactions and are separate from the main experiments on the standard EQUIP-

C.2
Hybrid spectral weighting
We next test whether combining two spectral weighting rules improves actor optimization. Specif-
ically, the hybrid method uses the curvature-equilibration weights of EQUIP when the projected
Hessian has robust positive and negative curvature. When the projected Hessian is robustly positive
definite, it instead uses the inverse-absolute-curvature weights of PN (Equation (53)).
Both the hybrid and standard EQUIP-SAC use the same hyper-parameter settings, including rmax =
32, h = 32, K = 64, and λ = 0.3. As shown in Table 9, the hybrid method achieves a higher
mean AUC on Walker2d (1670.5 versus 1537.5). Ant also shows a positive mean difference, whereas
the other tasks do not. Overall, the hybrid method does not consistently improve on the standard
EQUIP-SAC. We therefore do not include it in the main experiment results in Section 7.2.
C.3
Temporal averaging of projected Hessians
Finally, we investigate whether curvature information from earlier stages of actor training can improve
the estimation of the current projected Hessian. Since the actor loss changes during training, averaging
projected Hessians computed at different stages may reduce estimation variance and provide more
reliable curvature information for actor optimization.
However, EQUIP constructs its search subspace from recent actor gradients, which evolve during
training. Consequently, projected Hessians computed at different stages may correspond to different
search subspaces and cannot be directly averaged. We therefore develop a basis-aligned temporal
averaging mechanism.

Experimental settings.
We evaluate temporal averaging using a maximum subspace rank of
rmax = 32, history capacity h = 32, refresh interval K = 64, and gradient mixing weight λ = 0.3.
Each geometry refresh uses ncurv = 4 curvature minibatches. We examine three temporal averaging
weights, β ∈{0.25, 0.50, 0.75}. For comparison, we use the same four-minibatch curvature estimator
without temporal averaging. All other settings remain unchanged. The experiments cover 6 MuJoCo
benchmarks using 10 training seeds per task, with performance evaluated over 300k environment
interactions.
Experimental results.
Compared with the four-minibatch estimator without temporal averaging,
the temporal variants improve mean AUC on Pusher by 0.079–0.373 across the three values of β.
However, no consistent improvements are observed on the other benchmark tasks. Overall, temporal
averaging does not consistently improve sample efficiency across the 6 benchmark tasks.
To understand these results, we examine how frequently historical curvature estimates satisfy the
conditions required for temporal averaging. During 300k environment interactions, none of the
geometry refreshes on HalfCheetah, Walker2d, or Ant satisfy these conditions. On Humanoid,
Swimmer, and Pusher, fewer than 0.2% of refreshes allow temporal averaging. Consequently, the
algorithm almost always relies on the current projected Hessian estimate.
These findings suggest that the trajectory-informed search subspaces change substantially during
actor training, making historical curvature information difficult to reuse. Given how rarely temporal
averaging is applied, these results do not demonstrate a clear benefit from reusing historical curvature
information.
