Abstract
Actor-critic methods achieve high returns in robotic con-
trol, yet trained actors exhibit high-frequency action os-
cillations.
Most smoothness methods constrain the ac-
tor, whereas PAVE regularizes the critic by separately pe-
nalizing action-gradient variation and encouraging nega-
tive action curvature.
The same gradient change, how-
ever, moves the critic’s preferred action farther where
the critic is flat, and separate penalties do not measure
this motion.
We characterize this motion through the
greedy shift, which approximates greedy-action displace-
ment across state transitions to first order under local con-
cavity. Since displacement alone does not reflect its critic-
value consequences, the analysis further shows that the
shift’s curvature-weighted energy approximates the value
lost by retaining the previous greedy action to second or-
der.
Based on this relationship, POISE regularizes the
curvature-weighted energy along temporal and spatial state
displacements. It employs a saddle-free curvature metric
and reward-scale-consistent scaling while leaving the ac-
tor unchanged. On six continuous control tasks with TD3,
POISE reduces the smoothness score on every task by 5.9×
on average (4.5× geometric mean), compared with 2.2×
(1.9×) for the strongest baseline, and achieves the lowest
score on five tasks. Paired return intervals include zero on
four tasks, with a 14% increase on Ant and an 11% decrease
on Hopper.

tee smooth control: trained actors exhibit high-frequency
action oscillations [5, 23], which accelerate mechanical

Most existing smoothness methods focus on the ac-
tor.
Some penalize action differences, or their changes,
across consecutive or neighboring states [17, 19, 23], others
align actions with predictions derived from preceding tran-
sitions [18], and architectural approaches constrain the sen-
sitivity of the policy network to its inputs [29–31]. These
methods limit variations in the actor’s output and do not
directly regularize the critic’s action-gradient field, which
continues to guide the actor during policy improvement.
A more direct approach is to regularize the critic, which
provides the learning signal that guides the actor. While ear-
lier critic regularization methods primarily targeted learning
stability and robustness, PAVE [20] established a connec-
tion between the critic’s differential geometry and the sensi-
tivity of its induced greedy policy. PAVE characterizes this
sensitivity through two geometric factors, the change in the
action gradient across states and the local action curvature

of the Q-function, and regularizes them separately. How-
ever, the two factors jointly determine greedy-action mo-
tion. The same gradient change induces a large shift along
a flat direction but a small shift along a sharply curved di-
rection, while a Euclidean penalty on the gradient change
assigns equal costs to both. Regularizing the two factors
separately therefore does not directly regularize their com-
bined effect on greedy-action motion.
We address this limitation by analyzing how the critic’s
preferred action moves across state transitions. From the
first-order optimality condition of the previous greedy ac-
tion, we derive the greedy shift, which combines the change
in the action gradient with the inverse action curvature. Un-
der local concavity and a sufficiently small transition along
the same branch of maximizers, the greedy shift approxi-
mates greedy-action displacement to first order. However,
action displacement alone does not account for its conse-
quences in the critic’s value landscape. We therefore ana-
lyze the critic-value loss incurred by retaining the previous
greedy action after a state transition, termed hold-regret. We
establish that this loss is approximated to second order by
half the curvature-weighted energy of the greedy shift. To-
gether, these results identify a single energy that captures
the combined effect of gradient variation and directional
curvature while assigning a local critic-value cost to greedy-
action motion.
In this work, we propose POISE (POlicy-Induced Shift
in the critic’s curvature mEtric), a critic-side regularization
method that penalizes the curvature-weighted energy de-
rived from our analysis. Since the theoretical energy as-
sumes local concavity, POISE employs a saddle-free cur-
vature metric to extend the formulation to replayed actions
where this assumption does not necessarily hold. With this
metric, the regularizer evaluates action-gradient changes
along observed state transitions and randomly reflected state
displacements, extending regularization around the data
without an additional perturbation-scale parameter. The re-
sulting energies are combined into a single penalty with
curvature-dependent scaling, matching the reward-scaling
behavior of the temporal-difference loss. POISE integrates
this single penalty with one weight into critic learning while
leaving the actor unchanged.
On six continuous control tasks under a unified training
protocol (Figure 1), POISE with TD3 reduces the smooth-
ness score on all tasks by 5.9× on average (4.5× geometric
mean), compared with 2.2× (1.9×) for PAVE, and achieves
the lowest score on five tasks. The paired return intervals
include zero on four tasks, with a 14% increase on Ant and
an 11% decrease on Hopper.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We analyze greedy-action motion across state transitions
and derive the greedy shift as its first-order approxima-
tion. We further establish a second-order relationship be-
tween its curvature-weighted energy and the local critic-
value loss of retaining the previous greedy action.
• We formulate POISE, a single critic-side regularizer that
jointly accounts for gradient variation and directional cur-
vature through a saddle-free metric and a reward-scale-
consistent formulation, without modifying the actor.
• We evaluate POISE on six continuous control tasks with
TD3 and SAC, demonstrating a 5.9× average reduction in
the TD3 smoothness score, the largest among the actor-
side and critic-side baselines. Critic diagnostics further
examine the theoretical relationships under their local ap-
plicability conditions.
2. Related Work
2.1. Actor-side smoothness regularization
Penalty-based methods augment the actor’s objective with
terms that limit how much its output changes. CAPS [23]
penalizes action differences between consecutive states
and between a state and Gaussian-perturbed neighbors.
L2C2 [17] narrows the spatial neighborhood using the dis-
placement between consecutive states and constrains the
value function in the same way, on its output rather than
its action gradient. Grad-CAPS [19] penalizes changes of
consecutive action differences, and ASAP [18] aligns each
action with the action predicted from the preceding state.
Other methods bound the Lipschitz constant of the pol-
icy network, through spectral normalization of a stability-
certified controller [31], a gradient penalty on the policy [4],
gradient normalization in LipsNet [29], or Jacobian regular-
ization with a Fourier filter layer in LipsNet++ [30], and ac-
tion chunking with temporal ensembling in imitation learn-
ing [34] smooths the executed action sequence. CATS [1]
shows that a temporal penalty on observed transitions also
bounds the expected action difference between states that
share a next state, and ramps the penalty up linearly during
training. Apart from the value constraint of L2C2, all of
these constrain the outputs or the architecture of the policy;
POISE instead regularizes the action-gradient field of the
critic.
2.2. Critic geometry and gradient regularization
Previous critic regularization methods primarily address
the stability and robustness of value learning rather than
the motion of the induced policy.
Gradient and Jaco-
bian penalties have been used to improve the generaliza-
tion [28] and the training stability [25] of learned func-
tions, while spectral normalization controls network sen-
sitivity, applied to the value network [11] or to both actor
and critic [2]. SR2L [26] also introduces critic-side reg-
ularization for policy smoothness, but constrains changes
in Q-values under bounded state perturbations rather than
the critic’s action-gradient geometry. PAVE [20] explicitly
2


connects the critic’s differential geometry to greedy-policy
sensitivity and regularizes the action-gradient field and ac-
tion curvature with separate penalties, Euclidean terms on
the gradient field and a hinge with a fixed margin on the
curvature. However, these penalties do not directly account
for how the two factors jointly determine greedy-action mo-
tion. POISE instead regularizes their combined effect, the
greedy shift, in the critic’s curvature metric.
2.3. Second-order structure in continuous control
Second-order information usually enters RL as an opti-
mizer: natural-gradient methods precondition the actor’s
parameter gradient with the Fisher information [16], and
saddle-free Newton methods replace the Hessian by its ab-
solute value to escape saddle points [6].
Value-gradient
learning [9] trains the gradient of the value function directly,
since a greedy policy depends on the value only through its
gradient, and quadratic critics [12] make the greedy action
available in closed form. POISE uses the action Hessian in
neither role: it is neither an optimizer nor a parameteriza-
tion, but the metric in which the motion of the greedy action
is measured and penalized, and its parameters are trained
with the same first-order optimizer as every baseline.

4. How Critic Geometry Moves the Greedy Ac-
tion
The local sensitivity bound does not directly quantify the
displacement of the critic’s preferred action along a particu-
lar state transition. This motivates a transition-level analysis
of greedy-action motion and its associated local critic-value
cost.

6. Experiments
6.1. Experimental setup

benchmark comprised the six Gymnasium [33] and Mu-

were TD3 [10] and SAC [13] on Stable-Baselines3 [24],
and all methods used SiLU activations [8]. The baselines

6.2. Main results
Table 1 evaluated whether POISE reduced the smoothness
score while retaining the return of the unregularized Base,
in comparison with actor-side and critic-side regularization.
For TD3, actor-side regularization yielded limited and in-
consistent smoothing: the mean reduction of the Base score
remained below 2× for all three methods, and CAPS and
ASAP increased the score on one and two tasks, respec-
tively. Critic-side regularization was more effective, and the
proposed method improved substantially on PAVE, with a
mean reduction of 5.9× against 2.2× and the lowest score
on five of the six tasks. The only exception was Reacher,
where the Base policy was already smooth and Grad-CAPS
attained 0.040 against 0.044 for our method. Hopper was
the only task marked in red for POISE: the mean return de-
creased by 11% on Hopper and increased by 14% on Ant.
Apart from Grad-CAPS on Reacher, every baseline that re-
turned more than POISE was less smooth: on Walker, all
four baselines returned more, with smoothness scores 3.8 to
5.2 times that of POISE.
In SAC, the Base policy was smoother than under TD3
on five tasks, and the reductions achieved by CAPS, Grad-
CAPS, PAVE and POISE were smaller. Both observations
are consistent with the objectives of the two actors: the TD3
actor ascends a single critic and tracks the greedy action
that POISE regularizes, whereas the SAC actor is trained
toward a distribution proportional to exp(Q/α) for the min-
imum Q of two critics and the entropy temperature α. The
mean of this target distribution varies continuously where
the greedy action jumps between ridges. The relative or-
der of the two families changed: the actor-side ASAP at-
tained a larger mean reduction than the critic-side PAVE,
1.4× against 1.3×, whereas the proposed method retained
the largest reduction, 2.3×, and the lowest score on Pen-
dulum, Hopper, Walker and Ant. On Lunar and Reacher,
where the SAC Base was already smooth, ASAP attained
the lowest scores, 0.188 and 0.043, the second tied with
Grad-CAPS, against 0.221 and 0.047 for POISE. The mean
return of our method was 15% below Base on Hopper, 6.5%
below on Walker and 6.4% below on Ant, and the only de-
crease marked in red was on Pendulum, from −149.3 to
−151.7. Every baseline that returned more than POISE was
again less smooth: on Hopper, all four baselines returned
more, with scores 2.6 to 3.3 times that of POISE.

6.5. Critic geometry
Figure 3 compared the critics that the methods produced.
At each step of a test trajectory, we evaluated Q(st, ·) along
the principal direction of the executed actions and marked
its maximum, the greedy action on this slice, together with
the executed action.
The Base critic had several ridges,
its greedy action jumped between them from one step to
the next, and the actor-side methods left this landscape
nearly unchanged. PAVE reduced the per-step motion of the
greedy action on Hopper from 0.48 to 0.21, and the POISE
critic kept a single ridge on this slice with a motion of 0.13,
3.7 times below Base on Hopper and 4.7 times on Walker.
The executed action followed the greedy action on this sin-
gle ridge: their correlation was 0.97 on Hopper and 0.99 on
Walker for our method, against 0.62 and 0.87 for Base.
6.6. Robustness analysis
The score of POISE fell as the weight grew on all six tasks
(Figure 4). At the shared weight with rewards scaled by 0.1
and 10, the score of POISE stayed within 0.18 to 0.27 on
Lunar, whereas that of PAVE rose from 0.28 to 1.37 (Fig-
ure 5a); the Lunar return of POISE was 183, 226 and 240
at the scales 0.1, 1 and 10. Under observation noise absent
from training, POISE kept the lowest score at every noise
level on Hopper and Walker (Figure 6a, b). With each ac-
tion held for four steps, every baseline lost 81% to 100% of
its return on both tasks, against 42% and 34% for POISE
(Figure 6c). Under a one-step observation delay, the re-
turn of POISE changed by +2% on Hopper and −24% on
Walker, against losses of 31% to 57% and 11% to 43% for
the baselines.
7. Conclusion
We analyzed how the geometry of the critic moves its
greedy action across a state transition and what critic value
this motion costs. The greedy shift measures this motion to
first order, and its curvature-weighted energy measures the
value lost by holding the previous greedy action. POISE pe-
nalizes this energy on the critic with a saddle-free curvature
8


metric and leaves the actor unchanged. On six continuous
control tasks, POISE reduced the TD3 smoothness score
by 5.9× on average, against 2.2× for PAVE, and a single
shared weight kept the lowest score among the regularized
methods on all six tasks.
Limitations.
This work evaluated POISE in simulation
on state-based tasks with at most eight action dimensions.
Future work will address hardware deployment, image ob-
servations, on-policy methods, and curvature approxima-
tions for larger action spaces.

A.2. Hyperparameters and weight selection
Table S1 lists the method-specific hyperparameters and Table S2 the measured time per training update. Baseline values are
the published per-task values of PAVE [20] and were not re-tuned. With SAC, CAPS and Grad-CAPS kept the listed values,
and ASAP and PAVE used the published SAC values. Every method was trained for 105 steps on Pendulum, 5 × 105 on
Lunar and Reacher and 106 on Hopper, Walker and Ant. The weight of POISE was set in two stages. First, validation runs
gave the shared rule λ = ˜λ/d with ˜λ = 0.1. Their five training seeds and their reset seeds were both disjoint from those of
Table 1, and Reacher had no validation runs. The runs behind this rule used λ ∈{0.01, 0.05} and also 0.2 on Pendulum. The
rule approximates the weights that these runs preferred, 0.05 at d = 2 and 0.01 at d = 6. Second, the weight in Table 1 was
chosen per task among POISE runs on the five training seeds and the test reset seeds of Table 1, as PAVE chose its weights.
The candidates were the shared weight and one to five further weights per task with TD3, and the shared weight and one to
three further weights with SAC. The choice took a low mean sm among weights whose paired return difference to Base had
a 95% bootstrap interval not entirely below zero. No weight qualified on TD3 Hopper and SAC Pendulum, where the weight
of highest return was taken. The per-task weights were therefore selected on the reported seeds. Section F reports the shared
weight without this selection, and Figure S1 shows every trained weight.

gradients, built the Hessian with d backward passes, and performed two eigendecompositions and two linear solves per
sample. One update of POISE therefore costs O(dC + Nd3) time and O(dM + Nd2) memory for each regularized critic
