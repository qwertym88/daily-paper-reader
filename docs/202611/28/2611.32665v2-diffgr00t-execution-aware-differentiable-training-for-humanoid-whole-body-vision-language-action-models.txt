I. Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models have made remark-
able progress in arm-centric manipulation, where predicted
end-effector poses or joint-space targets can be executed
through low-level servo controllers [1], [2]. Extending these
capabilities to humanoid robots, however, introduces fun-
damentally different control challenges. Unlike fixed-base
manipulators, humanoids exhibit underactuated floating-
base dynamics in which locomotion, manipulation, and
posture regulation must be coordinated under changing
contact and balance constraints. Directly predicting joint
targets does not guarantee stable whole-body execution,
while learning high-frequency stabilization directly inside a
large VLA remains difficult. Consequently, many humanoid
systems adopt a hierarchical architecture in which a VLA
generates high-level motion intentions and a pretrained
arXiv:2611.32665v2  [cs.RO]  25 Nov 2026


behavior foundation model (BFM) or whole-body controller
realizes them through proprioceptive feedback.
This hierarchical paradigm has driven substantial
progress in humanoid loco-manipulation. SONIC estab-
lishes a generalizable whole-body motion foundation
through a shared latent interface, while systems such
as WholeBodyVLA and OpenHLM pair task-conditioned
action generation with pretrained motion controllers [3]–[5].
Because these architectures train the VLA to emit motion
references and the controller to track them independently,
the controller provides reactive whole-body stabilization at
deployment, yet an action–execution training gap remains:
tracking and balance errors have no direct gradient path
back into the VLA’s motion-generation objective. Closing
this gap via end-to-end differentiable training requires
establishing a differentiable gradient pathway across the
policy–controller interface.
Bridging this gap is non-trivial because robust whole-
body control and differentiable optimization have tradi-
tionally followed separate training paradigms. Humanoid
motion trackers are commonly trained with model-free
reinforcement learning, where physical execution feedback
arrives as scalar rewards rather than differentiable state
transitions. Moreover, contact-rich humanoid dynamics
involve non-smooth support switches, making direct dif-
ferentiation through conventional rigid-body simulators
numerically brittle and computationally expensive. Al-
though differentiable physics methods such as DiffMimic
have explored analytical motion-imitation gradients [6],
scaling such optimization to high-dimensional whole-body
control and large VLA generators remains computationally
prohibitive.
An alternative to differentiating through the simulator
is to differentiate through a learned world model. Fitted
to state transitions collected from the simulator or the
robot, an action-conditioned neural world model is differ-
entiable everywhere—including across contact transitions—
allowing execution objectives to be backpropagated through
predicted states without invoking a contact solver, while
short rollout windows bound compounding prediction error.
SuperMimic [7] demonstrates this paradigm for humanoid
tracking by training a tracking policy with short-horizon
backpropagation through time (BPTT) on a learned world
model, and in a velocity-tracking experiment further routes
gradients through a shared decoder into a lightweight goal
encoder. Extending this execution-gradient pathway across
a frozen token-conditioned controller and a multi-step flow-
matching sampler into a humanoid VLA, however, remains
largely unexplored.
We present DiffGR00T, an end-to-end differentiable
training framework that couples GR00T N1.7 with the
pretrained SONIC behavior foundation model. We first
fine-tune the GR00T N1.7 action head on HumanoidArena
demonstrations carrying recorded SONIC motion tokens [8],
[9] to align the VLA with SONIC’s latent motion interface.
During execution-aware differentiable training, a learned
action-conditioned world model supplies multi-step state
transitions to evaluate a body-execution loss, allowing
execution gradients to propagate through the world model,
the frozen SONIC decoder, and the flow-matching sampler
directly into the VLA action head.
Our mechanism exploits the temporal structure of the
VLA’s action chunks. The flow-matching VLA generates
a 40-step motion-token sequence, of which the first 20
tokens are executed in closed loop. At each 50 Hz control
step, the frozen SONIC decoder maps the current token
and proprioceptive history to 29 joint targets, and the
world model predicts the resulting robot state, which is
fed back as proprioception for the next step. Multi-step
body-execution losses are then backpropagated through a
ten-step rollout window, the frozen decoder, and the four-
step flow-matching sampler to update only the VLA action
head (via LoRA adapters and the output projection), while
the world model and the training-only critic remain fixed
throughout. On four HumanoidArena simulation tasks,
DiffGR00T raises average task success by 12.5 percentage
points over the supervised base VLA (59.2% →71.7%).
Our main contributions are as follows:
1) A differentiable world model for whole-body
execution. We learn an action-conditioned dynam-
ics model for a 29-DoF humanoid from controller-
excitation replay and a small amount of closed-loop
policy data, holding its parameters fixed during
execution-aware differentiable training to serve as
a differentiable surrogate for the simulator over short
rollouts.
2) Execution gradients into a flow-matching VLA.
We backpropagate multi-step body-execution losses
through the learned world model, the frozen SONIC
decoder, and the four-step flow-matching sampler
to update the VLA action head, while keeping the
vision-language backbone, controller, world model,
and training-only critic fixed.
3) Execution-aligned supervision. We identify the
key supervision terms required for effective humanoid
execution feedback—root position and drift to curb
per-window drift, transition-frame weighting for task-
phase changes, and speed-coverage window sam-
pling to retain locomotion in the gradient budget—
calibrated to maintain scale consistency across objec-
tives.
II. Related Work
A. Flow-Matching Policies and Policy Improvement
π0 combines a pretrained vision-language backbone with
a flow-matching action expert to generate continuous
action chunks [1]. Building on this paradigm, recent work
explores alternative gradient pathways for supervised policy
learning: DIAL links the VLM and a reactive action
expert through predicted visual intent, backpropagating
the supervised flow-matching loss through this latent


intent into trainable VLM components during joint fine-
tuning [2]; VITA, a visuomotor policy mapping image
encodings to latent actions, differentiates a supervised
action-reconstruction loss directly through the Euler steps
of the flow-matching ODE solver [10].
A complementary line of research incorporates robot
execution experience into flow-matching policy updates.
RECAP fits a value function to execution rollouts and
conditions supervised VLA updates on value-derived ad-
vantage indicators [11], whereas Q-VGM learns an action-
chunk critic from rollout rewards and converts critic
gradients with respect to denoised action chunks into local
velocity targets, avoiding backpropagation through the full
denoising trajectory [12]. While these methods leverage
ODE differentiation, advantage conditioning, or value
guidance, none propagates a multi-step body-execution
objective through a whole-body controller and learned
action-conditioned dynamics into the flow-matching gener-
ator.
B. Humanoid Whole-Body Action Generation and Control
Humanoid loco-manipulation systems span diverse inter-
faces between high-level action generation and low-level
control. SONIC learns a generalizable whole-body motion
tracker from motion-capture data and demonstrates VLA-
driven loco-manipulation with GR00T N1.5 through a
shared motion-token interface [3]. HumanoidArena bench-
marks hierarchical policy–tracker pairings and documents
a direct prediction mode in which a VLA outputs 64-
dimensional SONIC body latents [9], providing the interface
and benchmark setting adopted in this work. Other hierar-
chical frameworks rely on different intermediate representa-
tions: WholeBodyVLA learns locomotion and manipulation
latents from action-free egocentric video and decodes
them into upper-body joint targets alongside locomotion
commands executed by a reinforcement-learning controller
[4], while OpenHLM combines joint-space teleoperation
with heterogeneous data co-training to generate whole-
body joint references tracked by an independent low-level
policy [5].
Beyond single-stage reference generation, HAF-VLA
produces cumulative whole-body commands via stage-wise
flow matching and cross-stage conditioning, with existing
reinforcement-learning controllers executing locomotion
and waist commands [13]. Departing from explicit reference
tracking, SENTINEL clones a simulator-trained whole-
body controller into a language-and-proprioception policy
that predicts low-level action targets directly, then freezes
that policy and trains a residual action head with PPO
[14]. Across these interfaces—shared motion tokens, joint-
space references, staged commands, and direct low-level
targets—execution discrepancies are absorbed by down-
stream trackers or a separate residual head rather than
backpropagated through the controller to update the high-
level VLA generator.
C. Differentiable Whole-Body Control and Execution-Aware
Learning
Propagating physical execution gradients directly into
policy learning has progressed from differentiable physics
to learned dynamics surrogates. DiffMimic trains a hu-
manoid motion-tracking policy by differentiating imitation
losses through a differentiable rigid-body simulator [6].
To avoid differentiating through contact solvers, Con-
trolVAE optimizes task policies for simulated characters
by backpropagating task losses through a frozen latent
skill controller and a learned world model [15]. MoConVQ
similarly uses a learned world model to train discrete
motion representations and their decoder, while training its
text-conditioned generator separately via token prediction
[16].
Closest to our humanoid setting are SuperMimic and
HWAM. SuperMimic fits a differentiable world model
from simulator transitions and trains a humanoid tracking
policy via short-horizon imagined rollouts; in a downstream
velocity-tracking experiment, execution gradients also pass
through the world model and the shared decoder (with
decoder weights frozen on that branch) into a velocity
goal encoder [7]. HWAM incorporates physical execution
by jointly modeling kinematic references and aligned
measured post-execution states, coupled with auxiliary
objectives that predict future video from joint trajectories
and reconstruct joint trajectories from visual transitions
[17].
In contrast, DiffGR00T performs execution-aware differ-
entiable training on a flow-matching humanoid VLA: multi-
step body-execution losses are backpropagated through a
short rollout of learned action-conditioned dynamics, the
frozen SONIC decoder, and the four-step flow-matching
sampler to update only the VLA action head (via LoRA
adapters and the output projection), leaving both the VLM
backbone and the controller fixed. We evaluate this pathway
along three axes: gradient connectivity into the flow head,
directional agreement with simulator returns, and closed-
loop task performance in simulation.
III. Method
A. Overall Architecture and Control Interface
As illustrated in Figure 2, the framework consists of three
coupled components: a flow-matching VLA, a frozen whole-
body foundation model SONIC, and an action-conditioned
differentiable world model. The VLA maps egocentric
observations, a language instruction, and robot state to a
chunk of motion tokens; the BFM decodes each token,
together with a proprioceptive history, into joint-level
commands; and the world model predicts how the robot
state evolves under those commands.
The architecture is shared by all tasks and has two
distinct execution modes. At deployment, only the VLA,
the frozen SONIC decoder, and the hand interface run: the
VLA produces an action chunk, SONIC decodes its body

B. Differentiable World Model
In training the simulator is replaced by an action-
conditioned world model Fϕ that predicts the transition
caused by a body action. The model predicts a one-
step state increment rather than an absolute next state:
an absolute target is dominated by the robot’s global
pose, while the transition over a 20 ms step is small and
approximately stationary. The network therefore outputs
dt, and the next state is recovered by integration. Let I
denote the integration operator that maps a state and a
predicted increment to the next state. Then
dt = Fϕ(st, at, ξ) ∈R70,

where st ∈R71 denotes the root pose (position and a unit
quaternion), the 29 joint positions, the root linear and
angular velocities, and the 29 joint velocities. The action
at ∈R29 is the normalized body action produced by the
BFM, and ξ ∈R33 is a simulation-context vector (friction,
encoder bias, and center-of-mass offset) held at nominal
values at evaluation. The state is 71-dimensional and the
increment is 70-dimensional because orientation is stored
as a quaternion in st but predicted as a 3-D rotation vector

Integration proceeds in two stages. First, the velocity
increments produce the next root linear velocity, root
angular velocity, and joint velocities. Second, these updated
velocities advance the root position, root orientation, and
joint angles over the 20 ms step. The predicted residual
increments ∆p, ∆θ, ∆q are added as local corrections on
top of this velocity-based update. For orientation, the 3-D
rotation vector is converted into a rotation through an
exponential map and composed with the current orienta-
tion. Root-related increments are expressed in the current
heading frame, so the target does not depend on the robot’s
global position or yaw. The increment is therefore small
and frame-local, while the full next state is recovered by
integration.

C. Execution-Aware Differentiable Training
Differentiable training preserves the computation graph
from the chunk through the BFM, the world model, and the
sampler, so that an objective defined on imagined execution
reaches the generator. Everything except the VLA’s action
head is held fixed during the update: the BFM, the SONIC
encoder, the world model, and the training-only critic keep
their parameters. The critic supplies a fixed tail value, while
the policy is the only module that changes the deployed
controller.
The execution objective is defined on a 0.4 s segment:
at 50 Hz the policy executes a 20-step block, and the
objective is evaluated on its 10-step differentiable prefix.
The remaining 10 steps are not differentiated step by step;
instead, a fixed, training-only critic estimates their tail
return from the imagined state at step 10. This value enters
the loss and remains differentiable with respect to the
imagined state. The critic is held fixed after initialization;
it is not updated during policy training. The reference
is a local segment reference used only during training to
define the drift target. At deployment the VLA receives
neither the reference trajectory nor the critic. The policy
therefore learns to reduce drift within each segment, rather
than fitting a complete closed-loop trajectory to a global
reference.
Policy starts are drawn only from A′ segments. Reference-
library frames are not used since they are idealized reference
states rather than states the robot actually visited, so
they have zero execution drift and do not cover the off-
trajectory or recovery states needed for correction. Closed-
loop VLA trajectories are also excluded because they do not
provide an aligned local reference segment for each visited
frame, which means the drift target cannot be formed. In
addition, their drift accumulates over a full episode and
cannot be corrected within a 0.4 s segment. Closed-loop
data is therefore used to train the world model rather than
as policy starts.
A frame from A′ is eligible as a policy start if it was
visited, its root offset from the segment reference is at


most 0.10 m, at least 10 frames of history exist, and the
20-step reference window stays inside the clip. We use
only training clips, sample eligible frames with the training
coverage weights, and read only frames and states, not
actions. The 0.10 m bound is a trust region because a 10-
step window can correct only a few centimeters; a larger
offset would act as a nearly constant error. Frames outside
the bound still train the world model, but they are not
policy starts.

IV. Experiments
A. Experimental Setup
Platforms and tasks. We evaluate on HumanoidArena
in Isaac Lab simulation with a Unitree G1, using 29
body degrees of freedom and 14 finger joints at a 50 Hz
body control rate, 40-step VLA chunks and a 20-step
replanning horizon. The simulation task set contains four
HumanoidArena tasks: OpenDoor, SitSofa, Boxing, and
VisionNavi. The main comparison uses these four tasks
with a complete baseline grid; all quantitative closed-loop
numbers in this paper come from the simulator.
Data and splits. Training data are 400 recorded
HumanoidArena episodes carrying SONIC motion tokens
(100 per task). Scene instances and instructions are sepa-
rated before temporal windowing, so held-out scenes and
paraphrases never appear in training. The VLA is fine-
tuned on these episodes. The world model uses the four-
source mix of Section III-B (clean A′, token-perturbed A′,
closed-loop pairs, and branch groups), is trained once, and
is held fixed during execution-aware differentiable training;
future states are used only to construct its regression
targets and are unavailable at deployment.
Metrics and protocol. All success comparisons use
the same protocol: four tasks, seeds 0–2, and repeats 0–
19, giving 60 episodes per task and 240 per arm, with
matched episode seeds across arms. Task success is the
primary metric. A cell is successful only if its executed
episode succeeded. Failed or timed-out episodes remain in
every denominator. Fall is an episode terminated by a fall.
Paired cells are tested with McNemar’s test. Two arms are
compared: the task fine-tuned VLA with a frozen SONIC
decoder (Base), and the same model with LoRA adapters
trained by execution-aware differentiable training (Full);
they share checkpoints, seeds, and episodes. The Full arm

C. Surrogate and Gradient Analysis
Connectivity is necessary. The execution objective alone
produces finite, non-zero gradients on the flow head, all
four sampler steps receive gradient, and the world model’s
input Jacobian remains active while its parameters are
frozen.
We use the surrogate as a direction, not as a calibrated
return. Two checks support this. First, its ranking of
counterfactual action sequences over 405 groups is well
above chance (70.4% four-way, τ = 0.67 at H = 10, chance
25%). Second, in a ±δ probe on 87 held-out windows
(H = 10, δ = 0.03), moving along the world-model
gradient improves the true return by +0.014 on average,
and the predicted and true return changes correlate at 0.689.
Checkpoints are selected on held-out validation windows
using the differentiable execution return. Among sampling-
gradient estimators, the pathwise one keeps token error at
0.115, the discrete adjoint matches it in return but not in
fidelity, and truncation is biased and raises token error to
0.174.

V. Limitations
Our quantitative evidence is confined to four simulated
tasks. The real-robot runs are qualitative closed-loop
inference after real-robot data collection and adaptation,
not a real-robot success-rate benchmark.
The learned world model is a coarse action-conditioned
simulator. It rolls out the execution block and supplies
differentiable execution gradients, but it is not accurate
enough to replace the simulator for evaluation or to provide
a calibrated return. On a held-out policy-transition probe,
its H=10 error is about 3–4 cm in root position and 7–8◦in
rotation on the four-task probe. This supports coarse credit
assignment over the execution window, not accurate long-
horizon prediction. The CL-root column of the data-source
table is a different measurement on held-out closed-loop
windows, not the same probe.
The differentiable update is also not a fully on-policy
loop: the world model is trained from a fixed data mix, so
the policy cannot track its own evolving state distribution.
This bounds the achievable gain; longer differentiable
training alone is unlikely to close the remaining gap.
The execution objective covers the 29 body joints. The
14 hand channels are initialized from the 8000-step task
fine-tune and are not directly optimized by the execution
objective; we do not evaluate dexterous hand manipulation.
Object-centric contact is outside the current state-only
world model, which does not explicitly model object states

data side, improving the coverage and balance of the
existing transition sources could better represent recovery
and contact states. Better calibration and periodic refresh
against the current policy distribution would further reduce
surrogate error. A more accurate model would make longer
differentiable horizons and larger trust regions safer, so
the execution gradient would rely less on surrogate error.
Extending the same treatment to the 14 hand joints is a
separate direction.
VI. Conclusion
We presented an execution-aware differentiable training
framework that couples a flow-matching humanoid VLA
with a frozen whole-body controller through a learned
action-conditioned world model. The key idea is to back-
propagate execution costs through the controller and the
four-step sampler into the action head, so that the policy is
optimized for the body it actually controls rather than for
tokens alone. On four HumanoidArena tasks, the H = 10
recipe raises average success from 59.2% to 71.7% and
lowers the cost per successful episode from 3102 to 2490
steps, with the largest gain on SitSofa; the architecture is
shared, and a gated root-motion anti-stall term is enough
to change contact behavior without changing the model.
We also close the inference loop on a physical Unitree G1
after real-robot data adaptation, qualitatively. The main
limits remain the coarse surrogate, the fixed data mix, and
the body-only gradient; an object-aware world model, on-
policy refresh, and held-out or robustness evaluation are
left for future work.
