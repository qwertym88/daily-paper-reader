Abstract
In many applications, neural networks must learn input–output maps that respect prior knowledge
such as physical laws, safety requirements, and operating limits. These requirements constrain
which outputs are admissible for a given input and often admit second-order cone (SOC) represen-
tations, including norm bounds and robust linear inequalities. Examples in control include friction
cones, thrust bounds, and input-robust control barrier function (CBF) conditions. However, exist-
ing approaches to enforcing general input-dependent SOC constraints require a conic solver, an
iterative scheme whose feasibility depends on convergence, or an interior feasible point for each
input. We propose SOC-Net, a neural network architecture that enforces input-dependent SOC con-
straints by construction for every input, even outside the training distribution. SOC-Net projects an
affine lifting of the backbone output onto the reachable slice of a fixed product of Lorentz cones
and maps the correction back to the output space. For separable constraints, this projection splits
into one projection per cone, and each is available in closed form or reduces to a single scalar
equation, regardless of the cone dimension. We prove that SOC-Net retains the expressive power
of the backbone network under regularity conditions and accommodates end-to-end training by
standard backpropagation with an explicit Jacobian. Experiments on contact force allocation with
friction constraints and 3-D obstacle avoidance with an input-robust CBF demonstrate that SOC-
Net satisfies the constraints at every evaluated input, with lower evaluation latency than the tested
solver-based and iterative baselines.
Keywords: Hard-constrained neural networks, HardNets, second-order cone constraints, learning
to optimize, learning-based control, constrained control
1. Introduction
As neural networks proliferate across scientific and engineering applications, they are increasingly
required to learn input–output maps that respect prior knowledge such as physical laws, safety re-
quirements, and operating limits. This knowledge restricts which outputs are admissible for a given
input. In control, such networks now serve as dynamics models (Shi et al., 2019), feedback policies
(Levine et al., 2016), and optimization-based controllers (Chen et al., 2018) of physical systems.
Because their outputs affect a physical system, the knowledge must also hold at deployment, where
a single inadmissible output can be unsafe.
Many of these requirements and limits can be written as second-order cone (SOC) constraints
∥Ay + b∥2 ≤c⊤y + d on the output y. SOC constraints express norm bounds and robust linear
inequalities (Lobo et al., 1998). Friction cones in contact force allocation (Boyd and Wegbreit, 2007)

and thrust and pointing limits in powered-descent guidance (Acikmese and Ploen, 2007) are SOC
constraints. So are robust control barrier function (CBF) conditions, whose safety margin grows
with the model uncertainty (Dean et al., 2021; Cosner et al., 2021; Taylor et al., 2021). In learning
problems the coefficients A, b, c, d usually depend on the input x, so the feasible set changes from
one evaluation to the next.
A common way to impose such constraints is to penalize their violation in the training loss.
Examples include fixed penalties, physics-informed residuals, augmented-Lagrangian terms, and
primal–dual constrained learning (Raissi et al., 2019; Dener et al., 2020; Chamon and Ribeiro,
2020; Fioretto et al., 2020). These methods reduce violations on the training distribution but do not
guarantee that every output is feasible. Violations can remain on unseen inputs or before training
has converged.
Hard-constrained neural networks (HardNets) instead guarantee feasibility for every input by
construction. HardNet-Cvx (Min and Azizan, 2025) enforces input-dependent convex constraints
by projecting network outputs onto the feasible set using differentiable optimization layers (Amos
and Kolter, 2017; Agrawal et al., 2019). Other hard-constrained architectures avoid a general-
purpose solver by mapping the backbone output to a feasible point explicitly. For instance, HardNet-
Aff (Min and Azizan, 2025) does so in closed form through metric projection but handles only
affine constraints. ECO (Goertzen et al., 2026b) develops a closed-form projection layer for convex
quadratic constraints. RAYEN supports SOC and LMI constraints through rays from an interior
feasible point (Tordesillas et al., 2023). When the constraint depends on the input, this point must be
supplied for every input. To avoid that, LMI-Net and ΠNet rely on iterative splitting or projection
(Tang et al., 2026; Grontas et al., 2026). Their outputs are feasible only upon convergence, so
truncated iterations may return infeasible outputs. HardNet++ (Goertzen et al., 2026a) addresses
nonlinear equality and inequality constraints through differentiable, damped local linearizations.
For general input-dependent SOC constraints, existing methods therefore rely on a conic solver, an
iterative scheme, or an interior point computed for each input.
We propose SOC-Net, a neural network architecture that enforces hard, input-dependent SOC
constraints on its output. SOC-Net rewrites the constraints as M(x)y + h(x) ∈K for a fixed
product K of Lorentz cones and projects the lifted backbone output onto the part of K reachable by
some output (Fig. 1). When the constraints are separable, as for friction cones at distinct contacts,
this projection splits into one projection per cone. When M(x) is surjective, this is the classical
Lorentz-cone projection. When it is not, we show that projecting onto the reachable slice reduces
to two coordinates and one monotone multiplier equation. This reduction holds regardless of the
cone dimension and the rank of M(x). The correction therefore needs neither a conic solver nor
a feasible anchor. It also remains feasible for any approximate root, unlike splitting layers whose
feasibility is tied to convergence.
Our contributions are as follows.
• We propose SOC-Net, a neural network architecture whose output satisfies input-dependent
SOC constraints by construction for every input. It needs only a closed-form projection or a
single scalar root per constraint.
• We prove that SOC-Net is feasible for every input even with an inexact root and leaves feasible
outputs unchanged. Under explicit regularity conditions on the moving slice, it also retains
universal approximation over continuous feasible targets. Its explicit Jacobian requires no
iterations, so the whole network can be trained end-to-end by standard backpropagation.

Remark 1
The separability condition (4) concerns reachability in the lifted space so that any
tuple of points from each reachable set is realizable by a common output. It allows rank-deficient
liftings and covers constraints that share output variables. Moreover, the learning objective need
not be separable: in Section 5.1, the per-contact friction constraints accompany a wrench-matching
objective that couples the contact forces. For a single-constraint case, (4) holds trivially for any
SOC constraint, including the non-surjective input-robust CBF constraint of Section 5.2.
The constrained network should meet three requirements: (i) hard feasibility, i.e., the output
lies in S(x) for every x ∈X, not merely on average or after training; (ii) differentiability almost
everywhere of the map from fθ(x) to the output, so that θ can be trained end-to-end; and (iii) no
loss of expressiveness, i.e., universal approximation over continuous functions satisfying (1).

5. Experiments
We evaluate SOC-Net on two control tasks, one for each case of Section 3: contact force allocation,
where several separable constraints each have a surjective lifting (Section 3.3), and safe control of
a 3-D aircraft under actuation uncertainty, where a single constraint has a non-surjective lifting.
Both use float64, with learned methods on an RTX 5090 GPU and conic solvers on one CPU core
(Appendices F and G).

6. Conclusion
SOC-Net is a novel neural network architecture whose output satisfies hard input-dependent SOC
constraints for every input. Its projection layer is differentiable almost everywhere with an explicit
Jacobian, so the whole network can be trained end-to-end while retaining universal approximation
of continuous feasible maps. This makes it suited to applications where prior knowledge must hold
exactly for every input and a conic solve per input is too slow, such as the control problems consid-
ered here. It would be interesting to extend SOC-Net beyond the separable setting of Assumption 1
to non-separable SOC constraints, which would make it applicable to problems such as trajectory
optimization where many coupled cone constraints act on the same decision variables.
