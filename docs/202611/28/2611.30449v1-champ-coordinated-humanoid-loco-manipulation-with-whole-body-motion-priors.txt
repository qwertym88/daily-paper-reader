Abstract
Coordinating locomotion and dexterous manipulation is
fundamental to humanoid robots operating in human en-
vironments.
A key challenge is learning motion priors
from diverse data and using them effectively for coor-
dinated robot control.
We present CHAMP, a unified
framework for humanoid loco-manipulation that combines
whole-body motion pretraining with adaptive feature ex-
traction. CHAMP pretrains a vision-language model to pre-
dict future body and hand motion from approximately 2,298
hours of heterogeneous human and robot data. A shared

motion representation enables unified whole-body motion
learning across embodiments from datasets with body an-
notations, hand annotations, or both. To ground these mo-
tion priors in robot control, we introduce a Coordination-
oriented Action Expert (CAE) and train it on replay data
generated using the target controller. Through sparse rout-

from multiple layers of the pretrained model to jointly gen-

In experiments, CHAMP
achieves overall success rates of 84.8% on seven simulated
tasks and 91.4% on seven real-world tasks with a Unitree
G1, outperforming the evaluated baselines in both settings.
In particular, CHAMP achieves 70.0% success on the long-
horizon popcorn-serving task, compared with 40.0% for the
best-performing baseline.
1. Introduction
Humanoid robots hold the promise of assisting people
across a wide range of everyday activities [8, 10, 26]. Ful-
filling this promise requires a broad repertoire of motor
skills. A key capability is loco-manipulation, which inte-
grates locomotion and object manipulation through whole-
body coordination [6, 13]. Locomotion and manipulation
are tightly coupled.
Body motion enables the hands to
reach and interact with objects, while the demands of ma-
nipulation shape how the robot moves and maintains bal-
ance [12]. These dependencies evolve throughout execu-
tion, requiring a policy to coordinate body and hand mo-
tion over time. We therefore aim to enable coordinated hu-
manoid loco-manipulation by learning whole-body motion
priors that capture these relationships and integrating them
into robot policies.
Learning such priors requires diverse examples of body
and hand motion, yet collecting demonstrations on physical
humanoids is costly and difficult to scale [22, 35]. Exist-
ing human and robot motion datasets offer a broader source
of supervision beyond demonstrations collected for a tar-
get robot [5, 15, 25]. Ψ0 pretrains a vision-language model
(VLM) to predict wrist poses and fingertip positions [28],
while WholeBodyWAM learns body-motion priors by pre-
dicting the future rotations of 21 body joints [31]. Despite
these advances, these methods do not jointly model body
motion and fine-grained hand motion during pretraining.
This motivates us to learn unified whole-body motion pri-
ors that explicitly capture body–hand coordination through
joint motion prediction, using heterogeneous datasets with
annotations for body motion, hand motion, or both.
Translating these whole-body motion priors into coor-
dinated robot behavior requires adapting representations
learned for motion prediction to robot action generation.
Ψ0 conditions its action expert on final-layer VLM fea-
tures [28]. GR00T N1.6 [19] and N1.7 [21] instead use
intermediate VLM features.
DiT4DiT [18] and Motion-
WAM [34] use features from a fixed video-model layer for
action generation, with DiT4DiT reporting better perfor-
mance using intermediate rather than final-layer features.
These methods use features from a source layer selected
in advance. However, as the task or observation changes,
the features most suitable for robot control may come from
different layers. We therefore investigate whether jointly
learning which layers to read and how to adapt their fea-
tures, under robot-action supervision, can better support co-
ordinated whole-body control.
To achieve coordinated whole-body control for hu-
manoid loco-manipulation, we present CHAMP, a unified
framework that combines whole-body motion pretraining
with adaptive motion feature extraction (Fig. 1). First, we
pretrain a VLM to predict future body and hand motion
from heterogeneous human and robot data, supervising only
the motion targets available in each sample. Second, we
use SONIC-replayed robot trajectories [17] to ground the
learned representations in robot control. In this stage, we
introduce a Coordination-oriented Action Expert (CAE)
that adaptively selects and fuses features from the pretrained
VLM. Through joint layer–expert routing, CAE uses the
fused features to condition flow-matching generation of
SONIC motion latents and hand actions. Finally, we fine-
tune the policy with task-specific demonstrations collected
through whole-body teleoperation. Our contributions are
threefold:
• We propose CHAMP, a unified framework for coordi-
nated humanoid loco-manipulation. It learns whole-body
motion priors from heterogeneous human and robot mo-
tion data and transfers the learned priors to robot control.
• We introduce CAE to adaptively extract motion features
from the VLM pretrained for whole-body motion predic-
tion. It jointly selects source layers and projection experts
for each input token, transforming and fusing these fea-
tures for coordinated whole-body control.
• Experiments demonstrate CHAMP’s effectiveness for co-
ordinated humanoid loco-manipulation, including long-
horizon execution.
CHAMP achieves overall success
rates of 84.8% on seven simulated tasks and 91.4% on
seven real-world tasks, outperforming the evaluated base-
lines in both settings.

CoorDex [12] learns separate body and hand control pri-
ors and coordinates them through reinforcement learning
with task-specific rewards. Motion-tracking models such as
SONIC [17] provide a reusable foundation for whole-body
execution, supporting diverse movements through a shared
controller.
Building on this foundation, OpenHLM [9]
adapts a VLA to generate full-body joint targets tracked by
SONIC. WholeBodyWAM [13] connects pretrained world–
action models to whole-body controllers through a unified
interface. To achieve more coordinated whole-body con-
trol for humanoid loco-manipulation, CHAMP focuses on
learning unified body and hand motion priors and adap-
tively extracting features from the motion-pretrained VLM
for robot control.
2.2. Learning Whole-Body Motion Priors
Human and robot motion datasets provide heterogeneous
resources for learning motion priors, with different embod-
iments, motion coverage, and annotations. Human datasets
such as Motion-X [15], EgoDex [7], ARCTIC [5], and
Xperience-10M [25] provide annotations of body motion,
hand motion, or both. Robot datasets such as HIW-500 [2]
and Humanoid Everyday [33] contain real-world humanoid
demonstrations of locomotion and manipulation. Ψ0 [28]
and λ0 [11] learn motion priors by predicting future hand
motion during pretraining and subsequently ground these
priors in robot control. WholeBodyWAM [31] learns body-
motion priors by predicting future rotations of 21 body
joints. WB-WAM [24] learns whole-body motion priors
from heterogeneous body and hand data in a robot-specific
action space. In contrast, CHAMP jointly predicts 3D body-
joint and fingertip positions, together with root motion, in a
shared motion representation across humans and multiple
robot embodiments. These shared targets support motion-
prior learning without requiring all sources to be mapped to
a single robot’s joint-angle space.
2.3. Pretrained Representations for Robot Control
The interface between a pretrained backbone and an action
policy determines how learned knowledge supports control.
GR00T models such as N1.6 [19] and N1.7 [21] extract fea-
tures from a fixed intermediate VLM layer. DiT4DiT [18]
and MotionWAM [34] condition action generation on fea-
tures extracted from fixed video-model depths. Although
these methods use attention to combine features, they ex-
tract those features from a fixed layer. ω-0 [14] extracts
a summary feature from the final VLM layer. Motion and
video queries then integrate this summary with additional
visual and language features. WholeBodyWAM [31] mod-
els video, motion, and actions through three branches that
interact via attention at each transformer layer. CAE ex-
tracts features adaptively from the motion-pretrained VLM.
For each input token, it jointly selects source layers and
layer-specific projection experts. By combining this adap-
tive selection with feature projection and fusion, the policy
learns both where to access motion knowledge and how to
adapt it for whole-body control.

3.2. Heterogeneous Motion Prediction Pretraining
We pretrain Qwen3-VL [1] to predict future body and hand
motion (Fig. 2A), using heterogeneous human and robot
data. Shared motion targets let datasets with different em-
bodiments and annotations contribute to the same VLM,
without requiring a common robot joint-angle space.
Pretraining data.
We use heterogeneous motion data
from humans, real robots, and simulation for pretraining.
Human data come from EgoDex [7], Xperience-10M [25],
and ARCTIC [5]. EgoDex provides hand-only annotations,
while Xperience-10M and ARCTIC provide both body and
hand annotations. Real-robot data come from HIW-500 [2],
PSI-Real [28], RoboCOIN [30], HumanoidEveryday [33],
and UnifoLM-WBT [27].
HIW-500 provides body-only

Embodiment-Sim [20] provides body and hand annotations

Motion representation and prediction.
Humans and dif-
ferent humanoid robot embodiments have key body parts in
common, including the pelvis, shoulders, elbows, wrists,
hips, knees, and ankles.
For embodiments with dexter-
ous hands, wrist and fingertip motion can likewise describe
hand movement.
We therefore use the motion of these
shared body and hand structures to define a common motion
representation across embodiments. Body motion mbody
comprises the pelvis position and orientation, together with
the 3D positions of 12 body joints, six on each side. Hand
motion mhand comprises the positions and orientations of
both wrists, together with fingertip positions per hand.

3.3. Robot Action Grounding
Robot action data.
To ground the pretrained motion prior
in the control of a target robot, we select robot datasets
whose body trajectories can be tracked by that robot. We
use Unitree G1 as the target platform.
The data come
from HIW-500 [2], HumanoidEveryday [33], and UnifoLM
datasets, spanning grippers and dexterous hands. We use
SONIC’s motion encoder [17] to convert the recorded body
trajectories into motion latents. Using these latents, we re-
play the body motions on a simulated G1 with the SONIC
controller to screen for instability and excessive drift. After
quality filtering, we retain 40,016 trajectory segments total-
ing approximately 254.5 hours. Each action in a retained
segment contains a 64-dimensional motion latent and the
corresponding end-effector commands. The resulting ac-
tion sequences are paired with RGB observations, instruc-
tions, and robot states. Motion encoding, simulation replay,
and data filtering are detailed in Sec. A.3.
Coordination-oriented Action Expert.
To generate co-
ordinated robot actions from the pretrained motion repre-
sentations, we freeze the VLM and train CAE on replayed
robot data (Fig. 2B). CAE combines a sparse mixture-of-

fusion with a diffusion transformer (DiT) for robot action

expert group with a distinct VLM depth. For each token,
a joint router selects layer–expert pairs, determining both
which motion representations to use and how to transform
and fuse them into the DiT hidden space. CAE has approx-
imately 589M trainable parameters.

3.4. Task Fine-tuning
For each task, we fine-tune the policy learned during robot
action grounding using simulation or real-world demonstra-
tions. Demonstrations are collected in both environments
using a PICO headset and handheld controllers for whole-
body teleoperation of a Unitree G1 equipped with Dex3
hands.
The demonstrations provide aligned RGB obser-
vations, instructions, robot states, and body and hand ac-
tion sequences. For the G1 robot with Dex3 hands, each
action contains a 64-dimensional motion latent and seven
joint commands per hand, giving da = 78. We keep the
VLM frozen and fine-tune CAE with the objective in Eq. 9
to adapt both feature selection and action generation to
each task. Collection details are provided in Supplementary
Sec. C.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Tasks and environments.
We evaluate CHAMP in both
simulation and real-world environments. In simulation, we
use seven humanoid loco-manipulation tasks from SIM-
PLE [29], illustrated in Fig. 4. BendPick and BendHan-
dover emphasize whole-body grasping and bimanual coor-
dination, respectively. OpenFaucet, OpenOven, and Open-
Trashcan involve articulated-object manipulation. Close-
Door and PushOfficeChair involve locomotion with object
interaction. Whole-body motion demonstrations for these
tasks are collected through teleoperation.
We evaluate seven real-world tasks on a Unitree G1
6


equipped with Dex3 hands, including the long-horizon pop-
corn serving task (Fig. 5). The tasks span kitchens, bed-
rooms, living rooms, restaurants, laundry rooms, and in-
dustrial settings. Fruit collection and bag shelving combine
walking with object pickup and placement. Bed making
and table wiping involve coordinated torso and arm mo-
tion. Cola dispensing involves positioning a cup under a dis-
penser, while laundry loading combines door operation and
clothes handling with repeated adjustments of foot place-
ment. Detailed task procedures are provided in Sec. C of
the supplementary material.
Baselines and evaluation.
We compare CHAMP with
six baselines.
π0.5 [3] and GR00T N1.6 [19] are gen-
eralist vision-language-action models.
Ψ0 [28] and ω-
0 [14] target humanoid loco-manipulation, while Diffusion
Policy (DP) [4] and Action Chunking with Transformers
(ACT) [32] are conventional imitation-learning methods.
We train all baselines using their official implementations,
adapted to the target robot’s control interface. For each task,
all methods use the same 100 demonstrations and number of
training steps.
We run 30 trials per simulation task, with 10 at each of
three environment randomization levels, and 10 trials per
real-world task. Overall success is the mean of per-task suc-
cess rates across seven tasks in each setting.
4.2. Simulation Results
CHAMP achieves the highest overall success rate of 84.8%
and ranks first on six of the seven simulation tasks (Ta-
ble 1). It outperforms the best-performing baseline, GR00T
N1.6 (61.9%), by 22.9 percentage points. CHAMP also
outperforms the humanoid loco-manipulation baselines Ψ0
(58.6%) and ω-0 (50.5%). On OpenOven, which requires
body adjustments during object manipulation, CHAMP
succeeds in 28 of 30 trials compared with 19 for Ψ0, yield-
ing the largest gain over the best baseline across tasks. On
bimanual BendHandover, CHAMP succeeds in 23 of 30 tri-
als, compared with 17 for GR00T N1.6. It also succeeds in
29 of 30 trials on each of CloseDoor and PushOfficeChair,
which combine locomotion with object interaction.
The
only exception is OpenTrashcan, where CHAMP succeeds
in 21 of 30 trials compared with 25 for GR00T N1.6.
4.3. Real-World and Long-Horizon Results
CHAMP achieves a mean success rate of 91.4% across the
seven real-world tasks in Table 2, completing 64 of 70 tri-
als. It outperforms GR00T N1.6 (74.3%) by 17.1 percent-
age points and ranks first on every task. CHAMP also out-
performs the humanoid loco-manipulation baselines Ψ0 and
ω-0, which achieve mean success rates of 57.1% and 61.4%,
respectively.
On laundry loading, CHAMP succeeds in 9 of 10 trials
compared with 6 for GR00T N1.6. During laundry loading,
the robot must repeatedly adjust its foot placement to open
the washing machine door, move closer to load clothes,
and step backward while closing the door, requiring flexible
lower-body control. During fruit collection, which requires
walking to the table and then grasping the fruits, CHAMP
succeeds in 9 of 10 trials compared with 7 for GR00T N1.6.
These gains span different coordination demands, including
body adjustment in bed making and walking while carrying
objects in bag shelving.
Interestingly, we observed adaptive responses that were
absent from the task demonstrations.
When an experi-
menter wiped up the cola spill as the robot reached for a
cleaning cloth, the robot withdrew its hand instead of pro-
ceeding with wiping. During fruit collection, if the robot
failed to grasp the fruit, it would autonomously try several
more times to complete the task. These qualitative observa-
tions suggest that CHAMP can adjust its ongoing behavior
in response to scene changes and failed interactions.
Long-horizon task.
Popcorn serving requires the robot to
grasp a scoop, pick up a bucket with its other hand, scoop
popcorn, and pour it into the bucket (Fig. 5g). It then walks
to a table while carrying the filled bucket and sets it down.
This sequence involves multiple object interactions and bi-
manual coordination. The robot must also place the bucket
securely on the table to avoid spilling the popcorn. A trial is
successful only if the robot completes the entire sequence.
CHAMP completes the full task in 7 of 10 trials, compared
with 4 for GR00T N1.6 and 3 each for Ψ0 and ω-0. The
conventional imitation-learning baselines DP and ACT suc-
ceed in 0 and 1 of 10 trials, respectively.
In one of CHAMP’s three failed trials, an unsuitable
scoop grip prevented scooping popcorn.
In the other
two, excessive scoop–bucket contact force during pouring
caused the robot to lose its grip. Such interactions are dif-
ficult to control, and similar failures can occur even during
teleoperation because no force feedback is available.

A. Data Preparation and Supervision
A.1. VLM Pretraining Data and Pose Representa-
tion
Data and temporal alignment.
The first-stage VLM is
pretrained to predict future motion from an RGB image,
a language instruction, and pose inputs. We first convert
the source recordings into a shared pose representation,
from which the VLM inputs and prediction targets are con-
structed. The nine sources in Table 4 contain 2,297.9 hours
across 20 processed subsets. All trajectories are stored at
30 Hz; resampling uses linear interpolation for positions
and spherical interpolation for rotations.
Pose representation for motion supervision.
To con-
struct the VLM’s motion targets, we represent pelvis and
wrist poses relative to the camera pose at the start of each
episode. This fixed reference frame is denoted by G = C0
and remains unchanged as the camera moves. Its axes fol-
low the OpenCV convention: +x right, +y down, and +z
forward. Positions are measured in meters. A source pelvis
or wrist pose at time u is converted to this reference frame
as
GTSu = (WTC0)−1WTSu,
(10)
where W is the source dataset’s world frame, Su is the
pelvis or wrist frame, and ATB maps coordinates from B
to A. We denote the resulting position and canonical ori-
entation by rG
u and Ru. Thus, W is used only to convert
source poses into the fixed camera reference.
Local articulation is represented separately: body-point
coordinates are relative to the pelvis at the same timestamp,
and fingertip coordinates are relative to the corresponding
wrist at that timestamp. These coordinates, denoted by bu,
describe body and finger configurations relative to their par-
ent joints.
We standardize these joint-attached axes across sources.
The pelvis frame uses x forward, y left, and z up. In each
wrist frame, x points distally toward the fingers and z ra-
dially toward the thumb; y completes the right-handed ba-
sis as z cross x. These directions define the joint-attached
frames, rather than the fixed camera reference. A source-
specific constant rotation converts each native joint basis to
this convention. We right-multiply the native orientation
by this rotation and apply its inverse to the local point co-
ordinates, leaving the reconstructed positions in the fixed
camera frame unchanged.
The resulting body vector contains 45 values: pelvis po-
sition (3), 6D rotation (6), and 12 local body points (36).
The points are ordered as the left shoulder, elbow, wrist,
hip, knee, and ankle, followed by the corresponding right-
side joints. The hand vector contains 48 values, with the left
hand before the right. Each hand contributes wrist position
(3), 6D rotation (6), and five local fingertip positions (15),
ordered thumb, index, middle, ring, and little.
Source-specific processing.
EgoDex provides ARKit-
world wrist and fingertip transforms. ARCTIC uses person-
alized SMPL-X and MANO models to recover body joints,
wrists, and fingertip vertices. For Xperience-10M, captions
define action intervals, while SLAM poses and calibrated
stereo-left camera-to-device transforms determine camera
poses. SMPL-H roots and keypoints provide body geom-
etry; wrists and metacarpals define palm frames, and valid
MANO annotations provide fingertip positions.
HIW-500 uses matched G1/Dex1 forward kinematics
from 29 motor positions and odometry pelvis poses, syn-
chronized to head-image timestamps with a nominal D435
camera mount. HumanoidEveryday uses matched G1/Dex3
and H1/Inspire models with recorded arm, leg, and hand
states.
PSI-Real combines arm forward kinematics with
recorded torso orientation and height. RoboCOIN aligns
source end-effector poses with bilateral G1/Dex3 wrist
forward kinematics before applying the nominal D435
mount.
UnifoLM-WBT uses floating-base poses and 29
body joints, with matched BrainCo or Inspire hand mod-
els and the wrist-yaw link as wrist root. The three GR00T-
X-Embodiment-Sim subsets use Fourier GR1 forward kine-
matics with complete leg coordinates.

B. Implementation Details
B.1. Model Architecture and Routing
Backbone and feature readout.
We use Qwen3-VL-2B-
Instruct and extract the outputs of decoder blocks L =
{4, 12, 20, 28}. Each output, including that of block 28,
passes once through the backbone’s frozen final normaliza-
tion. The normalized feature is shared by the router and the
projection experts assigned to that layer.
Routing and action generation.
CAE connects the
frozen VLM to the action DiT through sparse cross-layer
projection (Table 6). Each expert is an independent affine
map from one candidate layer to the action-conditioning
space. This structure extends single-layer MoE projection,
in which all experts receive features from the same depth,
and layer-only fusion, which assigns a single projection to
each layer.
A joint two-layer MLP with SiLU activation concate-
nates features at each token position across the candidate
layers and scores all layer–expert pairs. Within each layer,
it selects two experts and computes a layer score as the log-
sum-exp of their scores. The two highest-scoring layers
define four active pairs, whose scores are jointly normal-
ized by a single softmax at temperature 1 (Eqs. 6–8). Only
the selected experts are evaluated for each token.
Their
weighted sum directly produces a 1536-dimensional condi-
tioning feature, preserving token order and sequence length.
A linearly projected robot-state token is appended to the
fused conditioning sequence. Noisy actions are embedded
by an MLP with learned temporal position embeddings.
Each DiT block applies noncausal joint attention to action
and conditioning tokens, with separate query/key/value pro-
jections for the two streams and masking of padded condi-
tioning tokens. Flow time modulates adaptive normaliza-
tion.

Optimization.
Table 8 summarizes the settings for first-
stage VLM pretraining, second-stage action grounding,
and task-specific fine-tuning. VLM pretraining updates all
VLM parameters while keeping the fitted FAST tokenizers

Robot action grounding and simulation fine-tuning opti-

use AdamW with ϵ = 10−8, bfloat16 mixed precision, and
gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. For each simulation task,
CAE is initialized from the 100,000-step grounding check-
point and the optimizer is reset. Router scores, probabilities,
and balancing statistics are computed in float32.
Real-world fine-tuning.
Real-world task policies initial-
ize CAE from the action-grounding checkpoint and retain
the frozen motion-pretrained VLM. We optimize CAE us-
ing eight GPUs with 16 samples per GPU, yielding a global
batch size of 128. AdamW uses coefficients (0.95, 0.999),
weight decay 10−6, and ϵ = 10−8. The learning rate in-
creases during warmup to 10−4 and then follows cosine
decay. Training uses bfloat16 precision and gradient-norm
clipping at 1.0. All seven tasks retain the 43-dimensional
state, 78-dimensional action, and 30-frame action horizon.
Image preprocessing.
For VLM pretraining and action
grounding, images are resized with Lanczos interpolation
according to their source resolution (Table 7), followed
by patch-aligned resizing with the Qwen image processor
within a pixel range of 1,024–129,600.
Simulation frames are decoded at their aligned observa-
tion indices, downsampled from 640×360 to 320×180 with
Lanczos interpolation, and cached as RGB before augmen-
tation. Training reads the shared cache and applies online
color jitter, with brightness, contrast, and saturation factors
in [0.8, 1.2] and hue shifts in [−0.05, 0.05].
Heterogeneous action and state interfaces.
The ground-
ing datasets provide native action dimensions of 66, 76, or
78 and state dimensions of 31, 41, or 43 (Sec. A.3). We
accommodate all sources with a shared 78-dimensional ac-
tion head and 43-dimensional state interface. Native vec-
tors occupy the leading channels, with trailing channels
zero-padded.
Actions retain the source order of the 64-
dimensional SONIC latent followed by left- and right-end-
effector targets; states retain the 29 body-joint entries fol-
lowed by the corresponding end-effector states.

All four variants use the

D.2. CAE Routing Analysis
Measurement protocol.
We analyze seven separately
fine-tuned task models using sampled observations.
Tokens are grouped by their positions in the multimodal
sequence into image, instruction, and pose-text categories,
excluding special tokens.
Pose-text comprises wrist and
pelvis information in the VLM prompt, distinct from the
DiT’s robot-state token. We average weights within each
sampled observation and token category, then across sam-
ples and equally across the seven task models.
Layer and expert allocation.
Routing profiles differ
across token categories at both the layer and expert levels
(Fig. 6). Image tokens allocate 71.9% of their weight to
layers 12 and 28, while instruction tokens allocate 64.1% to
layer 4. Pose-text weights are more distributed, with layer
28 receiving the largest share at 39.8%. Within layer 28,
expert 5 receives 13.8% of image-token weight and 2.8% of
pose-text weight, whereas expert 2 receives less than 0.1%
and 10.0%, respectively.
Temporal changes across samples.
Routing also varies
across temporally ordered samples within each task model
(Fig. 7a). Between the initial and final thirds of each sam-
pled sequence, image-token weight on layer 28 decreases
by 15.4 percentage points for OpenFaucet and increases by
4.6 for CloseDoor. In BendHandover, layer-28 weight re-
mains nearly constant, while layer-4 weight decreases by
4.7 points and layer-20 weight increases by 4.1 points.
Route changes and their magnitude.
We compare rout-
ing at the same 60 image-grid positions between tempo-
rally adjacent sampled observations. The selected layer pair
changes in 23.8% of comparisons; in 30.4%, the layer pair
is retained but the selected experts change; and in 45.7%,
the expert set is unchanged (Fig. 7b). We quantify weight
variation by 0.5∥w′ −w∥1, where each vector contains the
weights of all 32 experts, with zeros for unselected experts.
This measure lies in [0, 1], with task means ranging from
18.3% to 35.6% when expressed as percentages (Fig. 7c).
