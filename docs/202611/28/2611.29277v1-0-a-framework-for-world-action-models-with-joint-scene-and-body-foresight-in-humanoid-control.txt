Abstract
World Action Models anticipate visual outcomes to guide
robot actions, yet humanoid interaction also depends on how
the robot’s body responds. Visual foresight alone can miss
body motion, while latent-distance losses do not directly
supervise action responses. We present ζ0, a framework that
turns vision-language-action policies into World Action Mod-
els by foreseeing future visual features and measured joint
trajectories in a shared latent representation. During train-
ing, a teacher conditioned on recorded futures provides a
reference action response, and dual-response transfer trains
the causal predictor to match it through the teacher and
the executing policy. All components are trained jointly,
and deployment uses only causally predicted futures. On
seven HumanoidArena tasks, ζ0 built on Ψ0 and GR00T N1.7
reaches 63.6% and 79.5% mean success, 18.8 and 9.0 points
above these bases. On six real-world tasks, it reaches 78.3%
and 85.0%, gains of 16.7 and 10.0 points. On GR00T, its
79.5% tops all compared seven-task joint policies. Project
page: https://chenyizhi123.github.io/zeta0-project/.
1. Introduction
Humanoid interaction couples whole-body motion with
changes in the surrounding scene. Pushing a box, for exam-
ple, moves the object while the arms keep contact and the
legs and torso support the motion. World Action Models
couple action generation with anticipated future observa-
tions [28, 33, 46, 50, 51, 56]. For a humanoid, the conse-
quences of an action also include its own body motion, which
egocentric images may capture only partially and measured
joint trajectories record directly. We therefore ask a policy
to foresee scene and body changes jointly.
Demonstrations provide this foresight as supervision:
each action is followed by recorded images and measured
body states. At execution time, those futures have not yet oc-
curred and must be predicted from causal inputs. A common
approach fits future predictions to recorded observations or
their latent representations [21, 23, 56]. Latent-distance su-
pervision, however, does not directly reflect how a policy
uses the prediction: nearby futures need not induce the same
action, and equally sized errors can have different effects on
behavior. We therefore also supervise prediction in action

space, asking whether a predicted future induces the action
that the recorded future would.
We introduce ζ0, a framework that turns a vision-
language-action policy into a World Action Model with joint
scene-and-body foresight for humanoid control, illustrated
in Fig. 1. During training, a future encoder summarizes sub-
sequent visual features and measured body trajectories into a
shared temporal latent representation, and a causal predictor
learns to estimate it from inputs available at execution time.
The representation, predictor, and action models are trained
jointly; at deployment, the policy acts on the predicted future
and current observations.
To supervise prediction in action space, we introduce
dual-response transfer. A teacher learns to generate actions
conditioned on recorded futures, providing a reference action
response, and we train the predictor so that predicted futures
induce this response through two readers. The teacher term
asks the prediction to carry the action-relevant information
the teacher extracts from recorded futures; the executing-
policy term asks for the same response from the reader used
at deployment.
We make three contributions:
• Joint scene-and-body foresight framework: ζ0 turns
vision-language-action policies into World Action Models.
• Dual-response transfer: a training objective that super-
vises causal prediction through the responses of a recorded-
future teacher and the executing policy.
• Consistent gains in simulation and the real world: ζ0
built on Ψ0 and GR00T N1.7 raises mean success over
each base by 18.8 and 9.0 points on seven HumanoidArena
tasks and by 16.7 and 10.0 points on six real-world tasks.
2. Related Work
Whole-body humanoid learning.
Whole-body humanoid
learning studies coordinated locomotion, posture, and manip-
ulation, with hierarchical control separating high-level deci-
sions from low-level execution [9, 13]. Tracking controllers
such as ExBody, ExBody2, HOVER, and SONIC learn to
execute reference motions or end-effector targets across the
body [14, 26, 30, 39], and ASAP aligns simulated and real-
world physics to learn agile whole-body skills [25]. Teleop-
eration systems collect coordinated demonstrations [24, 53],
while HumanPlus and 3D diffusion policies connect visual
observations to autonomous humanoid skills [17, 54]. Hu-
manoidBench evaluates locomotion and manipulation across
simulated tasks [45], and HumanoidArena emphasizes ego-
centric hierarchical whole-body learning [47]. These direc-
tions provide motion-control interfaces and demonstration-
based learning settings for humanoid interaction; ζ0 builds
on such an interface and asks how a high-level policy can
anticipate the consequences of its own commands.
Vision-language-action policies.
Recent VLA work
adapts pretrained vision-language representations to robot
control using demonstration data [6, 11, 31]. Building on
large-scale robot transformers [7], generalist policies such
as Octo and the π series learn across tasks and embodi-
ments [4, 42, 44]. Action generation has developed along-
side representation learning, with action chunking [55] and
diffusion- or flow-based generators [4, 15] providing inter-
faces for continuous robot commands. Receding-horizon
execution refreshes the observation context between action
chunks, combining multi-step generation with feedback dur-
ing control [15]. For humanoids, GR00T N1, Ψ0, and HuGe
combine pretrained vision-language backbones with action
generators [38, 41, 49], and Kimodo-Policy learns kinematic
action models for loco-manipulation [48]. These policies
provide foundations for studying how additional predictive
information can condition action generation.
World Action Models.
World models learn how an en-
vironment responds to actions, either to learn behaviors by
imagining latent trajectories [21–23] or as generative and
predictive models trained on large video corpora [2, 8, 40].
Text-guided video generation can serve directly as a pol-
icy [16], and frozen self-supervised visual features can serve
as world-model states for planning [57]. In feature-space
planning, candidate action sequences are optimized so that
predicted future features approach those of a goal observa-
tion [57]. Predictive policies couple action learning with
future visual prediction [28, 46, 50] or jointly model future
observations and actions [34, 58]. FLARE aligns policy fea-
tures with future visual latents [56], DIAL separates latent
intent prediction from execution [12], and ARPS learns pre-
dictive states from future features and their changes [20].
Being-M0.7 jointly models visual and motion futures, then
trains an action expert on a frozen, robot-adapted prior [52].
Most of these methods represent the future by images or
visual features and supervise prediction by reconstruction or
latent distance. ζ0 foresees the scene and measured body tra-
jectories in one shared latent representation and supervises
its causal prediction in action space, keeping the predictor
coupled to action learning throughout training.
Privileged information and action supervision.
Learn-
ing with privileged information transfers guidance from
training-only observations to deployable policies [10, 32].
Knowledge distillation matches a student’s outputs to a
teacher’s and thereby updates the student’s parameters [27].
Action-space constraints provide another form of supervi-
sion: cross-view action consistency matches flow velocities
across action-equivalent observations [29]. Dual-response
transfer in Sec. 3.5 also compares action responses, but it
holds the readers’ parameters fixed and updates the predictor
only through the readers’ input, the predicted future.

3. Method
ζ0 is a framework that turns a base vision-language-action
policy into a World Action Model by adding joint scene-and-
body foresight, a causal prediction of how the scene and the
robot’s body will change over a short future horizon. The
base supplies the vision-language backbone, the state and
action interfaces, and, where available, pretrained weights. It
adds a shared latent representation of future scene and body
changes, predicts it from causal inputs, and conditions action
generation on the prediction. We instantiate ζ0 on Ψ0 and
GR00T N1.7, denoting the resulting models by ζ0 (Ψ0) and
ζ0 (GR00T), as set up in Sec. 4.1. Sections 3.2–3.6 describe
the representation, its causal and world prediction, action
learning, dual-response transfer, and joint optimization and
deployment; Fig. 2 separates training from deployment.
3.1. Problem formulation
At time t, let xt = (ot, st, ℓt) contain the current image
observation, measured state, and instruction. Let pt be the
prefix of an action chunk already committed by the con-
troller [5]. A training demonstration additionally provides
an action chunk at and a continuation yt of future visual fea-
tures and body measurements, with timestamps and validity
masks. We divide this continuation into K temporal groups
ending at increasing horizons. Each group contains a visual
target and body measurements at timestamped query points;
the body target is a trajectory rather than a single joint vector
at the group endpoint.
The base policy’s vision-language backbone extracts cur-
rent visual-language context and visual features for future
supervision. Although both may use joint coordinates, the
measured body target differs from the commanded action:
the action is a target for the low-level controller, whereas the
measured trajectory records how the body actually moves
under contact, balance, and tracking dynamics, which is the
consequence the policy must anticipate.

4. Experiments
We evaluate ζ0 built on Ψ0 [49] and on GR00T N1.7, a later
release of GR00T N1 [41]. On seven HumanoidArena [47]
tasks, we compare ζ0 on each base with that base policy
and include ACT [55] and π0.5 [44] as additional baselines,
supplemented by published seven-task joint-policy results.
Component ablations on both bases examine the shared fu-
ture representation and its coupling to action generation. We
then measure body forecasts with ζ0 (Ψ0) and evaluate both
ζ0 models on six real-world tasks.
4.1. Experimental setup
Benchmark and data.
We use the Unitree G1 setup in
HumanoidArena and seven tasks: double desk, football, pick
and place box, boxing, open door, sit sofa, and visual naviga-
tion. Each task contributes 100 demonstrations, giving 700
demonstrations in total. Each policy trained in our experi-
ments is one shared model trained on all seven tasks with
uniform task sampling and global batch size 512. In these
runs, policies consume current egocentric visual context,
a 64-dimensional state, an instruction, and the committed
action prefix, and output 40-dimensional semantic actions.
Future-body supervision in our models comprises 29 mea-
sured joints: 14 arm, 12 leg, and three waist coordinates.
Instantiations.
ζ0 (Ψ0) uses the frozen Qwen3-VL-2B
vision-language backbone [3] released with Ψ0 and ran-
domly initializes the ζ0 task modules, whereas the Ψ0 base-
line starts from its full pretrained checkpoint. ζ0 (GR00T)
and the GR00T N1.7 baseline both start from the pre-
trained GR00T N1.7 checkpoint; in ζ0 (GR00T), the teacher
and executing policy are copies of its native diffusion-
transformer [43] action head, and the predicted future enters
its cross-attention as additional context tokens. All seven-
task policies we train, including ablations and baselines,
use 40,000 updates and are evaluated at the final check-
point; π0.5 and Ψ0 start from their pretrained checkpoints.
In all experiments, execution uses a 30-step planning win-
dow at 50 Hz: the controller executes 24 steps and retains
the last six as the committed prefix of the next chunk, cor-
responding to 0.6, 0.48, and 0.12 s. The forecast covers
this window in K = 3 groups ending at 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 s,
with zt ∈R3×8×256. Loss weights are λ∆= λD = 0.25,
λprior = λworld = λr = 1, and λreg = 10−3. Optimizer
and latent settings are listed in Appendix A.
Closed-loop evaluation.
We use scenario seeds 0, 1, and
2 with 20 episodes per seed, giving 60 episodes per task and
420 per setting. All policies in our runs share the benchmark
environments, success predicates, episode limits, and SONIC
low-level controller [39], and chunked execution with a com-
mitted action prefix, detailed in Appendix A.5. We report
per-task success and the equal-weight mean. Scenario seeds
vary evaluation conditions, and each of our settings uses one
training seed.

4.5. Real-world experiments
Setup.
We compare ζ0 (Ψ0) and ζ0 (GR00T) with their
respective bases and π0.5 on six Unitree G1 tasks: hair dry-
ing, pushing a chair, clearing a table, carrying tape around
an obstacle, placing a doll, and tidying a scene, shown in
Fig. 4. The tasks place different demands on the policy:
hair drying requires sustained arm motion near a person;
pushing a chair and placing a doll combine walking with
grasping; carrying tape around an obstacle couples carry-
ing with obstacle-aware locomotion; and table clearing and
scene tidying chain several object interactions in one episode.
All five policies share three RGB views, measured body-and-
hand state, the action interface, the SONIC controller, and
the execution protocol, and each is trained separately on each
task with the same demonstrations and 20,000 updates. Each
policy is evaluated for 10 trials per task with randomized
robot and object positions; failures and timeouts count as
unsuccessful, and Appendix B lists demonstrations, success
criteria, and per-task counts.
Results.
ζ0 (Ψ0) reaches 78.3% mean success versus
61.7% for Ψ0 and 50.0% for π0.5, and ζ0 (GR00T) reaches
85.0% versus 75.0% for GR00T N1.7, gains of 16.7 and
10.0 points over the bases, as Fig. 5 shows. The gains con-
centrate on table clearing, obstacle avoidance, and scene
tidying, three long-horizon tasks in which several stages
must succeed in sequence: ζ0 (Ψ0) succeeds in 19 of their
30 trials versus 11 for Ψ0, and ζ0 (GR00T) in 23 versus 19
for GR00T N1.7. For ζ0 (Ψ0), scene tidying rises from 20
to 60% and table clearing from 50 to 80%; for ζ0 (GR00T),
table clearing rises from 70 to 90%; on the other three tasks,
every policy succeeds in 8 to 10 of 10 trials. We observed
that small deviations in the relative placement of the body
and objects tended to accumulate from stage to stage in base-
line trials until a later stage failed, whereas ζ0 executed more
stably across stages. ζ0 (GR00T) matches or exceeds the
other four policies on every task; Fig. 1(a) shows one of its
scene-tidying episodes.
Model size and latency.
The deployed ζ0 (Ψ0) has 2.59B
parameters, of which 2.13B form the frozen backbone and
461M the task modules. On one RTX 4090, a policy call
takes 212 ms on average and 221 ms at the 95th percentile,
using 6.3 GiB, excluding sensing, networking, and control;
measurement conditions are in Appendix A.5.
5. Conclusion
ζ0 is a framework that turns vision-language-action policies
into World Action Models by learning joint scene-and-body
foresight together with them. A shared latent representa-
tion captures future visual features and measured body tra-
jectories, and dual-response transfer supervises its causal
prediction in action space through a recorded-future teacher
and the executing policy. Built on Ψ0 and GR00T N1.7, ζ0
raises mean success over both bases by 18.8 and 9.0 points
on seven HumanoidArena tasks and by 16.7 and 10.0 points
on six real-world tasks with 10 trials each. Removing future
conditioning causes the largest drop on Ψ0, whereas remov-
ing body futures causes the largest drop on GR00T N1.7;
dual-response transfer improves both bases. Foreseeing the
body as well as the scene, and judging that foresight by the
actions it induces, lets a policy anticipate the consequences
of its own whole-body commands.
Future work.
Pretraining the shared scene-and-body rep-
resentation on large, diverse humanoid and human-motion
data offers a direct path to broaden foresight across tasks
and embodiments; combining it with explicit planning or
language-level reasoning over longer task structure is a nat-
ural next step. Dual-response transfer could also supervise
other predictive signals, such as future touch or force.

A. Implementation Details
This appendix gives the initialization, losses and parameter

A.1. Initialization and vision-language backbone
In ζ0 (Ψ0), all task modules, in simulation and on the real
robot, are randomly initialized. Its simulation and real-robot
versions use the Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct vision-language
model released with Ψ0, frozen throughout. Each camera im-
age keeps its aspect ratio, is resized with bilinear antialiasing
to a short side of at most 240 pixels without upscaling, and
is padded symmetrically with RGB value 127 to a multiple
of 32; pixels are rescaled by 1/255 and normalized with
mean and standard deviation 0.5, and the backbone’s own
resizing is disabled. Each image yields at most 160 merged
visual tokens, which the backbone’s context sequence keeps
unchanged. The current-visual branch and the future visual
targets use post-merger features averaged over a 4 × 4 grid
with area weights over the valid image region, giving 16
tokens of 2048 dimensions per image; pure padding receives
zero weight (Fig. S1a).

A.2. Losses, masks, and parameter updates
Reconstruction errors are averaged over valid elements per
sample and combined with their loss weights; body errors are
additionally weighted per joint. Temporal scene differences
require valid visual targets in consecutive groups. Body
differences additionally require valid corresponding query
slots in the two groups. World prediction uses a cumulative
action-coverage mask: a missing action segment invalidates
the affected group and subsequent world groups. This eli-
gibility rule applies to world losses; the example remains
available for ordinary policy learning when its own targets
are valid.

Algorithm S1 summarizes joint training, with parameter

A.5. Action sampling and execution
In the action-flow convention of Sec. 3.4, clean data is at time
zero and Gaussian noise at time one, with velocity target
equal to noise minus the normalized action. ζ0 (Ψ0) inte-
grates backward from one to zero in ten steps; ζ0 (GR00T)
in simulation uses four Euler steps in its native time conven-
tion, as described above. A committed prefix remains clean
throughout sampling and is excluded from the action loss.
The predictor estimates the future latent once per chunk; it
has no flow-time input (Fig. S1b).
In HumanoidArena, the policy plans 30 action steps, exe-
cutes 24, and keeps the last six steps of the preceding chunk
as the committed prefix.
The real-robot latency of ζ0 (Ψ0) in Sec. 4.5 was mea-
sured after the trials on one RTX 4090 with batch 1, three
640 × 480 views, a BF16 backbone, and ten flow steps.
One call generates a 50-step chunk, from which execution
takes a 30-step window; it takes 212 ms on average (p95
221 ms) with no prefix and 216 ms with a 15-step commit-
ted prefix (deployment uses six), using 6.3 GiB, which fits
well within the 480 ms (24-step) execution window under
receding-horizon execution.

D. Body-Forecast Evaluation
The body-forecast statistics in Sec. 4.4 use one closed-loop
rollout per task of the shared ζ0 (Ψ0) checkpoint, fixed before
inference, with an anchor every 0.1 s over its first 8 s, giving
526 windows (the boxing rollout ends earlier). Each window
forecasts 30 steps at 50 Hz (0.02–0.60 s, or 10 query steps
per 0.2 s group) across the 29 measured body joints (14 arm,
12 leg, and 3 waist joints). The scene panels of Fig. 3 are
separate windows selected post hoc for display and are not
used in these statistics.
Figure S5 and Table S10 report the closed-loop joint
trajectories and error breakdowns across all seven tasks and
three limb groups. Along continuous rollouts, the causal
forecasts anticipate gait peaks in navigation left-knee flexion
(10.4◦RMSE versus 30.4◦for holding the anchor pose), hip
swing during football kicking (5.1◦versus 17.8◦), and right-
elbow extension in double-desk manipulation (7.2◦versus
12.3◦; Fig. S5a–c).
Although the lower limbs are frequently outside the head
camera’s field of view or occluded by manipulated objects
(Fig. S5d), the 12 leg joints incur the largest pose-persistence
error across the 526 windows (10.34◦pooled and 12.54◦at
+0.6 s) and gain the largest absolute error reduction from
ζ0 (4.35◦pooled and 5.23◦at +0.6 s; Fig. S5e). Across
all seven tasks, ζ0 lowers pooled RMSE and wins 83.46%
(439/526) of +0.6 s windows (Fig. S5f, Table S10); pick
and place box has the smallest drift (5.45◦, endpoint win
50.62%), yet still drops to 4.11◦.
Limitations. Each simulation setting uses one training seed
(420 episodes), so 1–2 pp ablation gaps should be read along-
side consistent cross-base trends. Real-world models train
per task (10 trials each), body forecasts omit root motion,
and the 212 ms latency excludes perception, networking, and
SONIC.
