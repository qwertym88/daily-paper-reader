JEPA-Shield: Safe Diffusion Planning with Latent World Models
via Conformal Shielding
Taekyung Kim1, Ege Yuceel2, Steven Hong3, Georgios Fainekos3, Dimitra Panagou1,4, Bardh Hoxha3
Abstract— Latent world models enable planning from images,
but their predictions do not directly establish whether a
robot will satisfy physical constraints. We present JEPA-Shield,
which connects a frozen action-conditioned Joint-Embedding
Predictive Architecture (JEPA) world model to task-space safety
enforcement. Two downstream models predict relative motion
from imagined latent rollouts and decode unsafe geometry from
the current image. Split conformal prediction turns end-to-end
motion-prediction errors into multi-horizon uncertainty tubes.
A backup-based shield checks these tubes against physical
constraints and screens both candidate trajectories and final
proposals in diffusion-inspired sampling-based model predictive
control. Under the stated exchangeability and initial-backup
assumptions, we establish probabilistic constraint satisfaction at
action-block boundaries for a returned plan executed without
replanning. We also introduce VParkBench, with geometric car
and tractor-trailer parking tasks and a editable photorealis-
tic Gaussian-splatting environment. In closed-loop evaluation,
JEPA-Shield achieves success rates of 84.4%, 82.3%, and
99.0%, respectively, with no observed constraint violations.
Ablations show complementary benefits from shielding and
uncertainty calibration. The results demonstrate the importance
of grounding latent world-model predictions in task-space
quantities and explicitly accounting for prediction uncertainty
in safety-critical planning. [Project Page]1 [Video]
I. INTRODUCTION
Planning from images requires more than predicting what
a robot will see next. A candidate maneuver must avoid
obstacles, remain within the workspace, and, for articu-
lated vehicles, avoid jackknifing. These requirements concern
physical motion and geometry, not similarity to a goal image.
Optimization-based planning can enforce explicitly modeled
constraints, but errors in perception and scene reconstruction
can make their evaluation unreliable [1].
World Models (WMs) learn predictive representations
from observation-action trajectories, enabling image-based
control through imagined rollouts [2]–[4]. Joint-Embedding
Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) predict representations
rather than reconstructing pixels [5]. LeWorldModel (Le-
WM), for example, learns action-conditioned latent dynamics
through next-embedding prediction and regularization [6],
[7], then plans toward encoded visual goals. Such representa-

A downstream probe can predict safety values from latent
rollouts, but its outputs reflect errors in both the predicted
latent trajectory and the learned safety mapping. Near a con-
straint boundary, an optimistic prediction can make an unsafe
maneuver appear acceptable. Thus, a favorable predicted
safety value alone does not establish physical constraint
satisfaction. Recent work has begun to address this gap. Kim
et al. calibrate a learned binary failure predictor for model-
predictive shielding [8]; SLS2 calibrates latent-dynamics and
constraint-prediction errors and propagates uncertainty sets
through robust model predictive control (MPC) [9]; and
concurrent work by Zhong et al. calibrates learned risk and
progress predictions before applying a separate deterministic
safety shield [10].
We propose JEPA-Shield with a guiding principle: predict
in latent space, evaluate constraints in physical space, and
account for prediction error before accepting an action
trajectory. A motion predictor converts the latent rollouts
of a frozen JEPA world model into multi-horizon relative
trajectories, while a spatial decoder estimates current unsafe
geometry from encoder features. Split conformal prediction
calibrates the end-to-end motion error to construct tubes
for robust constraint evaluation. A backup-based shield in-
tegrates these checks into diffusion-inspired sampling-based
MPC, retaining nominal prefixes only when their backup-
completed trajectories satisfy the robust constraints. Under
the stated initial-backup, exchangeability, and conservative
constraint-evaluation assumptions, the method provides prob-
abilistic constraint satisfaction at action-block endpoints over
a finite horizon. Fig. 1 summarizes the framework.
To evaluate the framework, we introduce VParkBench,
a visual parking benchmark and dataset spanning geometric
and editable Gaussian-splatting environments.2 On tractor-
trailer parking, JEPA-Shield increases success from 60.4%
to 82.3% relative to the unshielded ablation, while reducing
the observed unsafe rate from 34.4% to 0%. Across all
288 benchmark trials, the full method achieves the strongest
overall performance with no observed constraint violations.
Our main contributions are:
• We introduce downstream models that recover multi-
horizon relative motion from latent rollouts and decode
current unsafe geometry from spatial features of a frozen
JEPA world model.

III. JEPA-SHIELD
JEPA-Shield separates visual prediction from physical
constraint evaluation. After training Le-WM with (11), we
freeze its encoder and latent dynamics. Two downstream
models recover relative motion from imagined rollouts and
unsafe geometry from current spatial features. Calibrated
motion tubes and backup-based shielding determine which
proposed actions can be retained with a valid backup.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
A. Experimental Setup
1) VParkBench: We introduce VParkBench, a visual
parking benchmark for evaluating goal-directed planning
and control under safety constraints from bird’s-eye RGB
observations (Fig. 4). The benchmark includes car (tractor-
only) and tractor-trailer (TT) parking tasks. The robot must
reach a target parking configuration without collision or
workspace violations; TT must additionally avoid jackknif-
ing [14]. Nonholonomic dynamics and articulated geometry
require maneuvering through highly nonconvex safe sets.
We represent the car and TT states as
xcar = (xc, yc, sin θ, cos θ, v, δ),
xTT = (xr, yr, sin θT, cos θT, sin θR, cos θR, v, δ).
Here, (xc, yc) and (xr, yr) locate the car center and tractor
rear axle; θ, θT, and θR are the car, tractor, and trailer head-
ings; and v, δ denote velocity and steering angle. Calibration
uses (xc, yc, θ) for car and (xr, yr, θT, θR) for TT, so np = 3
and 4, respectively; v, δ are excluded from inflation. The
commands u = (vcmd, δcmd) are applied instantaneously at
0.10-s control intervals, with |vcmd| ≤1.5 m/s and steering
limits of ±35◦for car and ±45◦for TT.
The geometry-based simulator contains 16 parking spaces
for car and 40 bays arranged as 20 trailer pairs for TT. For the
photorealistic Gaussian-splatting (GS) setting, we reconstruct
a real 12-space parking lot from video using G4Splat [20].

4) Evaluation Protocol: An episode is classified as unsafe
if it contains a collision, workspace violation, or, for TT,
jackknifing. A violation-free episode is successful if its final
position error is at most 0.8 m and its heading error is at
most 15◦. For TT, both tractor and trailer headings must
satisfy the corresponding accepted goal configuration. All
other episodes are safe failures. The separate image-goal
evaluation instead requires the observed latent MSE to fall
below its checkpoint-specific threshold and to decrease by
at least 90% from its initial value; any unsafe transition
precludes success.
B. Results and Analysis
We organize the evaluation around four questions: Q1:
How does image-goal planning scale with physical goal
range? Q2: How well do policies learned from demon-
strations, including a fine-tuned VLA, generalize to unseen
parking goals and configurations? Q3: What are the contri-
butions of task-space prediction, shielding, and conformal
uncertainty calibration? Q4: How does performance change
in the photorealistic GS setting?
Image-goal planning (Q1). Fig. 2(a) evaluates Le-WM
CEM as the physical goal range increases. At Level 1 (L1),
success is 100.0%, 100.0%, and 96.9% for VParkBench Car,
VParkBench TT, and GS Car, respectively, but falls to 30.2–
33.3% at L2 and 0.0–20.8% at L3. Although the levels use
different held-out trajectories and therefore do not isolate
distance alone, the full parking benchmark in Fig. 2(c) shows
the same limitation: DINO-WM and Le-WM achieve no
success on the geometric car or TT tasks and only 6.3%
and 1.0% on GS Car, respectively. These results suggest that
latent image matching provides useful local guidance but
becomes less reliable in the evaluated longer-range tasks.
This is consistent with accumulating rollout error and the
absence of explicit task-space safety constraints in the latent
goal objective.
Policy generalization (Q2). Fig. 2(b) shows a pronounced
distribution shift for the VLA. Car success decreases from
100.0% on exact training-episode replay to 15.6% on unseen
VParkBench scenarios, while TT success decreases from
58.3% to 3.1%. The corresponding OOD car runs are 77.1%
unsafe, while 70.8% of the TT runs terminate as safe fail-
ures. Likewise, Diffusion Policy achieves only 9.4%, 0.0%,
and 30.2% success on car, TT, and GS Car, respectively


(Fig. 2(c)). Thus, policies learned from demonstrations,
including fine-tuning a large pretrained VLA, do not by
themselves provide reliable generalization to unseen parking
configurations or safety constraints.
Task-space safety and ablations (Q3). Even without
shielding, the task-space JEPA planner provides a substantial
gain: JEPA-Shield-Off achieves 75.0%, 60.4%, and 84.4%
success on car, TT, and GS Car, outperforming all non-
JEPA-Shield baselines. In comparison, Le-WM w/ Safety
Probe reaches 29.2%, 35.4%, and 57.3% success, while
producing 36.5%, 41.7%, and 12.5% unsafe episodes. A
learned scalar safety prediction evaluated along recursively
imagined rollouts remains subject to rollout and probe errors,
and a finite penalty does not constitute a constraint certificate.
Fig. 3 shows a representative TT comparison: Le-WM w/
Safety Probe reaches a jackknife configuration, whereas
JEPA-Shield completes the maneuver while maintaining the
articulation constraint.
Full JEPA-Shield achieves 84.4%, 82.3%, and 99.0%
success with no observed unsafe episodes across the 288
benchmark trials. Removing the conformal expansion re-
duces success to 78.1%, 72.9%, and 97.9% and reintroduces
unsafe episodes in the geometric settings. The mechanism is
illustrated in Fig. 4(b): because the conformal tube accounts
for uncertainty in the predicted pose coordinates, uncertainty
in the hitch angle enlarges the possible trailer occupancy even
when the nominal trajectory is nearly straight. The shield
therefore evaluates worst-case articulated placements within
the calibrated tube rather than only the nominal footprint.
Without shielding, TT has 34.4% unsafe episodes. Together,
the ablations demonstrate the complementary contributions
of shielding and conformal uncertainty calibration.
Photorealistic visual setting (Q4). Overall, performance
is generally higher on GS Car than on the geometric car
benchmark for methods evaluated in both environments.
Thus, despite the substantially more realistic visual appear-
ance, visual complexity alone does not appear to dominate
performance in these experiments. This comparison is not
controlled for appearance alone: GS Car also has a physically
smaller operating domain and different maneuvering geom-
etry. Rather, the results suggest that planning performance
depends strongly on the task-space geometry and safe motion
required to reach the goal, in addition to visual representation
quality. Fig. 4(c) shows a complete JEPA-Shield maneuver
in the reconstructed GS scene.
V. CONCLUSION
JEPA-Shield connects latent world-model prediction with
explicit evaluation of physical constraints. Its motion tubes
account for prediction error, while its backup-based shield
determines which proposed actions can be retained. Across
geometric and photorealistic parking tasks, the framework
combines strong task completion with no observed constraint
violations in 288 trials. The central lesson is that useful visual
predictions and reliable constraint evaluation are distinct
requirements: a world model can supply the former, while
task-space reasoning and uncertainty-aware shielding address
the latter.
