Near-Optimal Smooth Convex Optimization
with a Comparison Oracle
Peter Richtárik∗
Ammar Mahran∗
Abstract
We study the minimization of a convex d-dimensional function with L-Lipschitz gradient
when the only available information is a comparison oracle that reports which of two points
has the smaller objective value. Given an initial point within distance R of a minimizer,
the constants L and R, and A = LR2/ε, we give a deterministic method that returns an
ε-optimal point using O(
√
A [d log(2dA) + log2(2A)]) comparisons for A ≥1, counting all
comparisons spent in inner subroutines. The method is based on a normalized variant of
Nemirovski’s subspace method [20]: each iteration performs a line search along an estimated
gradient direction followed by a minimization over a two-dimensional affine subspace, and
both subroutines rely on a comparison oracle and basic arithmetic operations only. With
exact function values, combining finite-difference gradients with an accelerated method gives
the well-known bound of O(d
√
A) evaluations [7, 23]; our bound shows that one bit per
query suffices to match it up to logarithmic factors. Since two function values determine one
comparison, the exact function-value lower bound of Dorn et al. [7] implies that our bound
is optimal up to logarithmic factors when 32 ≤A ≤d2.
Key words: comparison oracle, derivative-free optimization, acceleration, oracle complexity,
smooth convex optimization

Acceleration is not immediate.
With exact function values, the complexity of the class of
functions satisfying Assumption 1.1 is known up to constants for 32 ≤A ≤d2. Finite differences
allow approximation of the gradient up to a small error, and combining them with an accelerated
gradient method gives an ε-optimal point after O(d
√
A) function values, deterministically with
coordinate differences [7, Corollary 2.3] and in expectation with random directions [23, Section 6],
which is optimal in this regime [7, Corollary 2.3]. Here, as for gradient methods [22], acceleration
refers to the improvement of the dependence on the accuracy from A to
√
A.
A comparison oracle only reveals the sign of f(u) −f(v), and two standard routes to
acceleration are not immediately accessible. First, the steps of accelerated gradient methods [22]
scale with the gradient norm, while comparisons cannot reveal it: since Ocf = Of for every
c > 0, comparisons determine at most the direction of ∇f, but not its magnitude. Second, the
standard reduction to strongly convex problems by regularization [1] is not available. Accelerated
methods for strongly convex functions, such as the comparison method of Lobanov et al. [18],
would be applied to the regularized function fµ(x) = f(x) + µ
2∥x −x0∥2 with µ of order ε/R2.
However, comparisons of f do not determine comparisons of fµ: the functions f and cf produce
the same comparisons for every c > 0, while fµ and cf + µ
2∥· −x0∥2 do not, in general.
The method we present in Section 3 circumvents these issues. It only uses unit gradient
directions, minimizations along rays, and minimizations over planes, and each of these can be
carried out to sufficient accuracy via comparisons (Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3).
Contributions.
Without strong convexity, the best known comparison methods for the class
of functions satisfying Assumption 1.1 use eO(dA) comparisons (normalized descent) or eO(d2)
comparisons (cutting planes) [33, Theorems 2 and 3]. Zhang and Li [33, Section 1] pose as an
open question whether comparison methods can be built on accelerated gradient methods, noting
that comparisons reveal the direction of the gradient but not its norm. We show that acceleration
is indeed possible with comparisons alone, and that—ignoring logarithmic factors—it improves
on both bounds when 1 < A < d2:

Related work.
Methods that only compare function values have a long history in derivative-
free optimization, among them Random Pursuit [27], which minimizes along random directions
and is invariant under strictly increasing transformations of the objective, the stochastic three
points method [2], and Gradientless Descent [9]. For smooth convex functions, these methods
need of order dLR2/ε iterations up to logarithmic factors, with R a radius of the level set
{f ≤f(x0)}, or assume strong convexity; an accelerated variant of Random Pursuit is proposed
in Stich et al. [27] as a heuristic without guarantees. Jamieson et al. [13] study optimization
with noisy comparisons and with noisy function values for strongly convex functions, with rates
that depend on how fast the probability of a correct reply approaches 1/2 as the two function
values approach each other, as in the Thurstone [29] or Bradley–Terry [3] models. Their lower
bounds [13, Theorem 1], and that of Kumagai [15, Theorem 14] for Gaussian noise, restrict
the queries to a compact set, so they do not apply directly to the unrestricted queries in (3).
Comparison-based optimization was further studied by Karabag et al. [14] and Zhang and Li [33].
For smooth convex functions and comparisons that are correct with a fixed probability p > 1/2,
a model that goes back to Feige et al. [8], Saha et al. [25] obtain eO(dA/(p −1/2)2) comparisons.
Comparison-based estimation of gradient directions appears in [6, 28, 33]. Lobanov et al. [18]
obtain acceleration for strongly convex functions with a randomized coordinate method: their
accelerated analysis treats the one-dimensional subproblems as solved exactly [18, Appendix C.2].
Scheinberg and Xiong [26] study comparison oracles without an underlying objective, and
measure accuracy by the distance from a preference level set to the optimal solutions, under
convexity, regularity, and growth conditions. For exact function-value oracles, Wu et al. [32]
and Dorn et al. [7] give lower bounds that match the finite-difference upper bound O(d
√

moderate-accuracy regime, for deterministic methods with queries in a ball and for randomized
methods with unrestricted queries, respectively. Concurrently with this work, the automated

but rescales ∇f. More generally, the iterates of Algorithm 1 do not change if f is replaced by
ϕ ◦f for a differentiable function ϕ with ϕ′ > 0, since gradient directions and exact minimizers
do not change; comparisons have the same invariance.

Comparison-based normalized subspace method
Algorithm 1 relies on three exact computations: a normalized gradient, a minimizer over a ray,
and a minimizer over a plane. In this section, we present a method that mirrors Algorithm 1
but only has access to the comparison oracle (3).
Note that none of the three computations can be carried out exactly with comparisons:
finitely many bits determine a direction or a minimizer only approximately, and the unrestricted
minima need not be attained (Remark 2.1). We therefore (i) estimate directions, (ii) solve the
ray and plane problems inexactly over large balls around x0, (iii) compare the output of each
solver with the point it started from and keep the better one, which makes the objective values
monotone, and (iv) run for a number of iterations fixed in advance. The last point replaces the
stopping test Gk = 0 of Algorithm 1: the method has no test for a vanishing gradient and does
not certify ε-optimality.
We proceed in three steps. Section 3.1 shows that the convergence proof of Theorem 2.1
tolerates errors. Section 3.2 constructs comparison routines for the three computations, and
Section 3.3 chooses their accuracies so that the errors stay within the tolerances of Section 3.1.

3.2
Comparison primitives
Recall that Algorithm 1 uses only gradient directions, not magnitudes. Such directions can be
estimated with finite differences, and the sign of a finite difference may be obtained via the
comparison oracle (3).
Directions.
For a small step h > 0, comparing F(p + hv) with F(p) reveals the sign of the
directional derivative ⟨∇F(p), v⟩, as long as this derivative is not too close to zero. Indeed,
by smoothness, the finite difference (F(p + hv) −F(p))/h differs from ⟨∇F(p), v⟩by at most
Lh∥v∥2/2. The routine Direction (Algorithm 3 in Section C) combines such sign tests in three
stages following Comparison-GDE of Zhang and Li [33, Algorithm 2]. First, it determines the
sign of every coordinate of the gradient. Second, it selects a pivot coordinate whose magnitude
is close to the largest one, by a tournament in which each comparison tests which of two
coordinates is larger in magnitude. Third, for every other coordinate, it determines the ratio of
that coordinate to the pivot coordinate by bisection, where each bisection step is a sign test
along a combination of two coordinate directions. Normalizing the vector of estimated ratios
gives the estimate of the gradient direction. The step sizes of the sign tests are chosen small
enough that the finite-difference error does not change any sign that matters for the result.

The comparison-based method
Algorithm 2 combines the two routines into a method that uses only comparisons. Each iteration
mirrors an iteration of Algorithm 1, with the following modifications:
(i) Directions. The unit gradient nk is replaced by the estimate bnk = Direction(xk; L, γ, ξ),
and the memory vector accumulates these estimates.
(ii) Inexact searches over balls. Instead of the whole ray {xk −αbnk : α ≥0}, the ray search
considers only the segment Ik of the ray that lies in the ball B(x0, Bk+1), and finds a
point z ∈Ik whose value is within ρline of the minimum of f over Ik. Likewise, instead of
the whole plane x0 + Vk+1, the plane search considers only the disk Ck+1 in which this
plane meets B(x0, Bk+1), and finds a point w ∈Ck+1 whose value is within ρplane of the
minimum of f over Ck+1.
(iii) Keeping the better point. The method compares z with xk and keeps the better of the two
as pk+1; it then compares w with pk+1 and keeps the better of the two as xk+1. Hence the
objective values f(xk) never increase.
(iv) Fixed horizon. The method runs for K iterations and returns xK.

Noisy comparisons.
Algorithm 2 is well defined and makes at most N = O(K[d log(dK) +
log2 K]) comparisons for every sequence of replies, correct or not (Lemma F.1). If each reply is
correct with probability at least a known p > 1/2 given all previous queries and replies (61), it
therefore suffices to repeat every comparison O((p −1/2)−2 log(N/δ)) times and to take majority
votes: with probability at least 1 −δ, all majority votes are correct, and Theorem 3.4 applies
(Corollary F.2). In this model, this improves the dependence of the bound of Saha et al. [25]
on the accuracy from 1/ε to 1/√ε. If the probability of a correct reply approaches 1/2 as the
two function values approach each other, then no p > 1/2 as in (61) exists and the repetition
argument does not apply.

Conclusion
A comparison oracle reveals neither function values nor gradient norms, yet it suffices for
accelerated smooth convex optimization. We gave a deterministic comparison-based method
that finds an ε-optimal point with eO(d
√
A) comparisons, which matches the accelerated
√
A
dependence of methods with exact function values and is optimal up to logarithmic factors
when 32 ≤A ≤d2. The key is that the analysis of the exact normalized subspace method
uses only gradient directions, one- and two-dimensional minimizations, and relaxed versions of
its orthogonality and descent properties, all of which comparisons can provide. The method
needs the constants L and R and the target accuracy in advance, its guarantees assume exact
arithmetic, and its search tolerances decrease polynomially in 1/K, which may make a floating-
point implementation demanding; noisy comparisons are covered when each reply is correct with
probability at least a known p > 1/2 given the previous replies (Corollary F.2). Removing these
requirements, giving finite-precision guarantees, closing the logarithmic gap to the lower bound,
and determining the optimal comparison complexity under persistent errors [4, 18], or under
noise whose probability of a correct reply approaches 1/2 as the function values approach each
other, remain open.

Noisy comparisons
This section shows that the guarantee of Theorem 3.4 extends to comparisons that are correct
with probability at least p > 1/2 given all previous queries and replies. Since Algorithm 2 makes
at most a fixed number N of comparisons for every sequence of replies, including wrong ones
(Lemma F.1), a union bound shows that repeating each comparison O((p −1/2)−2 log(N/δ))
times and taking majority votes makes all of them correct with probability at least 1 −δ; this is
the repetition argument of Feige et al. [8, Section 1.1] for noisy decision trees. On this event,
the run coincides with a run with exact comparisons, and Theorem 3.4 applies (Corollary F.2).
