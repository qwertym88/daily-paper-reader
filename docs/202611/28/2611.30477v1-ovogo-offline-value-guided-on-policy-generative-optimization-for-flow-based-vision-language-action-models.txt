ABSTRACT
Flow-based vision-language-action models can benefit from robot experience col-
lected during deployment, but repeatedly acquiring new physical interactions for
policy improvement is costly. Offline reinforcement learning provides a natu-
ral way to reuse collected experience, while flow-based policies offer an addi-
tional opportunity because fresh generation trajectories can be cheaply resampled
from the current policy at replay observations. This motivates separating offline
value learning from on-policy optimization of the generative process. We propose
OVOGO, an offline reinforcement learning framework that realizes this separa-
tion for flow-based VLAs. A central challenge in this formulation is to learn
a critic that remains grounded in logged robot experience while providing useful
value signals for newly generated actions. OVOGO introduces a Return-Anchored
Ranking Critic that combines in-sample distributional value learning with guarded
local ranking, improving discrimination among generated actions without assign-
ing them artificial return targets. The frozen critic then provides group-relative
value signals for selected-transition optimization of fresh action candidates sam-
pled from the current flow policy. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0, DexJoCo, and
LIBERO, as well as a real-robot dexterous manipulation task, show that OVOGO
improves the initial VLA across all four settings and achieves higher success rates
than the evaluated offline adaptation and value-guided baselines. These results
suggest that offline robot experience can be repeatedly reused for value learning
while inexpensive generative sampling supports policy optimization, providing an
effective approach to experience-based post-training of flow-based VLAs.

INTRODUCTION
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) have demonstrated strong manipulation capabilities and
generalization by combining large-scale, cross-task robot data with pretrained vision-language rep-
resentations Kim et al. (2024); Black et al. (2024); Intelligence et al. (2025b). However, further
improving these models still relies heavily on collecting high-quality demonstrations, whose ac-
quisition is expensive and difficult to scale. In contrast, deployment and autonomous execution
naturally produce large amounts of experience spanning successful, failed, and suboptimal behav-
iors, together with task-outcome signals that behavior cloning cannot fully exploit. Reinforcement
learning (RL) provides a natural mechanism for learning from such experience, and offline RL is par-
ticularly attractive for robotics because it can repeatedly reuse previously collected interactions and
their reward feedback without requiring new physical interaction for every policy update Kostrikov
et al. (2021); Intelligence et al. (2025a); Wang et al. (2026b). As VLA training moves beyond

imitation pretraining toward experience-based post-training, offline RL is emerging as a promising
approach for improving large generative VLAs from accumulated robot experience.
Applying offline RL to flow-based VLAs, however, is not straightforward. Because actions are
produced through a multi-step flow process, a central question is how value information should
be translated into updates of the generative policy. Existing approaches adopt different value-to-
policy interfaces. RECAP converts estimated advantages into conditioning variables and extracts an
improved policy through supervised training on collected experience Intelligence et al. (2025a). This
design is simple and scalable, but its improvement supervision is attached to replayed actions rather
than actions freshly sampled from the current policy. Other approaches, such as LWD and Q-VGM,
use the critic’s action gradient to construct improvement targets for the flow dynamics Wang et al.
(2026b;c). These methods enable more direct value-based policy improvement, but make the update
depend on reliable action-gradient information from the critic around policy-generated actions. Flow
policies offer a complementary opportunity because physical interactions are expensive and should
be reused extensively, whereas generation trajectories are inexpensive computational samples that
can be repeatedly resampled from the current policy at the same replay observation. This asymmetry
suggests that physical experience and generation trajectories need not be consumed at the same rate
during policy improvement.
Motivated by this observation, we decouple flow-based VLA reinforcement learning by separating
offline value learning from on-policy generative policy optimization. The former learns long-horizon
values from a fixed robot dataset, while the latter repeatedly samples fresh actions from the current
flow policy at replay observations. The critic serves as the key interface between these two pro-
cesses. Directly incorporating policy-generated actions into Bellman updates would make temporal
targets depend on value estimates for actions without observed environment outcomes. Errors in
these estimates, especially overestimation, can then propagate through bootstrapping and be further
exploited during policy improvement. Keeping Bellman value propagation in-sample provides a
more stable foundation for learning long-horizon returns because temporal targets remain grounded
in replayed experience. However, restricting all critic supervision to replayed actions would leave
the critic unable to reliably distinguish fresh candidates sampled from the evolving policy. The key
challenge is therefore to preserve in-sample temporal value propagation while extending the critic’s
discriminative ability beyond replayed actions, without compromising the return-grounded value
estimates on which offline learning relies.
In this paper, we propose OVOGO (Offline Value-Guided On-Policy Generative Optimization), an
offline RL framework for flow-based VLAs that combines stable offline value learning with fresh
on-policy optimization of the generative process. For offline value learning, OVOGO introduces a
Return-Anchored Ranking Critic (RARC) that separates long-horizon return propagation from local
action discrimination. RARC uses in-sample distributional value learning to propagate long-horizon
values through real replay transitions without bootstrapping through policy-generated actions, and
augments this return-grounded objective with local ranking supervision around replayed actions.
Synthetic actions receive only relative preference supervision rather than artificial Bellman targets,
while a guarded ranking objective combines temperature-dependent gradient decay with a gap cutoff
to control ranking pressure, helping preserve the return scale established by real experience. With
RARC frozen, OVOGO repeatedly samples groups of fresh action candidates from the current flow
policy at replay observations and constructs group-relative improvement signals directly from their
scalar critic values, without differentiating through the critic. We further adopt a selected-transition
flow update inspired by Flash-GRPO He et al. (2026), making on-policy generative optimization
tractable for large flow-based VLAs.
In our experiments, we evaluate OVOGO on RoboTwin 2.0 Chen et al. (2025b), DexJoCo Wang et al.
(2026a), and LIBERO Liu et al. (2023), as well as a real-robot dexterous manipulation task. The
results demonstrate that OVOGO more effectively improves flow-based VLAs from collected robot
experience than existing offline adaptation and value-guided methods, while additional comparisons
and ablations analyze the contributions of its critic design and generative policy optimization.

RELATED WORK
2.1
REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FOR VLA POST-TRAINING
Recent studies have explored reinforcement learning (RL) as a post-training paradigm for improv-
ing pretrained vision-language-action models (VLAs). One line of work directly applies on-policy
RL to VLAs by repeatedly collecting trajectories from the current policy and optimizing them with
PPO Schulman et al. (2017), GRPO Shao et al. (2024), or related objectives Tan et al. (2025); Li
et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2025a). While effective, such approaches require repeated environment
rollouts, which are expensive for robotic systems; consequently, many studies rely primarily on sim-
ulated environments for RL training. Another line of work seeks to better reuse previously collected
robot experience. RECAP Intelligence et al. (2025a) converts learned value information into advan-
tage conditioning and improves the policy through supervised learning, preserving the scalability of
imitation-style training but deriving its policy-improvement signal primarily from actions already
contained in the collected experience. More recent approaches such as LWD Wang et al. (2026b)
and Q-VGM Wang et al. (2026c) learn explicit critics and use action gradients of the critic to guide
flow-policy updates, enabling more direct value-based improvement but placing stronger require-
ments on the local first-order accuracy of the critic around policy-generated actions. In contrast, our
method resamples candidate actions from the current flow-based VLA and uses only scalar critic
values to construct relative policy-improvement signals, directly optimizing the current generative
policy without differentiating through the critic.
2.2
POLICY OPTIMIZATION FOR DIFFUSION AND FLOW POLICIES
RL for diffusion and flow policies has been studied primarily in generic continuous-control settings.
One class of methods treats the denoising or flow-generation process itself as a sequential policy
and directly optimizes the generative trajectory with reinforcement learning Ren et al. (2025); Hu
et al. (2025); another class avoids direct optimization of the full iterative generation process and in-
stead transfers value information through one-step surrogate actors, value-weighted behavior learn-
ing, or regularized policy extraction Park et al. (2025); Tiofack et al. (2025); Liang et al. (2026).
Most of these approaches are developed for conventional diffusion/flow control policies rather than
large-scale VLAs with pretrained vision-language backbones. OGPO Patil et al. (2026) combines
an off-policy critic for reusing expensive environment transitions with freshly sampled trajectories
from the current generative policy, and our separation between offline value learning and on-policy
generative optimization is inspired by this principle. OGPO, however, focuses on online fine-tuning
of relatively lightweight generative control policies, where the replay buffer is continually refreshed
through environment interaction, whereas we study offline post-training of large pretrained VLAs
and explicitly address how an offline-trained critic can reliably evaluate actions generated by the
evolving policy. Finally, recent work has shown that single-step policy optimization can substan-
tially reduce the cost of reinforcement learning for large diffusion models He et al. (2026); we adopt
this idea to make on-policy generative optimization practical for large flow-based VLAs.

METHOD
OVOGO consists of two stages. First, we train a Return-Anchored Ranking Critic (RARC) from
logged robot experience using return grounded value learning and local ranking supervision, where
synthetic actions provide only relative ordering constraints and never receive Bellman targets.
RARC is then frozen and used to evaluate fresh action candidates sampled from the current flow
policy at replay observations. Their relative critic values are converted into optimization signals for
selected transition updates of the action expert, without requiring gradients through the critic.
4.1
RETURN-ANCHORED RANKING CRITIC
At a replay observation, the actor may generate action chunks that were never executed by the robot.
Their environment outcomes are therefore unavailable, yet their critic scores determine the direction
of the actor update. RARC separates two sources of supervision. Executed replay transitions es-
tablish the long-horizon return scale, while ranking perturbations provide local ordering constraints
without receiving artificial return targets.

This removes the common value offset at the current observation while preserving the relative value
differences among candidates. The categorical state value head is used only for critic learning and
does not serve as the actor baseline. We then conservatively aggregate the centered scores across
Q heads. A candidate receives a nonzero advantage only when all heads agree on the sign of its
centered score. When all scores are positive, we use the smallest positive score. When all scores
are negative, we use the negative score closest to zero. A candidate receives zero advantage when
the heads disagree on the improvement direction (Patil et al., 2026). The resulting Ab,i is detached
and used as a scalar signal for policy optimization. This conservative aggregation updates the policy
only along relative improvement directions consistently supported by the critic ensemble, using
the most conservative magnitude among the agreeing estimates. RARC therefore interfaces with
the generative policy only through scalar value feedback, and policy optimization does not require
gradients through the critic.
Selected transition flow optimization.
RARC evaluates a candidate after its complete action
chunk has been generated. Updating the policy through an entire multistep flow trajectory, how-
ever, introduces substantial computational overhead. We therefore follow the selected transition
optimization of Flash-GRPO (He et al., 2026).
For each observation sb, we first sample a common generation transition for the entire candidate
group, kb ∼Uniform{1, . . . , K}, where transition kb maps x[kb−1] to x[kb]. The selected transition
uses a stochastic SDE update, while the remaining generation transitions follow deterministic ODE
dynamics. Each candidate is generated to completion and receives its advantage Ab,i from RARC.
Policy gradients are computed only through the transition at kb. All candidates within the same
group share this selected transition, so their relative comparison is not confounded by differences
across flow timesteps.
Let ρb,i denote the dimension-normalized likelihood-ratio surrogate between the current policy and
the rollout policy at the selected transition. Different selected timesteps induce different gradient
scales through the SDE discretization. Following Flash-GRPO, we apply temporal gradient rectifi-
cation with w(kb) = 1/λ(kb). The clipped update is
Lpolicy = −Eb,i [w(kb) min (ρb,iAb,i, clip (ρb,i, 1 −ε, 1 + ε) Ab,i)] .

objective preserves behaviors in the replay dataset that already achieve task success.

5
EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of OVOGO on flow-based VLA post-training. In
particular, we aim to answer the following research questions: (1) Compared with existing offline
adaptation and value-guided methods, how effectively can OVOGO improve a pretrained VLA from
the same robot experience? (2) Can RARC provide useful value signals for freshly generated actions,
and does generative policy optimization provide additional gains over inference-time selection or
guidance? (3) What are the contributions of return grounded value learning, local ranking, and the
guarded ranking design to policy improvement?
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Benchmarks and data.
We evaluate on RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025b), DexJoCo (Wang et al.,
2026a), and LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), as well as a real-robot dexterous manipulation task. For
each simulation task, the offline dataset contains 100 policy rollout episodes and 50 demonstration
episodes. Within each simulation benchmark, the actor and critic are trained jointly across all tasks
using one shared model for each. For RoboTwin, DexJoCo, and LIBERO, we use the publicly
released clean50 SFT, official multi-task π0.5, and RLinf LIBERO SFT checkpoints, respectively,
both for policy initialization and initial rollout collection. All methods within each benchmark
use the same task split, observation format, language instructions, action normalization, execution
horizon, and evaluation protocol.
Baselines.
We compare OVOGO with five baselines. Success BC adapts the action expert by be-
havior cloning successful trajectories from the replay dataset. RECAP is the value based policy
adaptation method introduced with π∗
0.6 (Intelligence et al., 2025a). We use the Evo-RL imple-
mentation (Contributors, 2026) and fully fine-tune both the VLM backbone and the action expert.
Best-of-N samples N action chunks from the policy and executes the candidate with the highest
RARC score. We set N = 8, matching the candidate budget used by OVOGO, and use the same
critic aggregation without updating the policy. QGF uses critic gradients to guide the flow genera-
tion process at inference time (Zhou et al., 2026). It starts from the same VLA and uses the offline
trained critic without optimizing the actor from group relative candidate scores. LWD (Wang et al.,
2026b) learns a distributional implicit value function and uses critic action gradients to optimize the
flow policy. We reproduce LWD following its reported algorithm and hyperparameter settings.
Training protocol.
Our primary experiments follow the standard offline setting. All methods start
from the same initial VLA within each benchmark and use the same fixed offline dataset without ad-
ditional environment interaction during optimization. For OVOGO, the pretrained VLM backbone is
frozen and only the action expert is optimized. We report the mean and standard deviation of success
rates over three seeds for the repeated simulation experiments. We additionally evaluate RECAP and
OVOGO in an iterative offline setting on RoboTwin. At round k, policy optimization is performed
on a fixed accumulated dataset D(k). The updated policy then collects 100 rollout episodes per task,
which are added to the dataset for the following round. The demonstration episodes remain avail-

5.4
ANALYSIS OF RARC
Contribution of return grounded value learning.
We examine the complementary roles of return
grounded value learning and local ranking in RARC. All variants use the same fixed offline dataset
and actor optimization procedure. As shown in Table 3, the return-grounded critic achieves 60.97 ±
1.38% success on RoboTwin, while using ranking supervision alone without return grounded value
learning reduces the success rate to 55.90±1.54%. Combining the two objectives in RARC improves
the success rate to 68.17±1.19%. These results suggest that ranking supervision alone is insufficient
for effective policy improvement, while return grounded value learning provides a foundation on
which local ranking can enhance action discrimination. A similar pattern is observed on LIBERO,
where guarded ranking alone achieves 53.26 ± 4.98% and the full RARC reaches 78.36 ± 2.00%.
Effect of temperature and gap cutoff.
Ranking with τR = 1 and no cutoff achieves 63.17 ±
0.76% success on RoboTwin. Lowering the temperature to τR = 0.1 without the cutoff increases the
success rate to 66.50 ± 0.56%. Introducing the gap cutoff at the same temperature further improves
the success rate to 68.17 ± 1.19%. These results show that both components of guarded ranking
contribute to policy improvement, with the larger gain coming from the reduced temperature. The
full guarded ranking design improves over ranking with τR = 1 and no cutoff by 5.00 and 7.31
percentage points on RoboTwin and LIBERO, respectively. On LIBERO, adding the cutoff at τR =
0.1 increases the mean success rate from 76.99 ± 1.04% to 78.36 ± 2.00%.
Validation of the local ranking prior.
We examine the local ranking prior by applying perturba-
tions at different scales to the action chunks generated by the initial policy on RoboTwin. Using the
same evaluation initial states and seeds, mild perturbations with δ = 0.04 and stronger perturbations
with 2δ = 0.08 reduce the success rate from 60.7% to 56.13 ± 0.80% and 49.40 ± 6.24%, respec-
tively. The decrease in success as the perturbation magnitude increases supports the local ranking
prior used in RARC.
Critic reliability after policy optimization.
Since RARC remains frozen during policy optimiza-
tion, its ability to evaluate actions generated by the evolving policy is important. On RoboTwin,
Best-of-N using RARC improves the initial policy from 60.7% to 64.23 ± 1.98%. Applying the
same frozen critic to candidates generated by the final OVOGO actors further improves their suc-
cess rate from 68.17±1.19% to 70.43±0.65%. These results provide evidence that RARC remains
useful for action selection after policy optimization.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we present OVOGO, an offline reinforcement learning framework for improving flow-
based vision-language-action models from collected robot experience. OVOGO learns a return-
grounded ranking critic and uses its value signals to optimize fresh actions generated by the current
flow policy. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0, DexJoCo, and LIBERO, as well as a real-robot dex-
terous manipulation task, show that OVOGO consistently improves the initial VLA and achieves
stronger performance than existing offline adaptation and value-guided methods. More broadly,
OVOGO shows how expensive physical experience can be repeatedly reused together with inexpen-
sive generative sampling, providing a scalable path toward experience-driven post-training of large
robot policies.

A.3
RARC CRITIC ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING
RARC uses frozen pretrained visual and language encoders together with a lightweight trainable
critic. The language backbone is Gemma 3 270M, and the vision backbone is SigLIP2-So400M
with 224 × 224 visual inputs. The pretrained Gemma 3 and SigLIP2 parameters remain frozen
during critic optimization; only the critic-specific projection layers and prediction heads are trained.
The trainable critic core and encoder projection contain approximately 7.78M parameters.
For RoboTwin, the critic receives three visual observations corresponding to the base camera and the
two wrist cameras, together with the language instruction and robot proprioceptive state. The same
critic design is used for DexJoCo, LIBERO, and the real-robot experiments with their respective
observation and action interfaces.
RARC contains M = 5 ensemble members. Each member contains two scalar action-value heads
and one categorical state-value head, yielding ten raw scalar Q estimates in total. The categorical
value head uses C = 201 atoms on the fixed support [−0.1, 1.1]. We retain the ten Q estimates
separately when evaluating policy-generated candidates.
EMA target critic.
For training stability, we maintain an exponential-moving-average copy of the
complete critic, including the trainable encoder projection, scalar Q heads, and categorical value
heads. The target Q predictions are used to construct the distributional value supervision, while the
categorical value distribution used for TD bootstrapping is evaluated with the EMA target critic. The
target critic is updated after every critic optimization step with coefficient ηtgt = 0.005.
The return-grounded distributional value objective, entropy-adaptive quantile extraction, scalar Q
objective, and guarded local ranking objective are defined in Section 4.1; we do not repeat those
formulations here. Their implementation-specific numerical settings are summarized in Table 5.

ual tasks and six single-arm tasks. The multi-task policy predicts action chunks of length H = 30.
Single-arm and bimanual tasks share a unified multi-task policy interface using the benchmark-
specific state and action representations.
We initialize from the official multi-task π0.5 checkpoint.2 The released checkpoint is provided in
JAX/Orbax format. We convert the checkpoint to PyTorch before training, including the vision-
language and action-expert parameters and the corresponding normalization statistics. The con-
verted model is used consistently for all DexJoCo comparisons.
LIBERO.
We evaluate on 40 tasks from LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), covering four task suites:
LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long (LIBERO-10), with ten tasks
in each suite. The policy predicts a 10-step action chunk and executes the first five actions before
the next policy call.
We initialize the policy from the publicly released RLinf π0.5 LIBERO SFT checkpoint.3 The same
checkpoint is used for all LIBERO comparisons and for collecting the initial policy rollout episodes.
Except for benchmark-specific observation and action interfaces, we use the same OVOGO training
configuration across the three simulation benchmarks.

B.4
BASELINE CONFIGURATIONS
All policy-training baselines start from the same pretrained policy and use the same initial offline
dataset as OVOGO. For the policy-training methods in simulation, policy optimization runs for
10,000 steps, and we evaluate the best saved checkpoint.
Success BC.
Success BC performs supervised adaptation using successful trajectories from the
same offline replay dataset. We optimize the native flow-matching objective of π0.5 while keeping
the pretrained vision-language backbone frozen and updating the action expert. Failed replay tra-
jectories do not contribute to the Success BC objective. We use a learning rate of 1 × 10−5 and a
global batch size of 256.
RECAP.
We implement RECAP (Intelligence et al., 2025a) using the Evo-RL implementa-
tion (Contributors, 2026). RECAP starts from the same pretrained policy and the same initial replay
dataset as OVOGO. We follow the Evo-RL training configuration for advantage-conditioned policy
adaptation and fully fine-tune both the vision-language backbone and the action expert. We use a
learning rate of 2 × 10−5 and a batch size of 64. For comparable value representations, we use the
same Gemma 3 270M language encoder and SigLIP2-So400M vision encoder with 224×224 visual
inputs as in RARC.
19


Best-of-N.
Best-of-N is an inference-time selection baseline and does not update the policy pa-
rameters. At each observation, the policy generates N = 8 candidate action chunks. The candidates
are evaluated using the same frozen RARC used by OVOGO, and the candidate with the highest
aggregated critic score is executed. Using the same candidate budget as OVOGO isolates the benefit
of policy optimization from the benefit of critic-based candidate selection.
QGF.
QGF is an inference-time value-guidance baseline. We use the same pretrained policy and
the same RARC critic as OVOGO. During flow generation, action gradients of the critic are used to
steer the generation trajectory toward higher-value actions, while the actor parameters themselves
remain unchanged. This comparison separates inference-time value guidance from parameter-space
policy optimization.
LWD.
LWD combines distributional implicit value learning with critic-guided optimization of
the flow policy. Unlike OVOGO, which uses scalar critic values to construct group-relative policy
optimization signals, LWD uses critic action gradients to construct policy improvement targets. As
the official training code was unavailable, we reproduce LWD following the algorithmic details and
hyperparameter settings reported in the original paper. LWD uses the same initial policy and offline
replay dataset as OVOGO within each benchmark.
B.5
OFFLINE DATA AND TRAINING PROTOCOL
We distinguish the standard offline setting used for the main comparison from the iterative offline
experiment conducted on RoboTwin.
Standard offline setting.
For the main comparison, all methods are trained from the same fixed
offline dataset and receive no additional environment interactions during optimization.
RoboTwin contains ten tasks. For each task, the initial replay dataset contains 100 policy rollout
episodes and 50 demonstration episodes. The complete initial RoboTwin dataset therefore contains
1000 policy rollout episodes and 500 demonstration episodes.
DexJoCo contains eleven tasks. Each task likewise contains 100 policy rollout episodes and 50
demonstration episodes, resulting in 1100 policy rollout episodes and 550 demonstration episodes
in total.
LIBERO contains 40 tasks across four task suites. Each task contains 100 policy rollout episodes
and 50 demonstration episodes, resulting in 4000 policy rollout episodes and 2000 demonstration
episodes in total.
The initial rollout episodes are collected using the corresponding pretrained policies described in
Section B.1. Their initial task-macro success rates are 60.7%, 27.5%, and 71.40% on RoboTwin,
DexJoCo, and LIBERO, respectively.
Within the standard offline setting, the replay dataset remains fixed throughout critic learning and
actor optimization. Fresh candidate actions generated by the flow policy are used only to construct
policy-improvement signals. They are not treated as new environment transitions, are not assigned
synthetic rewards, and are not inserted into the replay dataset.
Iterative offline setting on RoboTwin.
We additionally evaluate OVOGO and RECAP under an
iterative offline protocol on RoboTwin. Let D(k) denote the replay dataset available in round k.
Critic and actor optimization within that round use only the fixed dataset D(k).
After completing the offline optimization stage, the updated policy is deployed to collect 100 new
rollout episodes for each of the ten RoboTwin tasks, giving 1000 new rollout episodes per round. We
denote this newly collected experience by E(k+1) and construct the replay dataset for the following
round as
D(k+1) = D(k) ∪E(k+1).
(31)
The original demonstration trajectories remain available throughout all rounds.

This protocol preserves the offline nature of each optimization round: new environment experience
is collected only between rounds, whereas policy-generated candidates sampled during optimization
never enter the replay dataset.

B.7
REAL-ROBOT EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Hardware and observations.
Our real-robot platform consists of a Universal Robots UR5e arm
with six revolute joints and a five-finger INSPIRE Robots RH56DFX-series dexterous hand with six
actuator channels. A fixed external Orbbec Femto Bolt camera observes the workspace, and a wrist-
mounted Intel RealSense L515 provides a close-up view. The policy receives RGB images from
both cameras, resized to 224 × 224 pixels, together with the language instruction and the current
robot state.
State and action representation.
The proprioceptive state and the executed action each have 12
dimensions. The state contains the measured joint and actuator positions, while the action specifies
absolute position targets in the same channel order. The first six dimensions correspond to the
UR5e arm joint positions in radians. The remaining six dimensions correspond to the little finger,
ring finger, middle finger, index finger, thumb flexion, and thumb rotation of the dexterous hand,
represented in raw actuator counts.
At each policy call, π0.5 predicts an action chunk of length H = 32. The internal flow representation
has dimension 32 × 32, and the first 12 channels at each step form the 32 × 12 action chunk passed
to the robot controller. The policy executes 32 actions before the next policy call under the default
execution setting.
Initial policy and rollout collection.
We collect 50 demonstrations of the block-stacking task
and use them to fine-tune π0.5 for 30,000 optimizer steps with a global batch size of 256. We use
AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, ϵ = 10−8, and weight decay of 10−10. The gradient norm is
21


clipped to 1.0. The learning rate warms up linearly for 1,000 steps to 5 × 10−5 and follows a cosine
decay schedule with a final learning rate of 5 × 10−6.
The checkpoint at step 30,000 serves as the initial policy for all real-robot comparisons. This policy
is also used to collect 100 additional rollout episodes for offline post-training.
Evaluation.
Each method is evaluated on 20 real-robot trials. We report the number of successful
trials and the corresponding empirical success rate in Table 2. The demonstration-trained initial
policy and the subsequent SFT baseline are reported separately.
B.8
COMPUTE PLATFORM
All experiments are implemented in PyTorch. We use four NVIDIA A100 GPUs for distributed
OVOGO critic and actor training.
During critic optimization, the Gemma 3 270M language backbone and the SigLIP2-So400M vision
backbone with 224 × 224 inputs remain frozen. Only the critic-specific projection and prediction
modules are updated. During actor optimization, RARC and the pretrained vision-language back-
bone remain frozen, and only the π0.5 action expert is optimized.
