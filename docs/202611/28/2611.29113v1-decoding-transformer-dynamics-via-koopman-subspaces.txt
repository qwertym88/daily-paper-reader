Subhradeep Kayal

Abstract
We apply Koopman operator theory and Dy-
namic Mode Decomposition (DMD) to char-
acterise how large language models (LLMs)
process different input topics.
Treating the
layer-to-layer evolution of hidden states as a
dynamical system, we fit a linear Koopman
operator and take the subspace spanned by its
dominant modes as a per-topic spectral finger-
print. Four findings emerge. First, the depth
map is near-linear: a single linear operator pre-
dicts each next-layer state to within ≈18% rel-
ative error (interior variance explained ≈0.99).
Second, over the 57 MMLU subjects the fin-
gerprint is topic-discriminative, matching the
MMLU taxonomy at 0.72 nearest-neighbour
purity and each subject’s semantic neighbour-
hood at 0.75 any-hit@3.
Third, it is a re-
producible signature (split-half self-retrieval
≈0.97). Finally, these findings are consistent
across seven base models (Qwen2.5 0.5-7B,
Llama-3 1-8B), with a suggestive trend of align-
ment rising with MMLU accuracy. The spec-
tral fingerprint thus provides a probe of how
transformers process different topics across
depth. Code is available at https://github.
com/DeepK/koopman-llm-eacl27.

Introduction
Large language models process all inputs through
the same fixed sequence of transformer layers, re-
gardless of input complexity or domain. While
prior work has studied how much and how fast each
layer changes a representation (via cosine similar-
ity between consecutive hidden states (Men et al.,
2025), angular distance (Gromov et al., 2025), or
velocity and acceleration profiles (Fernando and
Guitchounts, 2025)), these measures do not capture
how the representation is transformed: which direc-
tions it moves along, and whether features are am-
plified, suppressed, or mixed together. They there-
*This work was done outside of Amazon.
fore cannot tell whether two inputs are transformed
in the same way or only by the same amount.
We propose a different lens: the hidden states a
prompt produces across layers form the trajectory
of a dynamical system, and Koopman operator
theory gives its spectral structure. The Koopman
operator linearises nonlinear dynamics by acting on
observable functions, and its eigenvalues describe
the fundamental modes of the system. Applied
to an LLM, the corresponding modes are feature
directions in hidden-state space along which repre-
sentations are transformed across depth.
Our central object is the spectral fingerprint:
the subspace spanned by a topic’s dominant Koop-
man modes, an embedding-like signature of how
the model processes that topic. We obtain it from
linear DMD, which we show captures the one-step
layer-to-layer dynamics well, suggesting that the
bulk of the LLM’s layers are nearly linear. Compar-
ing these mode subspaces across topics defines a
dynamics space in which two topics are “close” if
the model transforms them along similar directions,
regardless of whether they are semantically related.
This lets us ask questions that magnitude measures
cannot: which topics a model processes alike, in-
cluding some that are not semantically close (Sec-
tion 5.3); whether a topic has a stable processing
signature at all (Section 5.5); and where in depth a
linear description holds and where it breaks down
(Section 5.1).

Related Work
2.1
Transformers as Depth-Wise Dynamical
Systems
Viewing deep networks as discretised ordinary dif-
ferential equations (ODEs) has a rich foundation (E,
2017; Chen et al., 2018); for Transformers, this lens
treats the residual stream’s layer-wise updates as
a trajectory in representation space. Recently, Fer-
nando and Guitchounts (2025) formalise the resid-
ual stream as a spatial dynamical system, report-
ing systematic velocity and acceleration profiles
across depth. These measures describe how much
and how fast representations change; we instead
fit an explicit linear operator to each topic’s tran-
sitions and use its dominant modes to identify the
directions of change. A complementary line finds
consecutive layers near-linearly related and partly
redundant: adjacent representations align almost
perfectly under a linear map (Razzhigaev et al.,
2024), and many layers can be removed with little
loss (Gromov et al., 2025; Men et al., 2025), which
has motivated architectures that allocate depth con-
ditionally per token (Elhoushi et al., 2024; Raposo
et al., 2024). These results motivate treating each
layer-to-layer step as approximately linear, which
our fingerprint exploits.
2.2
Dynamic Mode Decomposition and
Koopman Theory in language models
DMD and Koopman theory are increasingly used
to study foundation models, though along dimen-
sions orthogonal to ours. One line models physical
or fluid dynamics with attention-based architec-
tures (Nagdi et al., 2026). Closer to text, Akrout
(2023) applies DMD to extract semantic modes
from embeddings across sequence length, detect-
ing structural drift and hallucination, and Kayal
and Tsatsaronis (2019) apply higher-order DMD
across word sequences to build spectral sentence
embeddings. Most related, Kim et al. (2026) profile
the Koopman spectrum of a transformer’s residual
dynamics at initialisation to predict training insta-
bility, treating depth-wise evolution as a dynamical
system whose spectrum is diagnostic.
The first two works apply spectral analysis
along the sequence dimension (token-to-token or
sentence-to-sentence). Like Kim et al. (2026), we
apply DMD along the depth dimension (layer-to-
layer) within a single forward pass; they fit a sepa-
rate operator per layer as a training-stability diag-
nostic, whereas we fit one operator across all layers
of a trained model, per input topic, so that topics
can be compared in a common dynamics space.
2.3
Depth-Wise Spectral Analysis and Model
Compression
Depth-wise DMD has emerged in computer vision
for architectural efficiency: recent work applies
it to Vision Transformers (ViTs) to test whether
sequential non-linear blocks can be approximated
by a low-rank linear operator for block pruning and
compression (Aswani and Jabari, 2026). We use
the same depth-wise decomposition for a different
purpose. That work fits the operator to replace
blocks of layers and so compress the model; we fit
it per topic and compare the resulting fingerprints.
2.4
Mechanistic and Structural
Interpretability
Our fingerprints relate to work mapping how in-
ternal representations evolve across layers. Struc-
tural probes such as the Logit Lens (nostalgebraist,
2020) and Tuned Lens (Belrose et al., 2025) decode
hidden states by projecting them into vocabulary
logit space, and representation engineering (Zou
et al., 2025) isolates directional vectors for high-
level concepts. Whereas these identify what a layer
contains, typically per concept, our approach mod-


els how representations are transformed between
layers and summarises this for a whole topic.

Methodology
3.1
Koopman Operator Theory
Consider a nonlinear dynamical system xt+1 =
F(xt) where xt ∈RD. The Koopman opera-
tor K (Koopman, 1931) is an infinite-dimensional
linear operator that acts on observable functions
g : RD →C:
(Kg)(x) = g(F(x))
(1)
K is linear even when F is nonlinear; linearity is
achieved by lifting into the (infinite-dimensional)
space of observables. Its spectral decomposition
underpins modern operator-theoretic analysis of
dynamical systems (Mezi´c, 2005; Brunton et al.,
2022). The Koopman eigenvalues λj and eigen-
functions φj satisfy:
Kφj = λjφj
(2)
We interpret the transformer’s layer-to-layer map
as F, with hidden states hl ∈RD as the state at
“time” (depth) l. Each eigenfunction is paired with a
Koopman mode ϕj, an eigenvector in hidden-state
space that identifies a coherent feature direction the
dynamics act on.
Relation to the layer Jacobian.
A more familiar
linearisation of a layer is its Jacobian Jl(h) =
∂Fl/∂h, the first-order Taylor approximation of
layer l around a single hidden state h. It is accurate
near that state but changes from input to input, so it
describes the layer at one point. The DMD operator
(Section 3.2) is instead the least-squares best linear
map over all of a topic’s layer transitions, across
every prompt and depth. Its modes are therefore the
directions along which the model transforms that
topic on average, which makes them a topic-level
descriptor where a pointwise Jacobian is not.

Experiments
4.1
Setup
Models.
We evaluate seven base models across
two axes (results in Section 5.6):
• Scale: Qwen2.5-0.5B / 1.5B / 3B / 7B
• Family:
Qwen2.5, Llama-3.2 (1B, 3B),
Llama-3.1-8B
Data.
We use MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)
with its 57 subjects spanning STEM, humanities,
social sciences, and other categories. We extract
100 prompts per subject from the test split: we
cap at 100 because the smallest subject’s test set
contains only 100 examples, so 100 is the largest
sample size available uniformly across all 57 sub-
jects.
Compute.
All experiments run on a single CPU
machine (32-core Intel Xeon, 64 GB RAM).
4.2
Research Questions
We organise the study around the following re-
search questions.
• RQ1. Can a Koopman/DMD operator capture
the layer-to-layer dynamics of a transformer?
– RQ1a. Is a linear operator sufficient?
• RQ2. Does the mode-subspace fingerprint
encode meaningful semantic structure?
– RQ2a. How many modes does a stable
fingerprint need?
– RQ2b. Is the resulting fingerprint a reli-
able, reproducible signature?
• RQ3. Is the fingerprint’s quality consistent
across model families and scales? That is, is
a reproducible, category- and semantically-
aligned fingerprint present in every model
tested, or only in a particular family or size?
5
Results and Analysis
5.1
How Well Does a Koopman Operator
Capture the Dynamics? (RQ1)
We quantify how well the fitted operator captures
the layer-to-layer dynamics with a mean per-step
prediction error: each layer transition is predicted
from the true previous state and the relative L2
error is averaged over depth, so errors do not com-
pound. This isolates the one-step dynamics, the
property relevant to a meaningful eigenvalue spec-
trum. Because the operator is fitted jointly to every
transition of every prompt, a low error means that
one shared linear map fits the whole distribution
of a topic’s layer transitions. This is a stronger
statement than linearity near a single state (the Ja-
cobian view of Section 3.1). On Qwen2.5-3B (36
layers, D=2048, 100 prompts per subject), linear
DMD (rank 100) applied to all 57 MMLU sub-
jects attains a mean per-step error of 0.184 (range
0.121-0.207, median 0.184), a per-step variance
explained (R2 = 1 −err2) of 0.91 averaged over
layers (range 0.87-0.94).
Interpreting the error.
A per-step error of 0.184
means that, given the true hidden state at a layer,
the operator predicts the next layer’s state to within
≈18% relative L2 error on average. Because con-
secutive residual-stream states are similar (hl+1 =
hl + δ), part of this accuracy reflects representa-
tional continuity; we quantify this with an iden-
tity baseline that predicts ˆhl+1 = hl.
On the
five subjects used for the decomposer comparison
(Section 5.2), where linear DMD’s error is 0.190
(vs. 0.184 over all 57), the identity baseline yields
0.245; linear DMD improves on it by ≈22% (con-
sistent across topics, 20-25%), which is transfor-
mation beyond continuity.

Category alignment.
We quantify category co-
herence with 1-NN purity. For each subject we
compute the Grassmann distance between its top-
50 mode subspace and that of every other subject,
identify its single nearest neighbour, and check
whether that neighbour belongs to the same cate-
gory; the 1-NN purity is the fraction of subjects for
which it does (chance ≈0.24, given the category
sizes). The fingerprint reaches a 1-NN purity of
0.72: a subject’s nearest neighbour in dynamics
space usually belongs to the same MMLU category.
A two-dimensional t-SNE of the fingerprint dis-
tances (Figure 2) makes this visible: same-category
subjects occupy distinct but overlapping regions.
Dynamics space vs. semantic space.
Category
labels are coarse, so we also compare the fin-
gerprint against a continuous semantic reference:
does similarity in dynamics space (fingerprints)
match similarity in semantic space? We embed
each subject’s label text (underscores replaced
by spaces, e.g. “high school european history”)
with a sentence-transformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2;
Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) and take cosine dis-
tance, a semantic representation independent of the
LLMs under study. Taking each subject’s k nearest
semantic neighbours as a reference set, we ask how
many of its k nearest fingerprint neighbours fall in
that set (Table 4): the top-1 agreement (whether
the single nearest fingerprint neighbour is also the
nearest semantic neighbour) and the any-hit@3 rate
(the fraction of subjects for which at least one of the
three nearest fingerprint neighbours is also among
the three nearest semantic neighbours).

Interpretation.
The two spaces agree where a
subject sits in a tight, hierarchical family (biology,
chemistry, medicine, logic), which the model both
represents and processes similarly. Among the di-
vergences, one pattern stands out: semantically
distinct quantitative/algorithmic subjects (college
computer science, econometrics, computer secu-
rity) all have machine learning among their nearest
dynamics neighbours, and machine learning’s own
nearest dynamics neighbours are exactly these sub-
jects. One reading is that the model applies a shared
quantitative-reasoning computation to them regard-
less of domain. High school geography similarly
groups with social and political subjects (security
studies, public relations, sociology) rather than the
history subjects it is closest to semantically. The
fingerprint thus tracks surface semantics closely
but, where it departs, groups subjects by how the
model computes over them rather than by lexical
similarity.

5.5
Is the Resulting Fingerprint a Reliable,
Reproducible Signature? (RQ2b)
A fingerprint is only useful if it reflects a sta-
ble property of a topic rather than the particular
prompts used to compute it. We test this with a
split-half reproducibility check on Qwen2.5-3B.
For each subject we randomly partition its 100
prompts into two disjoint halves and fit an inde-
pendent fingerprint from each. We then perform
self-retrieval: for each half-A fingerprint we find its
nearest neighbour among all 57 half-B fingerprints
(by Grassmann distance) and ask whether it is the
same subject. We report the split-half self-retrieval
rate (the fraction of subjects recovered as their own
nearest neighbour; chance ≈1/57 ≈0.02) and the
mean self-retrieval rank (1 = perfect).
On Qwen2.5-3B (top-50 subspace), split-half
self-retrieval is 0.97 with a mean self-rank of 1.0,
against a chance level of 0.02: for almost every
subject, the nearest half-B fingerprint to its half-A
fingerprint is its own, and in the few misses the
correct subject ranks close to the top. A fingerprint
estimated from 50 prompts is therefore already spe-
cific to its topic, out of 57 candidates. Section 5.6
shows the same for all seven models.

6
Conclusion
We fitted a linear Koopman operator to the layer-
to-layer evolution of hidden states in seven trans-
former language models and used its dominant
mode subspace as a per-topic spectral fingerprint.
The depth map was found to be near-linear in its
interior, with its nonlinearity concentrated at the
input and output layers. The resulting fingerprint is
topic-discriminative: aligned with both the MMLU
taxonomy and semantic neighbourhoods, while
grouping quantitative subjects across categories.
The spectral fingerprint can, therefore, be seen as a
probe of the structure beneath a model’s behaviour,
and a starting point for future interventions. A natu-
ral extension is to fit separate operators to blocks of
layers, which could separate the roles of different
stages of the network, such as the nonlinear input
and output stages and the near-linear interior we
observe.
Limitations
Untested model classes.
Our cross-model evi-
dence covers seven dense, decoder-only base mod-
els from two families, up to 8B parameters. We
leave models with substantially different attention
or normalisation designs, mixture-of-experts mod-
els, instruction-tuned or RLHF-trained variants,
and larger models as future work.
Design choices and causality.
The operator rank
(100) and the fingerprint dimension (K=50) are
empirical choices; K was selected from a sweep on
one model and then applied to all, rather than tuned
per model. Our evidence is correlational through-
out: we show that fingerprint geometry aligns with
taxonomic and semantic structure, but we do not
intervene on the identified directions, so we cannot
establish that the model causally relies on them.
All experiments use English; whether the results
transfer to other languages is an open question.
Static baselines.
We do not compare the finger-
print against static topic representations that ignore
the layer-to-layer pairing, such as the mean hidden
state of a topic or the principal subspace of its hid-
den states. Such representations may also recover
the taxonomic and semantic alignment we report.
The dynamic analysis is, however, required for the
one-step near-linearity and interior-boundary find-
ings (Section 5.1), which static representations can-
not express.
