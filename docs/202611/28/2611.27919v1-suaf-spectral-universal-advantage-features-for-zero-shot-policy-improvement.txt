Abstract
Reward-independent representations enable reinforcement learning agents
to reuse knowledge across tasks, but limited representation capacity raises
three fundamental questions: what statistical dependence should these
representations capture, which components of that dependence should be
retained for policy improvement, and how does discarding the remaining
components affect decision-making? We address these questions through
canonical dependence analysis. For a reference policy, we construct an
action-centered, positive-lag canonical dependence matrix (CDM) that
isolates the additional predictive contribution of the current action beyond
the current state. Its singular decomposition yields Spectral Universal Ad-
vantage Features (SUAF), paired features that recover delayed advantage
through linear reward projections. We show that the spectrum quanti-
fies mutual information between current action and future state–action
visitation conditioned on current state and determines reward-specific
approximation errors. Retaining the leading modes gives optimal rank-
constrained approximations under both isotropic average-case and reward-
uniform minimax criteria. Separating the immediate reward contrast also
avoids the spectral floor arising from direct compression of the full ad-
vantage operator. To learn SUAF from reward-free data, we develop an
action-centered Soft-HGR objective with bidirectional temporal-difference
estimation. We further connect spectral approximation errors to sufficient
conditions for policy improvement. Finite-state experiments validate the

Introduction
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and embodied artificial intel-
ligence (AI) have triggered a growing ambition to build general-purpose agents
that operate in open-ended environments Xi et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2025).
Unlike traditional task-specific models, these agents are expected to handle a
diverse array of goals, costs, and preferences within the same environment, often
specified on the fly by human instructions or changing task contexts. Although
these changes alter the reward function, knowledge of how actions influence fu-
ture outcomes remains reusable. Reward-independent representations seek to
capture this knowledge and support new tasks without learning a separate critic
from scratch.
Among reward-independent representations, successor features (SFs) pro-
vide a foundational framework for such reuse by separating reward weights
from the discounted accumulation of features Dayan (1993); Barreto et al.
(2017). Building on this, more recent successor-measure (SM) representations
seek to encode behavior across policies using reward-free interaction data Blier
et al. (2021); Touati & Ollivier (2021); Touati et al. (2023). However, a critical
challenge arises: which features should be learned? To address this problem,
Ollivier (2025) connects optimal features to the spectral structure of the full
advantage operator through expected downstream performance under strong
behavior-cloning regularization. Despite these theoretical advances, limited rep-
resentation capacity still raises three fundamental questions: what statistical
dependence should these representations capture, which components of that de-
pendence should be retained for policy improvement, and how does discarding
the remaining components affect decision-making?
To address these problems, we introduce Spectral Universal Advantage
Features (SUAF), a reward-independent representation of delayed advantage
with respect to the corresponding behavior policy. Our starting point is that
future outcomes depend on both state and action, while advantage measures the
action’s contribution relative to a reference policy. We therefore isolate the de-
pendence that remains after subtracting the policy-averaged future state–action
visitation. Building on canonical dependence analysis Huang et al. (2024), we
construct an action-centered, positive-lag canonical dependence matrix (CDM)
between current state–action pair and future visitation, where action center-
ing is conducted by subtracting the policy-averaged visitation and positive lag
excludes the current time step from the visitation count. The singular value
decomposition of this matrix yields paired features of current and future state–
action pairs, which defines the proposed SUAF. The full expansion of SUAF
recovers the advantage function by projecting a downstream reward onto the
future features. We further show that the immediate reward contrast imposes a
2


strictly positive lower bound on the worst-case error of directly approximating
the full advantage operator at low rank. We call this lower bound the spectral
floor. When the immediate reward contrast can be evaluated directly, SUAF
avoids this floor by keeping it explicit and compressing only the delayed com-
ponent. For this delayed component, SUAF gives an optimal rank-constrained
approximation under both average-case and worst-case reward objectives. Un-
der weak dependence, we further relate the sum of squared singular values to
the conditional mutual information between the current action and the future
state–action pair given the current state.
We then ask how discarding the
remaining modes affects decision-making and derive an exact approximation-
error expression via projections onto discarded modes, distinguishing the total
dependence carried by the discarded modes from their contribution to the ad-
vantages for a given reward, and obtain a sufficient condition under which the
predicted policy-improvement gain exceeds the approximation loss.
We fur-
ther bound local representation reuse under policy changes within trust region.
To learn SUAF without constructing the CDM, we develop an action-centered
Soft-HGR objective Wang et al. (2019) with bidirectional temporal-difference
estimation, using forward and backward predictors from reward-free transitions.
The learned representation supports advantage readout and policy extraction.
Experiments examine the spectral floor, truncation errors, and feature-learning
accuracy, with zero-shot evaluations on discrete and continuous-control bench-
marks.
In summary, our contributions are threefold:
1. We introduce SUAF through an action-centered positive-lag CDM, con-
necting delayed advantage to the dependence between current actions and
future outcomes. We identify a spectral floor in the full advantage opera-
tor that motivates retaining the immediate reward contrast explicitly and
compressing only the delayed component.
2. We establish average-case and reward-uniform minimax optimality for
spectral approximation of delayed advantage. We derive reward-specific
truncation errors that yield sufficient conditions for policy improvement.
3. We develop an action-centered Soft-HGR learning method with bidirec-
tional TD estimation.
Experiments validate the spectral analysis and
feature-learning performance, and evaluate zero-shot policy improvement
across rewards in discrete and continuous environments.

5
Experiments
This section evaluates whether SUAF provides accurate advantage representa-
tions and effective zero-shot control across reward tasks. We first describe the
experimental setup and evaluation protocol. We then use finite-state environ-
ments to examine representation accuracy, the benefit of separating immediate
and delayed effects, and the resulting policy improvement. Finally, we evaluate
zero-shot performance on continuous locomotion, navigation, and manipulation
tasks against feature-learning and successor-based methods.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Discrete Finite Environments We evaluate on Grid World Chevalier-Boisvert
et al. (2023), Four-room Sutton et al. (1999), and Taxi Dietterich (2000) to
cover basic navigation, narrow passages with long-range credit assignment, and
compositional tasks (navigation, pickup, delivery), respectively. These domains
vary in state-space size and temporal structure. For each, we partition reward
tasks into in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) sets while fixing
dynamics. Representations are learned from reward-free transitions under a be-
havior policy and rewards are used only for policy extraction. Since states and
transitions are enumerable, we compute the optimal return J(π∗) via dynamic
programming.
Continuous State and Action Environments. We further use four state-
based continuous-control domains: walker and cheetah from ExORL Yarats
et al. (2022), and antmaze-large-navigate (denoted as antmaze-large for short)
and cube-single-play from OGBench Park et al. (2025).
These jointly cover
locomotion under varied rewards, goal navigation, and robotic pick-and-place.
Representations are trained on reward-free offline transitions and reused across
downstream tasks. As tabular dynamic programming is infeasible, we follow
benchmark metrics: undiscounted episodic return for ExORL and task success
rate for OGBench. Futhermore, we evaluate generalization to held-out reward
tasks by retraining all methods under predefined ID/OOD splits. For Walker,
stand and walk are ID tasks, while run and flip are OOD tasks. For Cheetah,
run and run backward are ID tasks, while walk and walk backward are OOD
tasks. For both Antmaze-large and Cube-single-play, Tasks 1–2 form the ID set
and Tasks 3–4 form the OOD set.
Baselines For baselines, we compare against methods that are commonly used
and are the state of the art in reward-independent features, including Laplacian
features Wu et al. (2019), BYOL-γ Lawson et al. (2026), TD-JEPA Bagatella
et al. (2026), ICVF Ghosh et al. (2023), HILP Park et al. (2024), Forward-
Backward Touati et al. (2023) and One-Step FB Zheng et al. (2026).

Conclusion
In this work, we introduced SUAF, a reward-independent representation that
preserves the immediate reward contrast and allocates learned capacity to de-
layed action effects. This decomposition avoids the immediate-term spectral
floor of full advantage compression and yields optimal spectral approximations
of the delayed operator. We connected representation errors to sufficient condi-
tions for policy improvement and characterized local reuse under policy shifts.
Bidirectional TD provides a practical approach to learning the paired features
from reward-free data. Experiments support their utility for zero-shot policy
extraction across diverse reward tasks. The results highlight the value of sep-
arating directly computable reward information from learned temporal effects,
while leaving improved estimation of weak spectral modes and reliable reuse
over larger policy changes as directions for future work.

Policy Update within Trust Region
After pretraining the SUAF features, we use the right features as a reward
encoder. For a reward r, its task representation is

A single reward-conditioned actor is shared across all tasks:
πθ(a | s, r) = πθ(a | s, β(r)).
Thus, the actor receives both the current state and the SUAF reward represen-
tation, while all rewards are optimized jointly using the same parameter vector
θ. This allows policy knowledge to be shared across tasks rather than training
a separate actor for each reward.
The SUAF critic is associated with a reference policy πref. During policy
optimization, its features are reused while the current policy remains inside a
trust region. We refresh the critic only when DKL(πθ ∥πref) > δ. At a refresh,
new on-policy transitions are collected, the critic features f and g are warm-
started from their previous parameters and recalibrated, and the current policy
becomes the new reference policy. The reward coordinate supplied to the actor
may remain defined in the original pretrained coordinate system, providing a
stable task representation while the policy-specific critic is updated.

D.1
Assumptions
In this work, unless stated clearly, we consider a fixed discounted MDP with a
known discount factor 0 < γ < 1 and a reference policy π. Our construction
uses the following assumptions:
1. Access to the reference policy. We can evaluate π(a | s) and sample
actions from π(· | s). This allows us to center functions across actions at
a given state and to define the state–action transitions under π.
2. Access to transition data. We have trajectories under π, or appropri-
ately weighted transition data, from which the discounted occupancy dπ,
one-step pairs, and positive-lag pairs can be estimated. The transition
probabilities need not be known explicitly.
3. Access to downstream rewards.
Representation learning does not
use task rewards. When adapting to a task, its bounded reward function
r(s, a) can be evaluated at the state-action pairs needed to compute the
immediate reward contrast and the reward-feature coefficients.
4. Well-defined feature distributions. The weighted feature construc-
tion is restricted to the supports of dπ and ν+,π, where the required ex-
pectations and second moments are finite.
The exact finite-MDP policy-recovery result additionally assumes the full-
support and exact-representation conditions stated in Theorem D.5.

F.2
Continuous Environments
We evaluate reward-independent representation learning and downstream re-
ward transfer on continuous-control environments from ExORL and OGBench
as shown in Figure 26. Within each domain, all downstream tasks share the
same observation space, action space, transition dynamics, and offline transi-
tion dataset. The representation is learned without access to task rewards. At
evaluation time, different control problems are created by changing only the
downstream reward or goal. This protocol tests whether a single representation
of the dynamics can be reused across multiple tasks.
ExORL locomotion environments. We use the walker and cheetah domains
from ExORL. These environments evaluate transfer among locomotion objec-
tives that share the same underlying physical system but favor different behav-
iors, velocities, or directions of motion.
For walker, we consider four tasks:
1. flip, which rewards completing a flipping motion;
2. run, which rewards fast forward locomotion;
3. stand, which rewards maintaining an upright stationary pose;
4. walk, which rewards forward locomotion at a walking speed.
The tasks therefore range from static stabilization to periodic locomotion and
agile whole-body motion.
For cheetah, we consider four tasks:
1. run, which rewards fast forward locomotion;
2. run backward, which rewards fast locomotion in the reverse direction;
80


3. walk, which rewards slower forward locomotion;
4. walk backward, which rewards slower locomotion in the reverse direction.
The forward and backward tasks test whether the learned representation can be
reused when the preferred direction of motion changes, while the walking and
running tasks additionally vary the desired locomotion regime.
OGBench AntMaze Large Navigate. This domain requires a continuous-
control agent to navigate between distant regions of a large maze. Each task
is specified by a starting region and a target region. The maze geometry and
transition dynamics remain fixed, but different start-goal pairs may require
substantially different long-horizon routes. We consider five tasks:
1. bottom left →top right;
2. center →top left;
3. center →bottom right;
4. bottom right →center;
5. bottom left →center.
These tasks evaluate whether the learned representation captures the long-
horizon connectivity of the maze well enough to support transfer among different
navigation objectives.
OGBench Cube Single Play. This domain is a continuous robotic manipula-
tion problem in which the agent must pick up a cube and place it at a specified
target location. The robot, object, and contact dynamics are shared by all tasks;
only the desired placement target changes. We evaluate five tasks:
1. pick and place to the left;
2. pick and place to the front;
3. pick and place to the back;
4. pick and place along a diagonal direction;
5. pick and place along an off-diagonal direction.
Unlike the locomotion and navigation domains, successful transfer in this en-
vironment requires the representation to capture contact-rich manipulation dy-
namics, including both grasping and task-dependent object transport.
For every domain, overall denotes the arithmetic mean of the evaluation
score over all downstream tasks in that domain.

For a training query originating from episode e, reference rows from the same
