PGX: Physically Grounded Execution for Long-Horizon Humanoid
Loco-Manipulation
Kai Lu1, Chenyang Ma2, Ioannis Havoutis1
Abstract— Robotic vision-language-action (VLA) models can
execute diverse individual manipulation skills, yet they still
struggle with long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks, where tem-
poral structure, skills, and precision are tightly coupled within a
monolithic model. We propose Physically Grounded Execution
(PGX), a framework that offloads temporal and precision con-
trol to two classical training-free components, allowing the VLA
to focus on stage-specific skill dynamics. PGX decomposes long-
horizon loco-manipulation into three layers: (i) a predicate-
based state machine governs task progression, using physical
predicates to gate phase transitions, trigger recovery, and
coordinate navigation and waiting. Shared predicate semantics
connect offline demonstration segmentation, online execution,
and stage-wise evaluation; (ii) VLA skills operate within active
manipulation phases, with flat or stage-trained models con-
centrating on task-relevant actions; and (iii) world-coordinate
locking (WCL) maintains spatial consistency through base
station keeping, object-relative positioning, and world-anchored
contact targets. We demonstrate the effectiveness of PGX with
extensive experiments conducted on three simulated tasks and
one real-world task using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot for
long-horizon loco-manipulation, showing improvements in stage
progression and task success rates. Project page is available at
https://kl-research.github.io/pgx.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid loco-manipulation for long-horizon tasks is
central to embodied intelligence and generalist robotics, with
applications in domestic assistance, healthcare, and industrial
logistics. Yet even seemingly routine tasks in these domains
demand complex, extended coordination between locomotion
and manipulation. For instance, fetching a coffee or clearing
a table interleaves walking, contact-rich manipulation, and
precise object-part interaction over long action sequences.
The challenge lies in jointly addressing three tightly coupled
aspects: temporal structure, skill dynamics, and precise con-
trol, all of which must be grounded in physical interaction
and integrated within a unified visuomotor control framework
(see Fig. 1).
Traditional methods for loco-manipulation separate these
concerns across modules: task and motion planning or be-
havior trees control timing [1]–[3], model-based whole-body
control owns precision, and manipulation is realized by hand-
designed motion primitives. These systems are transparent
and precise, but every skill must be engineered for a specific
object and scene, and contact-rich skills remain brittle.

Learning-based methods offer a more generalizable al-
ternative: Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [4]–[9]
and learned whole-body controllers [10]–[12] acquire trans-
ferable manipulation skills and locomotion behaviors from
data, reducing the need for task- or object-specific pro-
gramming. However, learning and executing the entire long-
horizon loco-manipulation task with a single policy in-
troduces difficulties in maintaining task progress, coordi-
nating reference frames, and handling highly imbalanced
demonstrations. Without an explicit representation of task
progress, visually similar observations from different stages
can lead to incorrect skill execution [13]. Moreover, actions
represented relative to the moving pelvis frame make precise
manipulation difficult, since the action reference changes as
the humanoid moves. Long-horizon demonstrations are also
highly imbalanced: walking and low-motion segments dom-
inate the trajectory, which can bias imitation learning toward
conservative or near-static actions (e.g., standing still). Thus,

these challenges remain major obstacles to reliable end-to-
end long-horizon loco-manipulation, despite rapid progress
in humanoid whole-body control [11], [12], [14] and VLAs.
To this end, we aim to establish a physically grounded
framework bridging robot foundation models (i.e., VLAs)
and structured robot planning and control. Our insight is
that the aspects a flat VLA handles least reliably, deciding
when and where to act, can often be resolved directly from
physical quantities, whereas learning how to execute a skill
is precisely where VLAs are most effective. PGX therefore
uses physical structure to determine task progress, skill initi-
ation, and spatial grounding, while leaving the execution of
transferable manipulation skills to the VLA. By decoupling
timing and world-frame precision from the learned policy,
we hypothesize that the same foundation-model backbone
can execute long-horizon loco-manipulation more reliably.
Building on this insight, we introduce PGX (Physically
Grounded eXecution), a training-free execution framework
that separates deciding when and where to act from learning
how to act. Rather than modifying the VLA, introducing a
learned planner to infer sub-tasks from images [15]–[17], or
folding temporal structure into the policy itself [18], [19],
PGX assigns task progression and spatial precision to physi-
cally grounded components while preserving the VLA as the
manipulation skill executor. Specifically, PGX decomposes
long-horizon loco-manipulation into three layers. 1) Timing:
a predicate-gated finite state machine (FSM) maintains task
progress and memory, representing each stage as a tuple
of instruction, executor, guard predicate, timeout, and retry
policy. 2) Skills: the VLA operates only within manipulation
stages, while the execution layer re-establishes the start-
ing conditions under which each skill was trained; failed
guards trigger retries, and reverse transitions recover from
prematurely entered stages. 3) Precision: world-coordinate
locking (WCL) computes navigation goals from the current
object pose, holds the base at its manipulation-entry position,
and anchors precise contact targets in the world frame. In
this way, physical structure governs task progression and
spatial precision, allowing the VLA to focus on transferable
manipulation dynamics.
We evaluate PGX in Isaac Lab Arena on three tasks (box
transport, mug placement, and coffee fetching) and in a real-
world coffee task using a Unitree G1 Humanoid Robot. We
fine-tuned the state-of-the-art foundational VLA models π0.5
and GR00T N1.6 on collected human demonstrations. On
coffee fetching, PGX improves the task success rate of a fine-
tuned VLA from 35% to 80% with unchanged weights, and
improves stage progression and success on all three tasks for
both backbones. The real humanoid G1 experiment further
demonstrates the effectiveness of the execution structure.
In summary, the main contributions of our work are:
• We propose PGX, a physically grounded execution
framework that incorporates VLA models with a
predicate-gated state machine and a world-coordinate
locking layer, improving long-horizon capability.
• We develop predicate-gated stage sequencing with retry
and reverse transitions, together with WCL components,
and show that the decoupled layers supply structured
robot behaviors and generalize across VLA backbones.
• We evaluate the effectiveness of PGX on three simulated
loco-manipulation tasks and on a real humanoid, where
it turns near-failing flat policies into reliable long-
horizon execution.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Humanoid Loco-Manipulation
Recent progress on humanoids builds on learned whole-
body controllers that track retargeted human motion or tele-
operation commands [10], [14], [20]–[23], with decoupled
upper-body and locomotion interfaces that expose velocity
and joint targets to a higher-level policy [11], [12]. On
top of such controllers, generalist VLAs [4]–[7], [24] and
humanoid-specific policies [8], [9], [25] learn manipulation
and locomotion from demonstrations, while data-generation
pipelines synthesize demonstrations in simulation through
whole-body planning or procedural motion generation [26]–
[28]. These works primarily improve the controller, the data,
or the skill policy. Alternatively, our work takes a tuned VLA
and a whole-body control stack as given and studies how to
organize their skills into a long loco-manipulation task.
B. Long-Horizon Manipulation with VLAs
Many recent works tackle long-horizon tasks by decom-
posing them into sub-tasks, with the active sub-task inferred
by an LLM or VLM planner [15], [16], [29], [30], by the
VLA itself through embodied reasoning [6], [31], or by
a learned success or failure detector [32]. A second line
strengthens the temporal representation inside the policy,
through phase-aware input masking [18], explicit mem-
ory [19], or action chunking and flow-based decoders [5],
[33]. A third line chains separately learned skills, regulariz-
ing terminal states so that one skill starts where the previous
one ends [13], [34]–[37]. These methods leave task progress,
the switching decision, and the initial condition of each skill
to a network or a semantic model, whereas PGX chooses to
keep task progress in an external state machine and switch
skills on physical events rather than inferred semantics.
C. Structured Robotic Execution and Hybrid Control
Combining task structure with continuous control has been
studied for years, and recent work replaces parts of this
pipeline with learned or language-based components. Lan-
guage models now generate the task structure as programs
or plans and delegate execution to a library of learned
skills [29], [30], [38]. [17] tracks progress from images with
a foundation model and adjusts the stance with a VLM before
manipulation, while our approach gates stages on physical
predicates rather than image-based inference. Hybrid plan-
ners chain learned skills under feasibility checks [35], [37].
These build on the classical machinery of task and motion
planning [1], [3] and behavior trees [2], which make the
discrete structure explicit and hand execution to model-based
control. On humanoids, large learned whole-body controllers
now expose one command interface to teleoperation and

III. PHYSICALLY GROUNDED EXECUTION
Our goal is to use a hierarchical execution structure
to address the key challenges of long-horizon humanoid
loco-manipulation, including task progress, spatial precision,
and highly imbalanced demonstrations. The overall frame-
work of PGX is shown in Fig. 2. A long-horizon loco-
manipulation task is executed by three layers: a predicate
state machine decides which stage is active and who executes
it, the VLA acts inside manipulation stages, and a world-
coordinate locking (WCL) layer keeps the base, the carried
object, and device targets consistent in the world frame.
The three layers are a division of responsibility rather than
three networks that every action must pass through. This
decomposition separates stages with distinct dynamics and
learning requirements, preventing long locomotion and low-
motion segments from dominating manipulation learning. We
state the problem in Sec. III-A, describe the three layers
in Secs. III-B–III-D, and show in Sec. III-C how the same
predicates segment demonstrations for training.
A. Problem Statement
We consider a long-horizon task given as a stage graph
G = (S, E), a trained VLA policy πθ, and a whole-body
control stack. The graph contains the nominal progression
path and the retry or reverse edges triggered by failure states.
The goal is to complete the task within a time budget.
We distinguish the policy observation ot, which contains
camera images and proprioception, from the executor state
zt, which additionally contains estimates of the base and
task-object poses, grasp evidence, and device states, provided
by the robot’s state estimation. The executor state is used
only for gating, navigation, and geometric control. Given ot
and the stage instruction ℓk, the VLA outputs an action chunk
of length H:
At = πθ(ot, ℓk),

targets, base velocity commands, and hand commands,
tracked by the whole-body controller. PGX does not change
the network or its parameters θ; at every control period it
selects, constrains, or replaces individual action channels
according to the stage and the physical state.
B. Predicate-Gated Stage Machine
Stage representation. Each stage is written as a tuple
sk = (ℓk, ck, gk, τk, ρk),
(2)
denoting its instruction, executor, completion guard, time
budget, and recovery policy. Walking stages are executed
by a navigation controller while the arms hold a ready
or carry posture; manipulation stages are executed by the
VLA while the executor manages the base; device waits are
handled by a state check or a timer, so the VLA never has
to generate a waiting behavior. In this work, the coffee task
has eight stages: approach, grasp, transport, place, press, re-
grasp, transport, and deliver, of which five are manipulation


stages, and the wait between press and re-grasp belongs to
the executor. Every stage exits on a physical outcome, never
on the end of an action chunk or a preset duration.
Predicate interface. Five predicates form the basic set:
grasped, placed, triggered, at-stance, and dropped. grasped
combines the hand state, the wrist–object distance, and a lift
above the object’s rest height,
grasped ⇔closed∧∥pW −pO∥< dg ∧zO −zrest > hg, (3)
so that closing an empty hand is not mistaken for a grasp.
placed requires the object to be inside the target region,
slower than vp, and released; for intermediate placements
it also requires the wrist to have retreated, leaving room
for the next interaction. triggered reads the device state, at-
stance compares the base pose with the stage’s reference
stance, and dropped fires when a held object leaves the hand
outside a placement region. Each predicate must hold for a
minimum duration, with hysteresis, so that one noisy frame
cannot advance the stage.
The key design choice is that one rule serves three
purposes. The same predicate determines skill boundaries in
demonstrations offline (Sec. III-C), gates transitions online,
and records task progress at evaluation. The training target,
the execution condition, and the metric are therefore tied
to one physical event; each use only sets thresholds and
hold times according to its sensor source, and nothing has
to redefine what “grasped” or “placed” means.
Recovery and reverse transitions. Each manipulation
stage receives a bounded number of attempts. When the
guard fails, the executor restores the stance and requests
a fresh action chunk, without releasing a held object and
without opening the hand close to the object. For stage
confusion the machine allows reverse transitions: if during
press the mug is grasped again while the device has not been
triggered, the executor returns to place and re-establishes the
pre-press condition, changing only the stage and the prompt.
An unrecoverable drop terminates the episode. Algorithm 1
summarizes the execution of one stage.
C. Skill Execution with Fine-Tuned VLAs
PGX treats the VLA as a policy that manipulates under
local conditions, not as the sole controller of the whole task.
Before a manipulation stage, the navigation controller brings
the robot to a stance relative to the target, the executor waits
for the stopping transient to decay and selects an arm posture
compatible with the coming skill: an empty-hand grasp starts
from the ready posture, a placement with an object starts
from the carry posture, and consecutive manipulations keep
a compatible end-effector state.
Two kinds of skill model are supported. A flat model is
trained on full demonstrations with one task-level instruction,
kept for every manipulation stage inside PGX. A stage model
is trained on manipulation segments cut from the same
demonstrations by the same predicates that gate execution: a
segment starts when effective hand motion begins after the
last base adjustment and ends on the grasp, release, or retreat

D. World-Coordinate Locking
WCL reduces the influence of base motion on skill inputs
and on interaction targets by maintaining task-relevant ref-
erences in the world frame. It acts on the stance and on fine
contact separately, rather than locking the end-effector for
all manipulation.
1) Object-relative stance. For skills whose start depends
on where the object currently is, the navigation goal is
expressed relative to the object rather than to the map:
the executor composes the current object estimate with the
relative stance observed in demonstrations, and refreshes this
goal before every attempt and retry, so that a placement error
does not carry over as a start-state error of the same size.
Fixed devices use a task reference stance.
2) Base station keeping. During consecutive manipula-
tions at one workstation the executor keeps a single world-
frame stance reference, instead of re-anchoring per stage and
accumulating error. A correction starts when the base drift
exceeds an outer threshold and stops inside an inner one,

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
In this section, we analyze long-horizon task performance
and stage progression, demonstrate the improvements of
PGX across multiple loco-manipulation tasks, evaluate the
effectiveness of execution structure and stage-wise training,
and conduct an ablation study of world-frame locking. We
further validate PGX on a real Unitree G1 humanoid robot.
A. Experimental Setups
1) Environments and tasks. Simulation experiments are
performed in Isaac Lab Arena [40] with a Unitree G1
humanoid, Dex-3 hands, and the corresponding whole-body
control stack; the actions of each model are mapped to a
common executor interface. We choose three tasks covering
bimanual transport, single-hand object transfer, and long-
horizon manipulation with device interaction. Box transport:
take a box from a shelf and carry it to a target container, with
100 official MimicGen demonstrations (200 manipulation
segments after cutting). Mug placement: take a mug from a
counter and transfer it to a target region, with 30 teleoperated
demonstrations (60 segments). Coffee fetching: take the mug,
operate the coffee machine, re-grasp, and deliver, with 50
Quest-teleoperated demonstrations (250 segments); this is the
main testbed. The real-robot task is to grasp a mug, place it
in the machine region, re-grasp, and deliver, with 44 Quest
demonstrations (176 segments). The real G1 uses the Dex-3
left hand; Vicon provides the poses of the robot, and head
and wrist cameras provide policy observations. Simulation
and real data (50 Hz and 30 Hz) are trained and deployed
separately.

D. Execution Structure versus Stage-Wise Training
This section and Sec. IV-E form the ablation of the
executor: the ladder flat →+ execution structure →+ spatial
stabilization, crossed with the two skill models. To isolate the
executor itself, we place the flat model inside PGX and keep
the same task-level instruction in every manipulation stage.
The first two columns of Table III therefore hold the weights
and the language input fixed and change only the execution;
the third column replaces the model by the stage-trained one.
For both backbones the executor brings a large gain
without retraining the policy. Stage-wise training, in contrast,
is a secondary and model-dependent factor: for π0.5 it
changes success by at most 5 points, and for GR00T the
flat weights are the stronger skill model on coffee. PGX
therefore separates execution structure from skill weights,
so that whichever model has the stronger local skill can be
used directly, without retraining a stage model.

stage instruction by a mismatched one changes the output
little. Together with the same-weight ablation, this indicates
that stage prompts alone do not explain the gain of PGX:

E. Effect of World-Frame Stabilization
Stance and drift. Switching off spatial stabilization (the
FSM-only rows of Table I) drops π0.5 on coffee from 60%
/ 55% to 32% / 50% and GR00T from 80% / 80% to 65%
/ 75%. This comparison removes object-relative stance and
station keeping together, and reflects their joint contribution.
To observe drift within a stage, we inject a base displacement
in a fixed direction every 3 s during manipulation. Fig. 5
shows that station keeping bounds the accumulated drift and
raises the completion ratio of placement attempts.
Without injected disturbance the natural drift of a standing
humanoid is 3–4 cm, within the station-keeping band, so
the benefit comes from the object-relative stance that re-
establishes the start state before each skill; under sustained
disturbance the error would otherwise accumulate across
consecutive manipulations, and station keeping reduces the
p90 of per-stage maximum drift to 9 cm, compared with
54 cm without it. Millimeter-level contact under such distur-
bance additionally requires the base to come to rest, which
the executor enforces before the button stage. The predicate
interface itself tolerates state noise: on the mug task, per-
episode object-position biases of 2, 3, and 5 cm with per-
frame jitter keep success at 75–90%.
Fine pressing. Over 14 evaluation runs without sustained
disturbance (two backbones, two skill models, and primitive
versions), the world-anchored primitive completed 243 of
245 entries into the button stage (99%), against 72–100%


when the stage is left to the VLA inside the full PGX, both
conditioned on reaching the stage.
F. Real-Robot Experiments
Fig. 6 shows our real experiment setup. We deploy PGX
on a G1 with a transferred stage graph and predicates from
simulation; the predicates read motion capture and joint
states, and the VLA is fine-tuned on 44 real demonstrations.
The task keeps the structure of the simulated one: the robot
walks to the counter, grasps the mug, carries it to the coffee
machine, places it, waits, re-grasps, and delivers it (Fig. 8).
Fig. 7 reports the completion rates of the four manipulation
stages, all executed by the PGX π0.5 stage model; each rate
is computed over the episodes that reach that stage. Grasping
transfers best among these stages, and the differences from
simulation concentrate at the two contact-rich stages, placing
and re-grasping. These gaps reflect the precision of the
fine-tuned skills on the real hand rather than the execution
structure.
V. CONCLUSION
In this work, we study long-horizon loco-manipulation
on a humanoid robot and propose PGX, which composes
existing VLA skills into a long task through predicate-driven
stage progression, skill execution under controlled start con-
ditions, and world-frame spatial stabilization. Experiments
on three simulated tasks and two backbones show that ex-
plicit execution structure raises task completion substantially,
while stage-wise training and precision control contribute
at different levels. We also validate our method on a real
G1 humanoid. PGX thus provides an effective and general
framework bridging robot foundation models and classical
control for loco-manipulation tasks.
Limitation and Future Work. PGX achieves higher
success rates yet costs longer execution time for stage
transitions, which we will improve in future work, together
with extending the framework to richer skills and tasks.
