# Abstract 

Whole-body motion tracking enables humanoid robots to reproduce diverse skills from human demonstrations. Exist-ing model-free reinforcement learning methods either distill motion specialists into a single policy at the cost of per-formance or train a generalist policy directly using cluster-scale computation. SuperMimic reduces this computational cost through a differentiable world model that implicitly learns the physics shared across motions by predicting the next state from the current state and action. However, this implicit formulation may not fully capture the underlying physical processes. Errors around changes of support can accumulate over imagined rollouts used to train the tracking policy. To address this challenge, we introduce DynaMimic, a differentiable world model that explicitly incorporates shared physics into state prediction. Specifically, its network predicts a contact wrench at each candidate support link and a residual generalized force. DynaMimic then uses these predictions to compute the next state through closed-form Lagrangian forward dynamics. Across 60 full LAFAN1 motions, DynaMimic reduces mean per-joint position error (MPJPE) by 18.6% relative to SuperMimic. For terrain tracking, we give both methods the same local height maps. Relative to terrain-conditioned SuperMimic, DynaMimic reduces MPJPE by 14.9% on A3 

1arXiv:2611.32944v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 Parkour (66.15 to 56.28 mm) and by 10.8% on GRAIL ter-rain clips (141.88 to 126.57 mm). On a real A3 humanoid, the learned policies can perform diverse skills, such as floor recovery, cartwheel and a box vault. 

# 1. Introduction 

Whole-body motion tracking allows a humanoid to repro-duce diverse skills from human demonstrations [5, 21]. By following different reference motions, a single tracking pol-icy can enable the humanoid to walk across a room, get up from the floor, or vault over an obstacle. However, training such a policy across a large motion library remains com-putationally expensive. Existing model-free reinforcement learning methods either distill motion specialists into a sin-gle policy at the cost of performance [13] or train a generalist policy directly using cluster-scale computation [1, 16]. Recently, SuperMimic [12] reduces this computational cost through a differentiable world model that implicitly learns the contact effects, underlying dynamics shared across motions by predicting the next state from the current state and action. However, this implicit mapping is difficult to learn. For example, during floor recovery, extending the elbow can raise the torso while the palm is planted, whereas a similar action mainly moves the arm after the hand leaves the ground. Prediction errors around such contact transi-tions can propagate through imagined rollouts and affect the gradients used to optimize the policy. Although the contact forces change, the body’s response follows the same govern-ing equations of motion. Given the current state, actuator forces, and contact forces, the robot’s known forward dynam-ics determine its acceleration. This motivates learning the interaction-dependent forces and using known mechanics to compute their effects across motions. To address these challenges, we introduce DynaMimic , a differentiable world model that explicitly incorporates this shared physics into each prediction (Figure 2). Given the current state and action, our differentiable world model pre-dicts a contact wrench, comprising a force and a moment, at each candidate support link. It also predicts a residual gener-alized force to account for effects such as unmodeled joint friction. DynaMimic combines these predictions with the actuator forces, computes the robot’s acceleration through Lagrangian forward dynamics, to obtain the next pose and velocity in closed form. This formulation grounds next-state predictions in the robot’s equations of motion. Besides the commonly used trajectory loss, we directly supervise the contact wrenches prediction at each candidate support link by using contact wrenches already computed by the simula-tor, requiring no additional manual annotation. We further extend DynaMimic to handle a more realistic setting human–scene interaction (HSI), where predicting the effect of an action also requires information about the scene. We represent the local scene with a terrain height map centered on the robot and aligned with its heading. The height map is utilised in the policy training for selecting an action and world model for better motion prediction. Overall, our contributions are twofold. i) We proposed DynaMimic, a novel method with a physically-informed differentiable world model that combines learned contact wrenches with the robot’s known forward dynamics in each state update. ii) We extend DynaMimic to human–scene interaction by conditioning both the tracking policy and the world model on local terrain geometry and updating the terrain input throughout imagined rollouts. We evaluate DynaMimic on benchmark datasets and outperform the State-of-the-art method consistently. We also deploy the learned policy on a physical humanoid to demonstrate the superiority of our proposed method. 

# 2. Related Work 

Humanoid motion tracking. DeepMimic [17] used prox-imal policy optimization (PPO) to train simulated char-acters to track reference motions. PHC [14] extended tracking to large motion libraries with a single policy, and PULSE [15] distilled tracking skills into a latent action space for downstream control. MaskedMimic [20] enables control from partial references, including sparse joint targets and keyframes. On physical humanoids, H2O and OmniH2O use human motion for whole-body teleoperation [4, 5], while HOVER [7] supports multiple control interfaces with one policy. ASAP [6] and BeyondMimic [13] further demon-strate agile motion tracking on hardware. Off-policy methods reuse simulator transitions through experience replay. FlashSAC [10] applies this approach to high-dimensional robot control, and Seo et al. [18] use off-policy training for humanoid locomotion and motion tracking. Model-based methods also use collected transi-tions to learn how a robot responds to an action, as in Day-Dreamer [22] and Robotic World Model [11]. For humanoid tracking, SuperMimic [12] fits one differentiable dynam-ics model across reference motions and updates the policy through short imagined rollouts. Its transition network learns the combined effects of actuation, contact, and body dynam-ics from state transitions. 

Physics-informed world model learning. Contact pro-vides information about how a body moves within a scene. In human motion recovery, PROX [3] uses body–scene con-tact to constrain pose, and WHAM [19] uses estimated foot contact to refine the global trajectory and reduce foot slid-ing. Body–scene distances also describe interactions in motion forecasting and generation [25, 27]. Other meth-ods model the forces underlying these interactions. Xie et al. [23] infer forces consistent with motion observed in video, PhysPT [28] uses contact and dynamics constraints to esti-2mate motion and forces, and InterPhys [26] models contact forces during motion synthesis in dynamic scenes. Hybrid simulators use learned forces to advance the state of a physical system. NeuralSim [8] embeds learned com-ponents in differentiable rigid-body dynamics, and Jiang et al. [9] combine analytical contact mechanics with data to predict the net contact impulse on a rigid body. DynaMimic uses a hybrid transition within the imagined rollouts that train a humanoid tracking policy. At each step it predicts a wrench for each candidate support link and a residual generalized force, then integrates their effects us-ing the robot’s known dynamics. Simulator-recorded link wrenches supervise how the load is distributed across the body, while the state trajectory supervises the resulting mo-tion. We optimize the tracking policy by differentiating its return through both the predicted contact wrenches and the rigid-body dynamics. For terrain-conditioned tracking, both the policy and world model receive local height maps that are updated as the predicted robot pose changes along imagined rollouts. 

# 3. Method 

Overview. DynaMimic is a humanoid motion tracking framework that resolves contact ambiguity by learning a physics-informed world model. Following SuperMimic [12], we improve the policy by differentiating the tracking objec-tive through short rollouts in a learned world model. Our world model moves beyond standard transition models that implicitly memorize state changes by first predicting the con-tact wrenches and residual forces from the robot’s current state and the policy’s action. It then uses rigid-body dynam-ics to compute the next state. We first introduce the training loop shared across reference motions (Sec. 3.1). We then de-scribe how DynaMimic computes a state transition and how we fit it to simulator trajectories (Sec. 3.2). Finally, we use the fitted model to optimize the tracking policy (Sec. 3.3). 

3.1. Training Loop 

DynaMimic trains a whole-body tracking controller in one al-ternating loop of simulator data collection, physics-informed world-model fitting, and imagination-based policy optimiza-tion (Fig. 2). Training alternates between three steps. First, the current policy acts in simulation, and we store the result-ing trajectories in a replay buffer together with the executed actions and measured link wrenches. Second, we fit fϕ to short windows from the buffer by rolling it forward under the recorded actions. Third, we fix the world-model parameters and let the policy select its own actions over short imagined rollouts. We update the policy by differentiating the track-ing return through these rollouts. The updated policy then collects the next batch of simulator data. Formally, at control step t, the policy πθ , with parameters 

θ, selects an action at ∈ R29 specifying targets for the actuated joints. Its inputs are the last L = 10 proprioceptive observations, written ot−L+1: t = ( ot−L+1 , . . . , o t), and a window gt of ten reference frames containing joint positions, joint velocities, and relative base orientations. Let st denote the robot state and ξ the episode’s friction coefficient, joint calibration offsets, and torso center-of-mass displacement. 3.3. Learning the Tracking Policy 

We now use the world model learned in section 3.2 to im-prove the tracking policy. Starting from a simulator state sampled from replay, the policy chooses its own actions and the frozen model predicts their consequences for 32 control steps. We optimize the tracking rewards over these steps and use a critic to estimate the remaining return from the final predicted state. During the actor update, gradients flow through the predicted contact wrenches and rigid-body dy-namics to the actions that produced the motion, as in Fig. 2c. 

Rolling out the policy. For the policy update (Fig. 2c), we initialize each imagined rollout from a replay state and its observation history. A reference encoder embeds a window of ten reference frames, and an action decoder combines that embedding with the ten most recent proprioceptive obser-vations. Each observation contains projected gravity, base angular velocity, joint positions and velocities, and the previ-ous action. For terrain-conditioned tracking, the actor and critic each encode the current local height map. At every imagined step, the policy selects an action, DynaMimic pre-dicts the next state, and the resulting observation enters the history for the next decision.

4.2. Datasets and Evaluation Protocol 

LAFAN1. LAFAN1 [2] contains 77 motion-capture se-quences from five performers, covering locomotion, dance, falls, and recoveries. We exclude motions that rely on obsta-cles because they are difficult to reproduce on level ground without the corresponding scene geometry. The remaining 60 sequences last 102–306 seconds each. These include the 18 challenging intervals identified by OmniXtreme [21]. 

A3 Parkour. We collected A3 Parkour, a dataset of 22 parkour motions paired with the geometry of the static box used in each recording. The boxes vary in height and length. The motions include hand-supported vaults, climbs onto the box, and dismounts that end in a roll. 

GRAIL. GRAIL [24] provides over 34,000 simulated hu-manoid motion sequences in its public release, spanning object pickup, sitting, and terrain traversal. We use the three terrain categories, which pair each motion with the geometry of the curb, slope, or staircase traversed by the humanoid. 

Evaluation. We train a separate tracking policy for each dataset and share it across reference motions within that dataset. On A3 Parkour and GRAIL, we evaluate the refer-ence libraries used for training (22 motions and 9,736 clips, respectively). The 18 challenging LAFAN1 intervals are drawn from the 60 full motions. For each full motion, we initialize the robot at the first reference pose and velocity and track continuously on level ground (LAFAN1) or with the paired scene geometry (A3 Parkour and GRAIL). For each challenging LAFAN1 interval, we reset to its first refer-ence pose and velocity, so tracking starts without drift from the preceding motion. Each rollout stops at the end of the sequence or interval, or earlier if the robot triggers a failure.

4.3. Results 

Level-ground tracking. DynaMimic and SuperMimic both complete all 60 full motions from LAFAN1 [2] and all 18 challenging intervals identified by OmniXtreme [21] (Table 1). DynaMimic achieves higher return and lower world-frame MPJPE on both sets. It also tracks body pose relative to the pelvis more accurately, with a larger reduction in root-relative MPJPE on the challenging intervals. At most training checkpoints, DynaMimic achieves higher return and lower WM error.(Fig. 3). 

Contact analysis. We group LAFAN1 segments by mo-tion intensity and whether the reference motion involves non-foot ground contact (Table 2). DynaMimic achieves higher return and lower WM error than SuperMimic in every group. MPJPE also decreases in every group except the low-intensity group with non-foot contact. DynaMimic shows its clearest advantage on high-intensity motions with non-foot contact, where it achieves the largest gain in return and the largest reductions in velocity, acceleration, and WM errors. Figure 4(a) illustrates a floor recovery: from the same initial state, DynaMimic completes the motion while SuperMimic terminates early. 

Terrain-conditioned tracking. Adding a local height map to SuperMimic substantially reduces MPJPE on both A3 Parkour and GRAIL [24] (Tables 3 and 4). With the same terrain input, DynaMimic further improves return and re-duces MPJPE and WM error on both datasets. On A3 Park-our, both terrain-aware policies complete all 22 motions, but DynaMimic tracks position, velocity, and acceleration more accurately. On GRAIL, DynaMimic also completes a larger fraction of clips. 

Ablation study. We evaluate the contributions of link in-puts, forward dynamics, and contact wrench supervision on the same ten-second LAFAN1 segments (Table 5). The full DynaMimic model achieves higher return and lower track-ing and WM errors than SuperMimic with the same link inputs. Removing either the link inputs or contact wrench supervision reduces tracking accuracy. Removing forward dynamics causes the largest drop in return and the largest increase in every error metric, indicating that it contributes most to tracking performance among the components tested. 

Real-world deployment. On the physical A3 humanoid, we run the trained tracking policy without the world model. The robot rises from the floor, performs a cartwheel, and vaults over a box (Fig. 1). These motions demonstrate changes of support on ground and an elevated obstacle. 

# 5. Conclusion 

DynaMimic trains humanoid motion tracking policies with a differentiable world model that learns contact wrenches and a residual generalized force, then uses the robot’s rigid-body dynamics to predict the resulting motion. DynaMimic re-duces MPJPE by 18.6% relative to SuperMimic on LAFAN1. When both methods receive the same local height maps, Dy-naMimic reduces this error by 14.9% on A3 Parkour and 10.8% on GRAIL terrain clips.
