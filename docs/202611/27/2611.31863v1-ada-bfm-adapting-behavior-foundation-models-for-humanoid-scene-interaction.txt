# ABSTRACT 

Behavior Foundation Models (BFMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for general-purpose humanoid control through pretraining on large-scale behav-ioral data. However, their proficiency in whole-body coordination does not readily extend to scene interaction, which requires adapting motion to scene-dependent geometric constraints and contact dynamics. To bridge this gap, we introduce Ada-BFM , a post-training framework that adapts pretrained BFMs for humanoid scene interaction. Ada-BFM augments the original architecture with a lightweight adapter, enabling interaction-specific adjustments while leveraging pretrained control capabilities. Rather than producing a separate controller for each task, the framework yields a shared model for each interaction domain, sup-porting diverse tasks involving terrain or object interaction. Extensive experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate that Ada-BFM enables diverse scene interaction behaviors, accelerates learning compared with training from scratch, and largely preserves pretrained behavioral competence. These findings establish Ada-BFM as an effective and efficient approach to extending the capabilities of BFMs beyond their pretraining settings while retaining their strengths in general whole-body control. 1arXiv:2611.31863v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Humanoid whole-body control has advanced substantially in recent years, achieving impressive capabilities across diverse motor skills (Ben et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2025; He et al., 2025a). However, existing approaches often rely on extensive reward engineering tailored to specific tasks, limiting their versatility and hindering generalization across scenarios. To address these limitations, recent research has adopted behavior learning as a shared objective across whole-body control tasks, giving rise to Behavior Foundation Models (BFMs), large-scale pretrained humanoid controllers that enable robust and versatile control in response to diverse behavioral specifications (Zeng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026c). Motion tracking (Peng et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026) is identified as a unified and scalable task for pretraining these models, allowing BFMs to acquire increasingly rich behavioral knowledge and gain consistent improvements in control capa-bilities as pretraining scales (Luo et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a). Despite this growth in behavioral knowledge, existing BFM pretraining remains largely scene-agnostic , focusing on the realization of behavioral specifications without explicitly accounting for the surrounding environments. This limits BFMs’ ability to interact with terrain and objects, where successful execution requires not only following control commands but adapting the execution to scene-dependent geometric constraints and contact dynamics (Yang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Weng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026). Although a broad repertoire of behaviors does not, by itself, ensure effective scene interaction, the underlying behavioral knowledge provides a potentially valu-able foundation for efficient transfer. This motivates scene-aware adaptation of BFMs that builds upon pretrained behavioral capabilities to meet the demands of physical interaction. To this end, we propose Ada-BFM , a post-training framework that adapts the pretrained BFM for humanoid scene interaction. The framework is designed around three core adaptation objectives: 1) Effectiveness , enabling a single adapted model within each interaction domain to handle diverse tasks and outperform baseline approaches; 2) Efficiency , leveraging the behavioral knowledge ac-quired through pretraining to accelerate the acquisition of scene-interaction capabilities; and 3) Re-tention , preserving the broad behavioral repertoire of the pretrained model while minimizing degra-dation of its original control capabilities. Together, these objectives extend the capabilities of BFMs beyond their pretraining while preserving their strengths as general-purpose humanoid controllers. As a concrete instantiation, Ada-BFM is built upon a pretrained BFM from ScaleBFM (Zeng et al., 2026). Its Humanoid Transformer backbone employs self-attention to capture temporal dependen-cies within behavioral trajectories and cross-attention to incorporate behavioral specifications that guide action generation. A learnable query token is appended to the end of the context sequence to aggregate control-relevant information and its output representation is used to predict actions. To enable scene-aware adaptation, Ada-BFM uses a lightweight adapter to incorporate scene-relevant information into Transformer blocks. The adapter comprises a gated cross-attention module and a gated feed-forward layer. This design extends the backbone’s conditioning mechanism: motion goals specify the intended behavior, while scene observations describe the environment in which it must be executed. Rather than concatenating scene inputs with the original specifications, the adapter incorporates them through a separate conditioning pathway designed to minimize interfer-ence with pretrained control capabilities. To evaluate the performance of the proposed framework in adapting pretrained BFMs for humanoid scene interaction, we consider two representative domains: terrain interaction and object interac-tion. Within each domain, we train a single adapted model to handle diverse tasks rather than a separate model for each task. Both domains use the same adaptation framework, with domain-specific interaction environments and rewards. Depth images provide a common representation of scene information across both domains. Throughout post-training, the pretrained backbone remains frozen and continues to process control commands, while the trainable adaptation pathway learns to leverage depth perception to modulate the resulting behavior for scene-aware physical interaction. Comprehensive experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate that Ada-BFM outper-forms baseline approaches on terrain-interaction and object-interaction tasks, accelerates learning of scene-interaction behaviors compared with training from scratch and largely preserves pretrained control capabilities. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness, efficiency and retention of the proposed framework, establishing it as a promising approach for extending BFMs beyond their pre-training settings while retaining their strengths in general whole-body control. 22 RELATED WORK 

2.1 GENERAL HUMANOID MOTION TRACKING AND BEHAVIOR FOUNDATION MODELS 

Physics-based imitation learns whole-body skills from human motion (Peng et al., 2018; 2022), and motion trackers expose them as an interface for teleoperation (Luo et al., 2023; He et al., 2025b; Yin et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026). Behavior foundation models apply this approach at larger scale, with competence improving as pretraining grows (Zeng et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c; Chen et al., 2026a). These models achieve strong whole-body motor capability. However, they are pretrained largely on flat-ground data and therefore lack the capability to interact with terrain or objects. 2.2 HUMANOID SCENE INTERACTION 

Existing scene-interaction controllers acquire interaction through task-specific engineering. For ter-rain, perceptive policies map depth or voxel observations to actions for a specific terrain (Wu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Ben et al., 2025b), while reference-conditioned trackers reconcile the ref-erence with the terrain by modifying motion priors (Wang et al., 2026b), relaxing tracking con-straints (Li et al., 2026a), or supplying scene-aligned 4D demonstrations (Brandes et al., 2026). Object interaction is learned from human videos (Weng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a), adversarial motion priors (Wang et al., 2025), or contact representations (Yu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b). Training such a controller from scratch requires acquiring whole-body coordination and interaction jointly. In practice, it relies on hand-designed rewards or teacher-student distillation. A pretrained motion prior, by contrast, allows the controller to learn the interaction skill more efficiently. 2.3 POST -T RAINING ADAPTATION 

Post-training adaptation extends the capabilities of a pretrained model. In language and vision mod-els, adapters and low-rank updates add few trainable parameters to a fixed network (Houlsby et al., 2019; Rebuffi et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2022; Alayrac et al., 2022). In humanoid control, GRAFT and C-GMT adapt the reference or the tracking objective (Li et al., 2026b; Parsuram et al., 2026), ViBe adapts perception features (Krishna et al., 2026), and ResMimic learns a residual over the action output (Zhao et al., 2025). Task Tokens and FIBER keep the backbone frozen and add a condition-ing pathway (Vainshtein et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026), and TokenHSI unifies multiple interaction tasks for simulated characters through a tokenized interface (Pan et al., 2025). Post-training adap-tation must extend the capabilities of the pretrained model while preserving as much of its original capability as possible.

Adaptive sampling. We adapt the distribution of episode starting points using failures observed during training. Each reference motion is divided into temporal intervals. Intervals with larger moving averages of failure counts are sampled more often, and a starting frame is sampled uniformly within the selected interval. A uniform component maintains coverage of the remaining intervals. 

Asymmetric observations. Following ScaleBFM, we use an asymmetric actor–critic. The actor receives proprioception–action history, motion goals, and depth observations. A separate critic also receives privileged simulator information about whole-body states and the terrain or object. This information supports value estimation and is not required at deployment. Depth provides a common scene representation for both domains. Preprocessing details are provided in Appendix A.3. 3.3 MODEL ARCHITECTURE 

Ada-BFM augments a frozen BFM with a trainable pathway for scene information. A depth encoder and tokenizer provide visual features to the adapter, which refines the action representation while retaining the original motion conditioning pathway. Figure 2 illustrates the architecture. 

Frozen pretrained backbone. Ada-BFM builds on the Humanoid Transformer in ScaleBFM (Zeng et al., 2026). Throughout adaptation, we freeze all pretrained backbone pa-rameters, including the tokenizers, the initial query embedding, the Transformer blocks, and the action head. The backbone interleaves historical proprioception and action tokens into a context sequence. A query token initialized with the pretrained embedding attends to this context and provides the representation used for action prediction. An attention mask allows the query token to read the historical context but prevents context tokens from attending to the query token. This separates the aggregation of information for action prediction from the processing of historical context. Reference cross-attention incorporates motion goals encoded by the pretrained goal tokenizer. # 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environment setup. We conduct experiments with the Unitree G1 humanoid. We train Ada-BFM in Isaac Lab on eight NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs, with 4096 environments per GPU. We adapt a shared model for each interaction domain. Training takes approximately 3.5 hours for terrain interaction and 19 hours for object interaction under this configuration. 

Dataset. Terrain interaction uses data from OmniRetarget (Yang et al., 2026) and GRAIL (Xie et al., 2026). Object interaction uses data retargeted from OMOMO using OmniRetarget. Ap-pendix A.2 describes the data used in each domain. We also evaluate on the BONES Test Set (Bones Studio, 2026) to assess whether the adapted policy preserves the original BFM’s motion capabilities. 

Baseline approaches. We compare against ScaleBFM and SONIC (Luo et al., 2026), full fine-tuning of the pretrained BFM, training only the action head, LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), Task Tokens (Vainshtein et al., 2025) and ViBe (Krishna et al., 2026). We also compare against PMT (Wang et al., 2026b) for terrain interaction, and HDMI (Weng et al., 2025) for object interaction. We use the public checkpoints for ViBe, PMT, and HDMI. We train the remaining baselines under the same configuration as Ada-BFM. Details of the metrics and success criteria are provided in Appendix A.1. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

We evaluate Ada-BFM against three objectives (Section 1): Effectiveness , Efficiency , and Retention .• Effectiveness: Can Ada-BFM support diverse tasks within each interaction domain using a single adapted model and outperform baseline approaches? • Efficiency: Can Ada-BFM leverage pretrained behavioral knowledge to learn scene-interaction capabilities faster than training from scratch? • Retention: Can Ada-BFM preserve the pretrained BFM’s control capabilities while ex-tending its ability to interact with scenes?

Efficiency. To answer the second question, we examine whether pretrained motion priors acceler-ate learning of humanoid scene interaction and whether stronger motion priors lead to faster learning and better task performance. We compare three methods for learning terrain and object interaction tasks: Ada-BFM, Ada-BFM (small pretrained), and training from scratch. For Ada-BFM (small pretrained), we select another ScaleBFM checkpoint with smaller model capacity, pretrained on a smaller reference motion dataset covering a narrower range of behaviors. For training from scratch, we train a BFM using the same training configuration without loading pretrained parameters. As shown in Figure 4(a, b), Ada-BFM converges faster and achieves a higher reward than training from scratch. These results show that pretrained motion priors accelerate learning and improve scene interaction policies. The termination curves in Figure 4(c, d) help explain this advantage. Ada-BFM reuses pretrained motion control capabilities and experiences fewer early terminations due to body position error. Training from scratch requires learning motion tracking alongside scene interaction. The pretrained prior therefore reduces this learning burden during adaptation. As shown in Figure 4(a, b) and Table 4, Ada-BFM converges faster and achieves higher rewards than Ada-BFM (small pretrained), while also attaining higher success rates on both the terrain and object interaction test sets. The results suggest that a higher-capacity BFM pretrained on a more diverse motion dataset provides a stronger foundation for learning humanoid scene interaction. As shown in Figure 4(a, b), Ada-BFM provides a larger improvement over training from scratch in terrain interaction than in object interaction, both in convergence speed and final reward. We attribute this difference in part to the training objectives. Terrain interaction focuses on accurately tracking reference motions, which aligns closely with the pretrained BFM’s objective. Retention. To answer the third question, we evaluate Ada-BFM on the BONES Test Set, with the scene pathway enabled. As shown in Table 5, the terrain- and object-adapted policies exhibit relatively small decreases in success rate. These findings show that Ada-BFM extends the pretrained BFM to humanoid scene interaction while largely preserving its original motion-control capabilities. Retention is stronger after terrain adaptation. As discussed in the efficiency analysis, this pattern is consistent with the alignment of terrain interaction with the pretrained motion-tracking objective, whereas object interaction introduces additional demands beyond tracking. Ada-BFM and Task Tokens preserve motion capabilities well through additional conditioning path-ways with frozen backbones. LoRA instead updates the backbone’s effective weights: it retains much of the pretrained capability after terrain adaptation but suffers severe degradation after object adaptation, alongside low success on held-out object-interaction motions (Figure 3). These results support separate conditioning pathways for balancing interaction learning and capability retention. Figure 5 shows success rate decreases across motion categories in the BONES Test Set, identifying the capabilities most affected by adaptation. Gestures, communication, object manipulation, object interaction, and basic locomotion styles remain well preserved, whereas dancing shows moderate degradation. The largest losses occur in Stunts and Unusual Locomotion, which include flips, rolls, and crawling. Limited coverage of these behaviors during adaptation and altered visual observations under unusual body poses may contribute to their greater degradation. 9Real-world deployment. We deploy the checkpoints adapted for terrain and object interaction on a Unitree G1 equipped with a RealSense D435i depth camera. We adapt a shared model for each interaction domain. All motions and scenes used in the real-world demos are unseen during training. The terrain checkpoint supports stair ascent and descent, jumping down, repeated curb traversal, slope ascent, and obstacle crossing (Figure 1(a–e)). The object checkpoint supports locomotion or rapid turning while lifting a box, lifting objects of varying shapes, materials, and weights, as well as kicking and pushing a box (Figure 1(f–j)). The robot can also lift a box containing more than 3 kg of water. These examples demonstrate that each adapted checkpoint supports multiple interaction behaviors on hardware. The policy can also recover from a misstep. In a curb trial, it steps into empty space, regains its balance, and continues (Figure 6).

We examine the contributions of the adapter components and depth observations using success rates on the terrain and object interaction test sets. 

Adapter components. Table 6 reports test-set success rates after removing either the adapter feed-forward network (FFN) or its attention branch. Both ablations reduce success in both domains, with removing the FFN causing a larger drop than removing attention. The degradation is more pronounced in object interaction. These results demonstrate the contribution of both components. 

Depth observations. To assess the role of depth observations in both domains, we train and eval-uate a zero-depth variant with depth inputs fixed to zero throughout training and deployment. As shown in Table 6, object interaction success drops substantially, consistent with the importance of depth cues for locating objects. Appendix B shows that depth reduces peak tracking errors and im-proves tracking accuracy beyond what the aggregate success rate captures. Experiments show that depth enables smoother terrain interaction and more accurate tracking than the zero-depth baseline. 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented Ada-BFM, a post-training framework for adapting pretrained behavior foundation models to humanoid scene interaction. By incorporating depth observations through a lightweight adapter in a frozen pretrained backbone, Ada-BFM supports diverse tasks with a shared model for each interaction domain. Experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate diverse terrain and object interaction behaviors, while simulation evaluations show faster learning than training from scratch and substantial retention of pretrained motion capabilities. A.1 EVALUATION METRICS AND SUCCESS CRITERIA 

We report success rate (SR) and global and local tracking errors. At each time step, the body tracking error is the mean Euclidean distance between the tracked body links and their reference positions in the global frame. Unless otherwise specified, a motion is successfully tracked if the maximum of this error over the entire sequence does not exceed 0.5 m. This criterion applies to flat-ground tracking and terrain interaction. For object interaction, we additionally require the maximum Euclidean distance between the object and its reference position in the global frame to be at most 

0.4 m over the sequence. Both the body and object position criteria must be satisfied. SR is the fraction of evaluated motions that satisfy the applicable criteria. For the success-rate curves that vary the body tracking threshold, the object position threshold remains fixed at 0.4 m. G-MPKPE and G-MPKRE measure the per-keypoint position and rotation errors of selected links in the global frame, respectively. L-MPKPE and L-MPKRE measure the corresponding root-relative errors after removing horizontal translation offsets and heading differences. Position errors are measured in meters and rotation errors in radians. A.2 DATASET DETAILS 

Terrain interaction. We use the robot–terrain sequences from OmniRetarget and the curb, slope, and stairs sequences from GRAIL. 

Object interaction. We use data retargeted from OMOMO using OmniRetarget. Specifically, we use only sequences involving the largebox object. Both the training and test sets for object interaction are drawn exclusively from this subset. For each interaction domain, we randomly split the clips into 80% for training and 20% for testing. A.3 DEPTH PREPROCESSING 

Following the preprocessing design of Hiking in the Wild (Zhu et al., 2026), both interaction do-mains use a buffer of four depth frames. At each episode reset, we sample a temporal offset from 

{0, 1, 2, 3} frames. We read the corresponding frame at each control step to model sensing latency. Each selected frame is cropped by two pixels per side and resized bilinearly to 18 ×32 . Depth values are clipped to [0 , 2.5] m and normalized to [0 , 1] in both terrain and object interaction. The buffer supplies a delayed observation rather than a stack of depth frames to the encoder. # B ROLE OF DEPTH PERCEPTION IN TERRAIN INTERACTION 

We compare depth-conditioned and zero-depth policies using tracking-error distributions and aver-ages of per-motion peak tracking errors to examine benefits beyond the small terrain success-rate difference in Table 6. The zero-depth policy receives zero depth inputs during training and deploy-ment. 

Peak tracking errors. For each test motion, we compute the maximum frame-wise tracking error over the entire sequence, where the frame-wise error is the mean global position error over the tracked body links. We then report percentiles of these per-motion maxima in Table 11. Without terrain offsets, the median is 0.067 m with depth and 0.072 m with zero depth, a difference of only 

5 mm. In contrast, depth reduces p99 from 0.728 m to 0.426 m, a reduction of approximately 30 cm. Thus, the benefit is concentrated in the tail of the error distribution rather than in its typical values. Visual inspection suggests that the motor capabilities inherited from the pretrained BFM allow the zero-depth policy to recover from some errors caused by mismatches between reference motions and terrain, such as inaccurate foot placement leading to missed steps and stumbles. The depth-conditioned policy largely avoids these errors in the inspected rollouts. This observation suggests why similar success rates can coexist with differences in tracking stability. ±5◦ in yaw. Under these perturbations, depth reduces p90 from 1.547 m to 0.589 m (Table 11).
