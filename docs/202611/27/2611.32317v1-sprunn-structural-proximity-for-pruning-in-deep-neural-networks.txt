A B S T R A C T 

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have demonstrated significant accuracy across various machine learning tasks. However, these networks suffer from a huge number of parameters and computations, which leads to time-consuming processes. Consequently, parameter pruning approaches have been proposed to compress DNNs and efficiently implement them. The main challenge in pruning is selecting the appropriate parameters for pruning without significantly damaging the accuracy. In this paper, we propose SPRUNN, an algorithmic pruning approach based on the structural proximity criterion for filter p ru ning in convolutional layers of deep neural networks. Structural Proximity (SP) calculates filter similarity by considering all filters and comparing each one with the others, effectively identifying less important filters for pruning in the convolutional layers. Furthermore, we integrate this approach with fine-grained pruning in Fully Connected (FC) layers to compress the DNNs that include both convolutional and FC layers. To enhance the accuracy of the pruned model, we propose a learning rate refinement strategy during the retraining phase. Experimental results demonstrate that SPRUNN achieves significant compression with high accuracy across diverse architectures and datasets. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in machine learning tasks using Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are considerable [43]. However, the huge number of parameters in DNNs creates a bottle-neck in processing speed during inference, which leads to time-consuming and high-cost power in transferring data between memories as well as computations. Therefore, DNN compression has been a trending topic in the last decade. Among various compression techniques, pruning has gained considerable attention in recent years due to its effectiveness in accelerating the inference of DNNs [11, 13, 14, 27, 15, 29, 37, 18, 48, 8]. The main challenge in pruning is identifying the weights that can be removed while preserving model accuracy. Con-sider a network with 𝑛 weights. Since each weight can either be retained or pruned, the number of possible pruning con-figurations is 2𝑛 , implying an exponential search complexity for finding the optimal pruning configuration. To address this challenge, most pruning methods rely on criterion-based approaches, where a predefined criterion is used to estimate the importance of weights and identify candidates for re-moval. Some criterion-based methods use the magnitude of the weights to identify candidate weights for pruning [13, 34, 16, 37, 40], while some of them utilize similarity criteria for pruning [14, 29, 52, 42, 25]. In convolutional layers, all filters are convolved with the same input feature maps. Consequently, when some filters are highly similar to others, their removal can often be compensated for by the

Another important challenge in pruning is maintaining the accuracy of the pruned model. To mitigate the accu-racy degradation caused by pruning, several studies have proposed retraining the network after pruning [11, 27, 28, 24, 17]. ## 2. Related works 

Previous works in pruning can be discussed from dif-ferent aspects. In this section, we elaborate on various ap-proaches to pruning. 

2.1. Unstructured and structured pruning 

In unstructured pruning, the candidate weights for prun-ing are removed without considering the model structure, while in structured pruning, all weights in a specific structure are removed [47]. The unstructured approach more precisely prunes less important weights, typically enabling higher pruning rates with minimal accuracy degradation. However, it leads to irregular sparsity and presents challenges for DNN implementation on hardware, where specialized hardware is required. Because of this, the unstructured approach does not significantly improve computational performance unless in high pruning rates [32]. References [11, 37] are some samples of unstructured pruning works. In contrast, struc-tured pruning removes weights in a regular pattern, making it suitable for implementation on DNN hardware accelerators without requiring specialized hardware [34]. However, this approach may inadvertently remove some important con-nections within the structured pattern. Consequently, main-taining model accuracy during structured pruning can be more challenging compared to unstructured pruning. Since structured pruning algorithms are designed based on the network architecture, they may not apply to the various types of networks. References [13, 14, 27, 15, 2, 7, 34, 29, 49, 52, 42, 10, 25, 26, 39] propose filter pruning and References [19, 40, 33] suggest channel pruning. Filter pruning leads to a significant reduction in the num-ber of parameters and floating point operations (FLOPs) in Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs). Since the

number of filters in a given layer determines the number of input channels in the subsequent layer, pruning filters in one layer not only removes those filters but also eliminates the corresponding input channels, thereby decreasing parame-ters in the following layer. Some previous methods focusing on filter or channel pruning, such as [13, 14, 15, 51], do not address pruning in the FC layers, limiting the compression potential of DCNNs that include such layers. In this work, we propose a multi-granularity pruning, in which a coarse-grained structured filter pruning method for convolutional layers is effectively integrated with fine-grained pruning for FC layers, enabling efficient compression of DNNs with FC layers. 

2.2. Criterion-based pruning 

Most pruning methods are criterion-based. In the criterion-based approach, candidate weights for pruning are selected based on their values. The most popular criteria for pruning are computed based on the magnitude and similarity of the weights. In magnitude-based pruning approaches, the belief is that the smallest weights are the best candidates for pruning, while similarity-based approaches concentrate on the similarity measures. The idea behind magnitude-based methods arises from the observation that small weights have less contribution than large weights to accuracy. References [13, 34, 16, 37, 40] suggest magnitude-based pruning. The Soft Filter Pruning (SFP) method [13] prunes filters with smaller 𝑙 𝑝 -norm. Reference [34] suggests a magnitude-based filter pruning, where the importance of each channel throughout the entire network is deter-mined using normalized 𝑙 2-norm. Dynamic Processing Unit (DPU) [16] proposes a dynamic pruning, where candidate weights for pruning are determined based on magnitude. Sparse Optimization Guided Pruning (SOGP) [37] suggests a magnitude-based pruning approach. Instead of directly applying a criterion to specify candidate weights for pruning, SOGP determines these weights in the loss function by introducing fine-grained sparse regularization. References [14, 42, 29, 52, 49, 18, 25] propose a similarity-based pruning. Filter Pruning via Geometric Median (FPGM) [14] suggests pruning redundant filters using the GM as a similarity criterion, in which filters closest to the GM are considered redundant and subsequently pruned. Effective Automatic Channel Pruning (EACP) [29] first clusters filters using k-means++, then preserves the filter with the smallest distance to the cluster center while pruning the remaining fil-ters in each cluster. Progressive Local Filter Pruning (PLFP) [42] introduces a local filter pruning criterion that computes the similarity of a filter with its 𝑘 nearest neighbors, rather than considering all filters. Reference [49] performs filter pruning based on the uniqueness of the feature maps, where feature maps are first transformed into the frequency domain using a Discrete Cosine Transform. Then, feature map uniqueness is computed based on the correlation, and feature maps with low uniqueness, as well as their corresponding filters, are removed. Consecutive Layer Collaborative filter Similarity (CLCS) [52] suggests filter pruning based on the Cosine similarity criterion, measuring the similarity of filters in two consecutive layers rather than within a single layer. Considering the weight-sharing mechanism and the fact that each filter convolves over all input feature maps in convolutional layers, we argue that similarity-based crite-ria are particularly well-suited for filter pruning. However, existing similarity criteria exhibit limitations in effectively identifying truly redundant filters. For instance, methods based on the GM compute similarity globally and prune filters that are closest to a central geometric point. However, the GM criterion may miss subtle critical local structural variations within the filter space. Similarly, Cosine criterion identifies filters with similar directional orientation but may fail to capture differences in magnitude or element-wise dis-tribution. In this paper, to address these limitations, we adopt Crowding Distance from the Nondominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) [3] and introduce a novel similarity criterion, SP, to identify and prune less important filters in convolutional layers. The SP criterion quantifies similarity based on the relative distribution of filter elements, offering a more comprehensive measure of structural redundancy. 

2.3. During-training and post-training pruning 

Some prior works integrate weight pruning into the training process from scratch, so that model optimization and pruning occur simultaneously. Conversely, some works train networks with all the weights initially, and after the unpruned model converges, pruning is performed, followed by retraining to fine-tune the remaining weights. References [13, 50, 2, 16, 7, 9] perform during-training pruning. The SFP method [13] prunes filters from scratch and omits fine-tuning procedure. This method allows pruned filters to be updated in subsequent epochs, preserving the capacity of the original network and potentially enabling pruned weights to gain importance in later epochs of train-ing. StructADMM [50] presents a framework that applies dynamic ADMM regularization during training to overcome the problem of accuracy degradation when increasing the pruning rate for both structured and unstructured pruning approaches. In the FPC method [2], the pruning is conducted during training. First, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is applied to decompose the output of the layer, and then the output feature maps with smaller singular values are removed. Consequently, the filters corresponding to those feature maps are pruned. However, this method is compu-tationally expensive, especially in deep networks. DPU [16] performs weight pruning during training, allowing pruned weights to be updated throughout the training process to re-fine the identification of candidate connections for pruning. References [11, 14, 27, 15, 42] conducted post-training pruning. The approach in [11] involves three key steps: first, training the network until convergence; second, pruning less important weights; and third, fine-tuning until the desired accuracy is achieved. Pruning and retraining are conducted iteratively, with weights smaller than a certain threshold be-ing removed at each stage, followed by retraining the sparse

2.4. Iterative and one-shot pruning 

Pruning methods can be divided into one-shot and it-erative pruning. In iterative pruning, the pruning rate, the number of pruned layers, or both are gradually increased during training or retraining until the desired compression ratio is achieved. In contrast, one-shot pruning removes all less important weights in a single step without gradual pruning. Reference [46] conducts one-shot filter pruning, and References [11, 42, 34, 7, 1] perform iterative pruning. PLFP [42] does not immediately drop the candidate filters; instead, it gradually decreases their impact by de-creasing their values in the subsequent epochs. Moreover, incorrect candidate weights for pruning can be recovered in the next epochs. Loss-Aware Automatic Structured Pruning (LAASP) [7] employs an iterative filter pruning approach. This method suggests a loss-aware pruning strategy. Pro-gressive Fisher-Rao Channel Pruning (PFRCP) [44] adopts a progressive strategy that alternates Fisher re-estimation and structured channel removal with short recovery fine-tuning to mitigate accuracy degradation. Structural Decoupling-Driven Loss-Aware filter pruning (SDDLA) [45] employs a two-level iterative strategy that sequentially prunes layers and progressively removes small groups of filters within each layer. The SFP [13] and Meta-attribute-based Filter Pruning (MFP) [15] methods prune filters in a one-shot manner during each training epoch. However, the pruned filters are allowed to be updated during subsequent training iterations and may therefore become active again in later epochs. As a result, these methods perform pruning in an iterative manner throughout the training process. One-shot pruning utilizes the pruning procedure only once, making it simpler and more computationally efficient compared to iterative pruning, which repeatedly applies the pruning process over multiple stages. While iterative pruning can more effectively mitigate accuracy degradation by carefully pruning weights in stages, it has a greedy nature that might result in the removal of important weights, potentially reducing accuracy. In this work, we adopt one-shot pruning and address any accuracy degradation through retraining and updating the remaining weights. In summary, this paper introduces SP, a novel similarity-based criterion that effectively identifies less important fil-ters by comparing each element within a filter to the corre-sponding elements across all other filters in the same layer. Unlike most existing filter pruning methods, the proposed approach effectively integrates coarse-grained structured fil-ter pruning in convolutional layers with fine-grained weight pruning in FC layers. This combination enables more precise removal of less significant weights in DNNs that include FC layers. Pruning is performed in a post-training, one-shot manner after model convergence, allowing more accurate selection of pruning candidates and eliminating the need for iterative training and pruning cycles. Moreover, it en-ables direct application of the method to pretrained models, thereby reducing computational overhead and facilitating faster deployment in practical applications. Following prun-ing, the model is retrained to update the remaining weights. To further improve accuracy, we propose a learning rate refinement strategy to be used during retraining.

3.1. Structural proximity for filter pruning 

The proposed method introduces a novel similarity-based criterion, SP, for filter pruning in convolutional layers. As illusterated in Fig. 2, filters with smaller SP values are identified as less important and pruned from each convo-lutional layer. The SP criterion evaluates filter similarity by comparing each filter with all others in the same layer. In convolutional layers, where weight-sharing allows all filters to convolve with all input feature maps, removing highly similar filters has minimal impact on accuracy. This is because the remaining diverse filters can sufficiently compensate for the functionality of those that are pruned. The SP criterion is inspired by the Crowding Dis-tance concept from the NSGA-II algorithm [3], a widely used multi-objective evolutionary algorithm. In NSGA-II, Crowding Distance helps maintain population diversity by measuring the distance of each individual to its nearest neighbors across all objective dimensions. This mechanism reduces the chance of selecting individuals that are too simi-lar to others, thus enhancing population diversity. Similarly, in our approach, the SP criterion evaluates filter similarity to identify and eliminate redundant filters, thereby improving the pruning process. SP precisely computes the similarity degree of each filter by comparing it with all other filters. For this purpose, filters in each layer are treated as vectors and sorted according to each element, as shown in Equation (1). Notably, in the following, vector refers to a filter.

3.2. Pruning in fully connected layers 

In FC layers, unlike convolutional layers, weight-sharing is not employed, and each weight is connected to and mul-tiplied by a single input feature. Consequently, pruning based on similarity criteria tends to be less effective. In contrast, unstructured pruning allows for more precise iden-tification of less important weights. Therefore, we adopt a fine-grained, magnitude-based pruning strategy, in which weights with smaller magnitudes, typically those contribut-ing least to model accuracy, are removed. Specifically, we prune 𝑝 FC % of the weights based on their absolute values, as depicted in Fig. 2. 

3.3. Retaining and learning rate Refinement 

SPRUNN conducts pruning after the training and con-vergence of the DNN, ensuring that the optimal weight values are established and enabling the precise identification of candidates for removal. Subsequently, the pruned model is retrained to update the unpruned weights and adapt to the new structure. Typically, the final learning rate from the training phase is used for fine-tuning [35]. However, some previous works, such as [35, 21], have proposed techniques for selecting an optimal learning rate during retraining to achieve higher accuracy. SPRUNN suggests a learning rate refinement ap-proach specifically for the retraining phase, as represented in Fig. 2. Notably, this refinement strategy is defined based on utilizing the final values of the remaining weights from the training phase during retraining. In our approach, the learning rate for retraining the pruned model is set to a value smaller than its initial value during training, denoted as 𝛾 initial retrain < 𝛾 initial train , where 𝛾 rep-resents the learning rate. During training, Learning Rate Annealing is commonly applied [30], where the learning rate is gradually reduced over several epochs. In this situation, the initial learning rate for retraining is determined to be close to the final learning rate of the training phase, while also satisfying the following condition:

Using learning rate refinement, we control the level of exploration to be lower than at the beginning of the unpruned network training, where the weights are randomly initialized. Conversely, since the converged weights of the unpruned network utilize as the initial weights for retraining, employing a smaller learning rate increases exploitation and is more efficient. 4.2.2. Pruning rate 

Selecting different pruning rates for each layer is highly empirical and computationally expensive [26]. Although increasing the pruning rate generally improves the com-pression ratio, it may also lead to a reduction in model accuracy. Consequently, determining appropriate pruning rates requires a trade-off between compression and accuracy. To simplify this process, we employ a fixed pruning rate for all convolutional layers ( 𝑝 conv %) and a separate fixed pruning rate for the FC layers ( 𝑝 FC %), thereby eliminating the need for a computationally expensive layer-wise search for optimal pruning rates. In this work, the pruning rates for convolutional layers are selected based on commonly adopted settings in previous studies, particularly those used by FPGM [14]. For convo-lutional layers, we begin with a pruning rate of 30% and further evaluate pruning rates of 40%, 50% and 60% to assess the behavior of the proposed method under increasing levels of compression. For ResNet-18, ResNet-20, and ResNet-32, pruning rates of 30%, 40%, and 50% are evaluated. For deeper architectures with larger number of parameters, such as ResNet-56, ResNet-110, and MobileNetV2, we addition-ally evaluate a pruning rate of 60%. For LeNet-5 and VGG-16, which contain FC layers that comprise a large proportion of the model parameters, different pruning rates are considered for the FC layers. Since FC layers are generally less sensitive to pruning, as will be detailed in Section 4.7, higher pruning rates of 90%, 95%, and 99% are applied to the FC layers. In all models, the final output layer is not pruned.

## 5. Discussion 

Because filters in a convolutional layer share weights across spatial locations and operate on all input feature maps, removing highly similar filters can have a limited impact on feature representation, as the remaining filters can preserve similar features. However, existing similarity-based pruning criteria often suffer from inherent limitations, which can negatively affect pruning effectiveness and model performance. One commonly used similarity measure is Cosine similarity [49, 52, 1]. Cosine similarity presents a key limitation when applied to filter pruning in CNNs. Two filters may exhibit similar direction in a high Cosine simi-larity score, yet differ substantially in specific element-wise positions, which can significantly affect their functionality in feature extraction. Another similarity criterion, the GM criterion, has also been employed in previous studies [14]. The GM criterion emphasizes overall proximity rather than element-wise similarity, potentially missing specific filter patterns. GM identifies a central point in the filter space by minimizing the sum of Euclidean distances to all other filters. Although this approach captures a notion of global centrality, it may fail to preserve critical structural character-istics. Specifically, it tends to prune central filters, those near the middle of the filter distribution, while potentially ignor-ing clusters of locally similar filters that reside in boundary regions farther from the median. As a result, GM-based pruning may retain redundant filters and inadequately reflect the local diversity needed for optimal feature representation. To address these limitations, we introduce the SP cri-terion. Unlike Cosine similarity, which measures directional alignment between filters, SP considers the relative position-ing of filters within a layer. In contrast to GM, which em-phasizes global centrality based on Euclidean distance, SP represents local similarity and evaluates structural relation-ships across the entire filter set. Specifically, SP computes the element-wise similarity between a target filter and all other filters in the same layer, enabling the identification and preservation of filters that exhibit unique or locally signifi-cant features. This ensures that filters with distinctive char-acteristics are retained, thereby preserving representational diversity and enhancing model performance. Comparison of SP, GM, Cosine, and L2 − norm criteria in Fig. 7 con-firms the effectiveness of the SP criterion for filter pruning and achieving high accuracy. Moreover, the computation of the SP criterion is efficient, with a time complexity of 

𝑂 (𝑛 2 log 𝑛 ), where 𝑛 denotes the number of filters in a layer. SP is a similarity-based criterion and effectively behaves for convolutional layers, however for the FC layers, where structured pruning is typically less effective in achieving high compression efficiency, SP is not suitable. SP is a similarity-based criterion that is effective for convolutional layers. However, it is less suitable for FC layers, where structured pruning is generally less effective at achieving high compression rates. To address this limitation, we adopt a fine-grained magnitude-based pruning for FC layers, enabling the proposed framework to better exploit the different characteristics of convolutional and FC layers. In many existing studies, retraining is typically carried out using fine-tuning, where the final weight values and learning rate from the original training phase are used for initialization [35]. Reference [35] proposes an alternative approach, known as learning rate rewinding, in which re-training begins with a learning rate taken from an earlier epoch in the training schedule, rather than the final one. In this paper, we propose an alternative strategy, learning rate refinement. Based on our experiments, we find that using a learning rate close to the final value of the original training phase yields superior results during retraining. This is likely because the final values of the unpruned weights are utilized as the initial values in the retraining phase, meaning that only minor updates are needed to guide the model toward a new optimal state. The effectiveness of our approach is demonstrated in Fig. 8, where learning rate refinement consistently outperforms both fine-tuning and learning rate rewinding. 

## 6. Conclusion and future Work 

In this work, we proposed SPRUNN, an algorithmic pruning approach that introduces the novel Structural Prox-imity (SP) criterion to identify candidate filters for pruning in convolutional layers. The proposed coarse-grained prun-ing for convolutional layers is effectively integrated with fine-grained pruning for FC layers to achieve comprehensive

model compression. Moreover, SPRUNN suggested an inno-vative strategy, learning rate refinement, which adjusts the initial learning rate during the retraining phase to enhance model accuracy. The experimental results confirm the effec-tiveness of the proposed method, as SPRUNN achieves sub-stantial compression while maintaining high accuracy across diverse DCNN architectures and datasets. Furthermore, the results demonstrate the contribution of each component of SPRUNN individually. For future work, we recommend de-termining the pruning rate based on the type and position of layers in a DCNN. Furthermore, investigating the influence of additional hyperparameters on the accuracy of pruned models during retraining, alongside the learning rate, would be valuable. While the SP criterion was used to assess filter similarity, it could also be extended to other structures within DNNs, such as kernels. In addition, the proposed SPRUNN framework could be implemented on FPGA platforms and evaluated using hardware-oriented metrics, such as latency, throughput, and energy consumption, to further investigate its practical deployment and acceleration capabilities.
