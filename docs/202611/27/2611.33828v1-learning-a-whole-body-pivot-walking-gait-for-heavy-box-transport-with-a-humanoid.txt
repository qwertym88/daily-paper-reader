# Learning a Whole-Body Pivot-Walking Gait for Heavy-Box Transport with a Humanoid 

Rikuto Hidaka 1, Tsung-Yuan Chen 2 and Kento Kawaharazuka 3

Abstract —Through pivot walking, humans can transport objects that are too heavy to lift. Previous humanoid studies have relied on planned sequences in which object manipulation and stepping are executed separately and the object is brought to rest between successive rotations. However, few studies have de-veloped a single learned controller for humanoid pivot walking. In this paper, we propose a single reinforcement-learning policy that jointly coordinates stepping, balance control, and object manipulation. The policy is trained using rewards that promote alternating pivot motions and a phase reference that progresses according to the displacement of the object. In simulation, we confirmed that the humanoid can pivot a box. For deployment, we fine-tuned the policy into an object-blind version that follows a periodic clock instead of the progress reference. On the real robot, the deployed policy transported the box forward by more than 1 m in all five trials; each trial ended at a base-tilt safety stop. These results demonstrate that coordinated humanoid pivot walking can be learned with reinforcement learning and transferred to a physical humanoid robot without direct object-state sensing. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid robots are becoming increasingly capable of dynamic locomotion, yet many useful tasks require moving objects that are too large or heavy to lift. Direct lifting may exceed the robot’s arm capacity, while pushing or dragging can require large sustained forces and substantial sliding at the floor. Humans can instead exploit ground support through pivot walking: the object is rocked between its bottom corners and rotated forward while most of its weight remains sup-ported by the ground. Pivot walking may therefore provide humanoids with a useful transport mode for heavy objects (Fig. 1). Pivoting has been studied extensively as a nonprehensile manipulation primitive, including for humanoid transport (Section II). Existing humanoid systems, however, rely on explicit contact models and planned quasi-static sequences. Object rotation and robot stepping are scheduled as separate phases, and the object is nearly stationary between rotations. These methods demonstrate reliable task-level planning, yet they do not realize pivot walking as a continuously executed

A. Reinforcement Learning for Legged Locomotion 

Reinforcement learning has enabled robust sim-to-real lo-comotion for legged robots [1], [2], while massively parallel simulation has made the training of such policies practical [3]. Periodic conditioning and reward composition have also been used to learn multiple bipedal gaits [4]. We build on this general training framework and consider a coupled loco-manipulation problem rather than command-tracking loco-motion. The policy must coordinate foot placement, balance, and upper-body forces with the rocking dynamics of a heavy external object. 

B. Humanoid Loco-Manipulation 

Humanoid systems have demonstrated constraint-based loco-manipulation [5], whole-body interaction with bulky objects [6], and transport of irregular loads using locomotion priors [7]. These approaches primarily lift, carry, embrace, place, or continuously push objects. In contrast, pivot walking repeatedly changes the object’s supporting ground corner while the robot continues to step. The policy must therefore coordinate locomotion with both contact forces and the object’s alternating rocking motion. 

C. Pivoting and Nonprehensile Manipulation 

Pivoting is a classical nonprehensile manipulation primi-tive in which an object rotates about a supporting environ-mental contact rather than being fully supported by the robot [8]. Prior work has studied the mechanics and planning of pushing [9], whole-body humanoid pushing of large objects [10], [11], and pivot-based reorientation [12]. Most closely related, Yoshida et al. [13], [14] demonstrated humanoid transport through planned quasi-static pivot se-quences, with object manipulation and robot stepping sched-uled as separate phases. Murooka et al. [15] subsequently added online object-state and force estimation to adapt planned humanoid pivoting to motion error and unknown physical parameters. Zhang et al. [16] formulated pivoting-gait modes with graph model predictive control, though on a fixed industrial dual-arm manipulator rather than a walking humanoid. Reinforcement learning has also produced in-hand 

pivoting [17], where a gripper supports the object and the mobile base remains fixed. In contrast, we propose a single whole-body humanoid policy that jointly generates stepping, balance, bimanual interaction, and alternating object rocking, without an explicit contact-mode schedule or a planned settling phase. The contribution is therefore the realization of pivoting as a continuous learned humanoid gait rather than a new pivoting primitive. 

D. Reward Shaping and State-Indexed Motion References 

Complex locomotion and manipulation strategies can emerge from sparse task objectives [18], [19]. In our set-ting, however, the outcome-only training routes tested in Section V-B converge to tilted dragging or unstable pushing rather than alternating pivoting. We therefore use skill-level reward shaping to make the desired object motion reachable during exploration. Unlike potential-based shaping [20], this shaping intentionally changes the task optimum by specify-ing an alternating box-rocking pattern, while leaving joint trajectories, foot placement, contact timing, and interaction forces to be learned. Phase-conditioned locomotion policies commonly use elapsed time or a periodic clock [4]. Conceptually related state-based phase variables appear in virtual-constraint con-trol, where motion is indexed by robot configuration rather than time [21]. We instead index the box-yaw reference by the object’s realized forward progress. The reference therefore pauses when the box stalls and reverses when it moves backward, coupling the desired rocking phase to task progress rather than elapsed time. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

B. Policy Observations and Actions 

At each control step the policy maps an observation to an action, at = πθ (ot). The action sets joint-position targets for all 29 actuated joints as offsets from a nominal pose. The policy runs at 50 Hz and a low-level PD controller tracks the targets. The box-observing training actor receives robot proprioception (joint positions and velocities, base angular velocity, and projected gravity), the previous action, and the box state relative to the robot base. The box state contains the relative position, the sine and cosine of the yaw deviation from the initial heading, and gravity projected into the box frame. Skill-reward training adds a three-dimensional reference-phase observation (Section IV-B). No walking ve-locity command is given. Uniform noise is added to each actor observation term in raw units, for example ±0.05 on the box terms. The critic receives the noise-free state, including variables that the deployment actor cannot observe. We study two actor settings. The box-observing policy above is used for gait learning. The object-blind deployment actor removes all box observations and instead receives a periodic phase signal and the robot yaw from gait onset (Section IV-E). 

C. Episode and Termination 

Each simulation episode lasts 20 s and terminates early if the box tilt exceeds 35 ◦, the robot falls (base orientation more than 70 ◦ from upright), or a foot contacts the box. Foot–box contact is treated as failure because the task is defined as bimanual pivot transport rather than leg-assisted pushing. Episode completion measures consistency across initial box orientations ( ±5◦, one box). Transport distance and strategy-level metrics are evaluated separately, because forward progress alone cannot distinguish alternating-corner pivoting from tilted dragging. The corresponding gait-validity metrics are defined in Section V-A. IV. M ETHOD 

A. Overview 

Our goal is a single whole-body policy that advances a ground-supported box through repeated alternating pivot motions. The policy controls all 29 robot joints and must coordinate box rocking, bimanual side contact, balance, and stepping without an explicit contact-mode schedule. Fig. 3 summarizes the training pipeline. The method has four stages. First, the robot learns to balance while keeping side contact with a tilted, ground-supported box. Second, a temporal yaw reference and box-level skill rewards let it discover an alternating pivot gait at low box mass. Third, the temporal reference is replaced by a spatial reference indexed by realized box progress, and the gait is adapted through a mass curriculum. Finally, box-state observations are removed and the policy is trained further with an internal clock for hardware deployment.

D. Training Curriculum 

Training uses PPO with 4096 parallel environments. The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with hidden di-mensions (512 , 256 , 128) and ELU activations. Each iteration collects 24 policy steps per environment and performs five optimization epochs, with γ = 0 .99 , GAE λ = 0 .95 , and a KL-adaptive learning rate. The policy is trained through four stages, each resuming the previous one. 1) Stance pretraining. At 4 kg , the robot learns to stay balanced while keeping side contact with a tilted, ground-supported box, with progress rewards disabled and box tilt and yaw randomized at reset. 2) Pivot-gait discovery. The temporal reference and skill rewards are activated at 4 kg together with the ampli-tude curriculum. Alternating yaw swings appear within approximately 2000 iterations. 3) Spatial-reference conversion. The temporal phase is replaced by the progress-paced reference of Eq. (4) and training continues. 4) Mass adaptation. The gait is adapted from 4 to 8 and then 14 .9 kg , with a separate continuation to 24 kg . The rewards and reference stay fixed apart from the heavy-box regularization of Section IV-C. We use this staged initialization because direct training at the heavier masses did not produce a stable gait in our runs. 

E. Object-Blind Deployment Transfer 

The policy πpath requires the box pose and the box progress that indexes its phase. Neither is available on our hardware, which has no motion capture or box-mounted sensing. We therefore transfer the gait to an actor that uses onboard measurements only, by modifying the actor input layer and continuing RL training. The box-state columns of the actor’s first layer are removed and all remaining weights are kept. The critic retains the box state as privileged information during continued training. We also append a zero-initialized input carrying the sine and cosine of the robot yaw from gait onset, which reduces slow heading drift. Because box progress is no longer observed, the spatial phase is replaced by a periodic clock, and the spatial-path term is replaced by a capped forward-speed reward. The box velocity in this reward is computed by the simulator, so removing box sensing from the actor does not restrict training. The learned gait has a full yaw cycle of about 1.0 s 

at 14 .9 kg , and for hardware-oriented training we choose a slower 1.4 s clock period to temper the motion. The resulting actor πclock observes joint positions and velocities (29 each), base angular velocity and projected gravity (3 each), the previous action (29), the three-dimensional clock signal, and the robot-yaw pair, 98 scalars in total, all available onboard. Continued training randomizes box mass, box–ground and hand friction, box center of mass, initial box placement, PD gains, and hand-contact geometry. A slow-start factor ramps the rocking amplitude and the speed reward over the first 

6 s of each episode. A final continuation adds symmetry and heading-drift regularization and re-centers the friction randomization on the measured floor. This policy, denoted 

πhw , is used in the physical experiments (Section V-G). V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 

Unless stated otherwise, each simulation result evaluates the deterministic actions of one trained checkpoint for 20 s in 256 parallel environments (128 for the 14 .9 kg path policy). The episodes randomize only the initial box orientation, with yaw, roll, and pitch sampled within ±5◦, not the training seed or the physical parameters. Reported forward travel is accumulated positive travel and can exceed net displacement, and “terminations” counts robot falls, box falls, and foot– box events. Training rewards progress toward a nominal 2 m 

target (Eq. 1). Evaluation instead runs for 20 s without a stop command, so travel beyond 2 m shows gait continuation rather than controlled delivery. Because scalar task metrics cannot separate pivot walking from tilted dragging, we also count amplitude-segmented yaw swings (excursions over 10 ◦ between sign changes) and report the lowest-corner motion proxy, the integrated planar speed of the lowest box corner divided by forward travel. The proxy is near zero for rotation about a stationary corner and near one for pure translation; it does not identify the active contact. For selected checkpoints we therefore replay deterministic trajectories in MuJoCo and measure per-corner box–ground contact forces, from which we report left–right support alternations, the fraction of time one corner carries over 60% of the normal force, and the loaded-corner slide per forward travel. Swings, support alternations, and hardware yaw reversals (Section V-G) use different detectors and are not interchangeable counts. 

B. Does Outcome-Only Training Discover Pivot Walking? 

None of the six outcome-only routes (one at 8 kg , one at 14 .9 kg , four at 24 kg , one training seed each) produces repeated alternating yaw swings. Five routes converge to stable constant-tilt dragging, holding a tilt of 25 ◦–31 ◦ and advancing only 0.18 –0.33 m per episode. The sixth is an unstable deep-tilt push that repeatedly topples the box. These routes show that the gait did not emerge under the tested settings, not that it cannot emerge, and they motivate the explicit pivoting objective. To test this within our pipeline, we train two branches from the same 4 kg stance-pretrained policy for 6000 updates. The only difference is the box-level skill rewards. With them, the robot learns repeated yaw swings (11.4 per episode), although it still often fails at this early stage. Without them, it learns no swings and often ends with a foot touching the box. In this single-seed test, only the branch with skill rewards learned the gait.

All five trials end 12 –22 s after gait onset, when base tilt exceeds 1.0 rad and the controller drops to Passive, after which the robot collapses; no box fall occurred during the preceding gait, and the box toppled in two trials during this triggered ending. The endings are systematic (a same-signed final yaw excursion in all five tracks and the same backward crouching collapse in the side views). The yaw drift is consistent with accumulated phase or heading error in the object-blind controller. However, we did not log joint and IMU data, so we could not identify what makes the base tilt grow. The campaign demonstrates repeatable short-horizon transfer of the gait; sustained delivery, direct measurement of support-corner alternation, and a controlled stop remain undemonstrated. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In summary, we presented a learned whole-body pivot-walking gait that jointly coordinates humanoid stepping, balance, and bimanual manipulation of a ground-supported box. A spatial reference paced by the realized box progress couples the alternating box motion to actual transport during training. The deployed policy instead follows a periodic clock. In the six outcome-only routes we tested, a transport re-ward alone does not produce the gait. With box-level pivoting rewards, the policy transports a 14 .9 kg box by an average of 4.12 m in 20 s over 128 initial box orientations ( ±5◦, one box) with no observed terminations, and contact-force replay of a posture-refined continuation verifies repeated alternation of the dominant support corner. On hardware, an object-blind continuation using only onboard observations reproduces the lean and yaw reversals and advances the box by more than
