Abstract 

LocoMuJoCo is an open-source benchmark for imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) on whole-body control. The library couples MuJoCo (CPU) with MJX and the MjWarp backend (GPU) under a single API, exposes 9 humanoid, 3 (musculo-)skeletal, and 4 quadruped embodiments, and ships a curated native motion-capture dataset for every humanoid, to which it can also retarget AMASS and LAFAN1. A modular plug-in archi-tecture makes observations, rewards, control modes, terrains, and domain randomization fully interchangeable, while compact JAX implementations of many RL and IL algorithms provide JIT-compiled end-to-end training, and the companion loco-mujoco-real repos-itory provides sim-to-real deployment. We describe the design, the SMPL-to-robot and robot-to-robot retargeting pipeline, and a usage example. LocoMuJoCo is released under MIT at https://github.com/robfiras/loco-mujoco .

Keywords: imitation learning, reinforcement learning, whole-body control, humanoid control, JAX, MuJoCo, benchmark

Because humans and humanoid robots share broadly similar embodiments, human motion data provide an abundant source of training demonstrations. 2 Related Work 

Recent humanoid IL/RL libraries each emphasize one strength: HumanoidBench (Sfer-razza et al., 2024) (RL tasks), MyoSuite (Caggiano et al., 2022) (musculoskeletal models), mjlab (Zakka et al., 2026) (Isaac-Lab-style API on MuJoCo-Warp), ProtoMotions (Tessler et al., 2025) (GPU motion tracking with retargeting), and MimicKit (Peng, 2025) (motion-imitation framework). None combines a single API spanning a serial NumPy and a parallel JAX backend, automated retargeting onto any registered robot, biomechanical models, trajectory-similarity metrics, and a sim-to-real pipeline (Table 1; Appendix A). General-purpose GPU frameworks such as Brax (Freeman et al., 2021) and MuJoCo Playground (Za-kka et al., 2025) share our simulation stack but provide no imitation-learning layer. 

3 The LocoMuJoCo Library 

3.1 Dual Environment Backends 

LocoMuJoCo executes each environment through either a serial NumPy/MuJoCo backend or a vectorized JAX/MJX backend. Environment components—including observations, rewards, controls, and termination conditions—are implemented once against a numerical-backend argument. Because jax.numpy closely mirrors NumPy, most code is shared verbatim and only backend-specific operations branch, avoiding duplicate environment logic. Loco-MuJoCo can additionally use MjWarp via use mjwarp=True , which reaches up to 7 .3 × 10 5

steps per second on one consumer GPU, on par with mjlab and MuJoCo Playground on their own tasks (Appendix G).

3.2 Modular Architecture 

The simulation core ( loco mujoco/core ) is decomposed into interchangeable plug-ins for observations, rewards, control functions (direct motor or muscle actuation, PD), domain randomization (gravity, friction, mass, joint dynamics, PD gains, observation noise), terrain (flat, rough heightfield), and initial/terminal state handlers. Plug-ins are stateless: each keeps its per-environment state in an explicit Flax dataclass that is passed through the step, so the entire environment step is JIT-compilable and nothing lives in hidden global state. The same training loop therefore runs across robots and across RL and IL (Appendix B). 

3.3 Embodiments, Datasets, and Retargeting 

LocoMuJoCo ships 9 humanoid robots (Unitree H1/H1v2/G1, Atlas, Talos, Apollo, Booster T1, ToddlerBot, Fourier GR1T2), 3 (musculo-)skeletal SMPL-derived human mod-els ( MyoSkeleton , SkeletonTorque , SkeletonMuscle ), and 4 quadrupeds (Unitree A1/Go2, BD Spot, ANYmal C). Three dataset sources are integrated: a native, retargeted Loco-MuJoCo dataset hosted on the HuggingFace Hub, the LAFAN1 motion-capture collection, and the AMASS aggregate (the latter requires the user to accept the SMPL license). The companion loco-mujoco-real repository adds real-robot environments for the Uni-tree G1 and the AgiBot X2 Ultra, which also serve as worked examples of adding a new robot to LocoMuJoCo, including its SMPL retargeting configuration. The SMPL-to-robot and robot-to-robot procedures are detailed in Appendix D; they map human motion to robots and transfer it across embodiments without manual per-trajectory annotation.

Appendix A. Detailed Comparison to Related Work 

This appendix expands the per-benchmark commentary that the main-text related work (Section 2) summarized in a single paragraph. We adopt the comparison axes of Table 1 and add context that the page-budget of the main text could not accommodate. 

HumanoidBench (Sferrazza et al., 2024). HumanoidBench is a CPU-MuJoCo bench-mark of 27 whole-body manipulation and locomotion tasks designed around the Unitree H1 with dexterous hands and the Agility Digit lower body. Its primary contribution is a task-design suite covering long-horizon and contact-rich behavior; training is RL-only and no expert demonstrations are shipped, so the suite cannot be used for IL out-of-the-box. The simulator is the standard CPU MuJoCo binding, with no JAX backend and no built-in GPU parallelism, which limits throughput at the high seed counts our benchmark targets. 

MyoSuite (Caggiano et al., 2022). MyoSuite ships high-fidelity musculoskeletal mod-els of the human body including a full muscle-actuated humanoid skeleton. It is a strong peer for the biomechanics axis of Table 1 — and indeed LocoMuJoCo’s MjxMyoSkeleton 

environment reuses the MyoSkeleton model under MyoSuite’s license — but the suite is RL-only, provides no expert IL data, no retargeting pipeline, and no GPU/JAX backend. It also does not address sim-to-real transfer. 

mjlab (Zakka et al., 2026). mjlab adopts the manager-based environment interface of Isaac Lab, trains with RSL-RL (Schwarke et al., 2025), and uses MuJoCo-Warp for GPU-accelerated simulation. The project ships reference velocity-tracking, motion-imitation, and manipulation tasks. Compared with LocoMuJoCo, mjlab is GPU-only (no serial NumPy backend at parity), does not provide a curated human-mocap dataset suite, does not include automated cross-robot retargeting, and has no biomechanical models. The environment design of our companion loco-mujoco-real repository closely follows mjlab’s G1 motion-tracking setup, which is explicitly positioned for sim-to-real RL. 

ProtoMotions (Tessler et al., 2025). ProtoMotions is a GPU-accelerated motion-tracking framework with built-in human-to-robot retargeting (PyRoki-based (Kim et al., 2025)) and supports multiple GPU simulators (IsaacGym, Isaac Lab, Genesis, NVIDIA Newton). It demonstrates zero-shot transfer of a single tracking policy onto the Unitree G1. The library is closer to a software toolkit than a benchmark — there is neither a curated, frozen dataset suite nor a shared evaluation protocol with explicit trajectory-similarity metrics. 

> 5Al-Hafez et al.

MimicKit (Peng, 2025). MimicKit unifies three tracking-style algorithms — Deep-Mimic, AMP, and ADD — into a single modular RL framework targeted at physics-based character animation. As a methods toolkit it is largely complementary to a benchmark such as LocoMuJoCo; the differences relevant to Table 1 are that MimicKit ships no cu-rated robot or dataset suite, no biomechanical models, no built-in trajectory metrics, and no sim-to-real instrumentation, and it is not designed for the parallel-JAX simulation regime that drives LocoMuJoCo’s training throughput. 

General-purpose GPU frameworks. Brax (Freeman et al., 2021) and MuJoCo Play-ground (Zakka et al., 2025) share LocoMuJoCo’s simulation stack but are not imitation-learning libraries, which is why they are not part of Table 1. Brax provides JAX physics pipelines and RL training for a set of continuous-control tasks. The original release of MuJoCo Playground is a suite of MJX environments for control, locomotion, and manipu-lation, trained in JAX and deployed on several robots; its current version moved to PyTorch and MuJoCo Warp and adds a whole-body motion-tracking task for the Unitree G1, which loads motions retargeted by external tools. Neither provides motion datasets or a retarget-ing pipeline of its own. LocoMuJoCo builds on MJX and MuJoCo Warp as well and adds the imitation-specific layer on top: datasets for every embodiment, retargeting, imitation algorithms, and trajectory-similarity metrics. 

Summary. The picks above are the libraries researchers in humanoid IL/RL today bench-mark against. They are individually strong on one or two of the five axes in Table 1, but none simultaneously covers the combination of dual-backend simulation, automated mocap retargeting onto any registered robot, biomechanical models, JAX-native trajectory metrics, and the companion loco-mujoco-real deployment pipeline that LocoMuJoCo provides. 

Retargeting pipelines. Our retargeting builds on the SMPL-fitting approach of PHC and H2O (Luo et al., 2023; He et al., 2024), which targets individual robots, and extends it into a general, configuration-driven pipeline: adding a robot only requires a configuration file and 15 mimic sites in its model, and trajectories transfer between robots. To the best of our knowledge, it was the first open-source pipeline of this generality, introduced in LocoMuJoCo v1.0.1; 1 earlier code, including ProtoMotions (Tessler et al., 2025), hard-coded the mappings for one or two robots. Later work builds on similar ideas: GMR (Ara´ ujo et al., 2026) uses non-uniform scaling and differential inverse kinematics instead of a shape fit, targeting real-time use and fewer artifacts, while OmniRetarget (Yang et al., 2026) preserves contacts with objects and terrain. LocoMuJoCo instead fits SMPL to each robot once, tracks the resulting SE (3) targets with soft MuJoCo constraints, and reuses the same mapping for robot-to-robot transfer. 

Foundational components. LocoMuJoCo builds on several open-source software and data assets without which the library could not exist. The simulation backbone is the MuJoCo physics engine (Todorov et al., 2012) and its JAX-vectorized counterpart MJX. The retargeting pipeline targets the SMPL parametric human body model (Loper et al., 2015), with human-mocap data drawn from the AMASS unified corpus (Mahmood et al., 2019) and the Ubisoft LaForge LAFAN1 animation dataset (Harvey et al., 2020). Appendix B. Modular Architecture 

The headline architectural decision of LocoMuJoCo is that everything that varies across robots, tasks, and research questions is a plug-in. Section 3.2 lists the slots; this appendix explains why each one is a slot and how they compose into the two canonical use cases of the library — pure RL for legged locomotion and RL-based IL from mocap. 

Why plug-ins. Robots in LocoMuJoCo differ in degrees of freedom (Apollo’s hands alone add dozens of DoF over a stripped-down H1), in actuator type (torque vs. PD vs. muscle), and in the observation channels exposed to the policy (e.g. proprioception alone vs. pro-prioception with foot contacts). Hard-coding any of these would force one Env class per (robot, task) pair and would block the very cross-embodiment experiments that motivate the library. Instead, each environment is parameterized by a small set of orthogonal com-ponents: 

• Observations. A list of named observation specs is composed at construction time; each spec returns a fixed-shape vector slice (joint positions, joint velocities, base ori-entation, mimic-site positions, contact forces, . . . ), and the environment concatenates them into the policy input. This is what makes the same training script work on every embodiment despite their different observation dimensionalities. 

• Rewards. The shipped reward classes range from NoReward (pure dynamics) and locomotion-style shaping (TargetXVelocityReward , TargetVelocityGoalReward ,

> LocomotionReward

, TargetVelocityTrajReward ) to imitation-style MimicReward , which scores tracking of the relative-site quantities described in Appendix H. Switching from velocity tracking to motion imitation is a single argument change. 

• Domain randomization. The default randomizer has independent switches for gravity, per-geom friction / stiffness / damping, link masses, the root center-of-mass offset, per-DoF joint friction loss / damping / armature, PD gains, and observation noise. The sampled parameters are stored in a Flax dataclass, so they live in explicit JIT-visible state rather than mutable Python globals. 

• Initial-state distribution. Resetting can use the model’s default or a user-specified configuration, a uniform sample over a loaded mocap clip (state-of-the-art for DeepMimic-style training), or from a user-defined sampler. The same hook is what lets us reset to a particular phase of a clip during evaluation when computing trajectory-similarity metrics. 

• Termination conditions. The terminal-state handler is selected by name: no ter-mination, a root-height range, a root height and orientation range derived from the dataset, or a bound on the root’s height and tilt error with respect to the current reference frame. Pure-RL setups typically terminate on a fall only; imitation setups also terminate when the policy strays too far from the reference pose, since this is what produces the truncated rollouts visible in the per-clip evaluation metrics. 

> 7Al-Hafez et al.

• Control mode. DefaultControl , which passes the action directly to the model’s actuators (motors or muscles), and PDControl define the action space exposed to the policy. The choice changes both the learning problem and the action dimensional-ity without touching the policy network or training loop. Torque control is high-bandwidth and stiff. PD control supplies lower-bandwidth reference targets and is commonly deployed in sim-to-real legged systems, whereas muscle actuation supports biomechanics studies on the SMPL-derived skeletal models. The training procedure for the muscle-actuated (musculo-)skeletal models follows Geiß et al. (2024). 

• Terrain. Terrain plug-ins (flat, or a rough heightfield regenerated at reset) modify the world geometry independently of every other slot. This decoupling matters because terrain curricula and rough-ground evaluation are routine in legged-robot research, and LocoMuJoCo lets the user keep everything else fixed — observations, rewards, datasets, randomization — while swapping the ground the robot walks on, so terrain effects are not confounded with changes to the training setup. 

Why this scales. Because every plug-in is stateless and keeps its per-environment state in a Flax dataclass, the entire environment step compiles cleanly under jax.jit and jax.vmap .A practitioner studying domain randomization on Booster T1, an IL researcher comparing GAIL and DeepMimic on Apollo, and a biomechanics-leaning user training muscle policies on MjxSkeletonMuscle all share the same training loop and metric pipeline. LocoMuJoCo’s task factories assemble each robot-task pair from the selected plug-ins, so these use cases differ only in configuration. The experiments in this paper (Appendix H) — covering algorithm and embodiment comparisons — are themselves an example of this scalability: every cell in every one of them is the same env core under different plug-in choices. 

Embodiment provenance and body scaling. The Atlas environment is adapted from Al-Hafez et al. (2023a). Beyond the rigid robots, the (musculo-)skeletal models (SkeletonTorque , SkeletonMuscle ) support body scaling: the same skeleton can be instan-tiated at different anthropometric sizes without any change to the observation, reward, or training pipeline — something the fixed-geometry robot embodiments do not permit. This was implemented to support research on different human sizes, following Al-Hafez et al. (2024) and Badie et al. (2025).

Appendix E. Algorithms 

LocoMuJoCo ships a suite of compact JAX/Flax learning algorithms spanning pure RL and IL. Each algorithm is implemented in one module, <algo> jax.py , in the package  

> loco mujoco/algorithms/

(the additional algorithms are in its experimental subpackage) on top of a small set of shared networks and base classes, with the training loop and policy update fused into a single JIT-compiled function built from lax.scan so that environment stepping, advantage computation, and gradient updates execute on-device without Python overhead. The following short summaries describe what each algorithm does and the role it plays in the benchmark. 

E.1 Core algorithms PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). Proximal Policy Optimization is an on-policy actor-critic policy-gradient method that improves the policy by maximizing a clipped surrogate objective, bounding how much the policy can shift per update. PPO is the default pure-RL backbone in LocoMuJoCo and is also reused inside the imitation algorithms below as the policy optimizer when they need an inner-loop policy update. It also trains data-parallel on several GPUs: each device steps an equal share of the environments and the gradients are averaged across devices, as in the training script of loco-mujoco-real .

GAIL (Ho and Ermon, 2016). Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning trains a dis-criminator to distinguish expert state(-action) pairs from policy-generated ones and uses the log-discriminator output as the reward signal for the policy (optimized by PPO in our im-plementation). GAIL is useful in IL settings where expert demonstrations are available but a hand-crafted task reward is not, and where exact frame-by-frame tracking is unnecessary. 

AMP (Peng et al., 2021). Adversarial Motion Priors combines a GAIL-style discrim-inator on state transitions (encoding motion style) with an additive user-supplied task re-ward (e.g. velocity tracking). The agent learns to pursue the task while staying within the distribution of stylistic motion shown in the reference dataset. AMP is the recommended algorithm for stylized RL on LocoMuJoCo’s humanoid envs. 

DeepMimic (Peng et al., 2018). DeepMimic is a tracking-style IL method that rewards the policy, at every step, for staying close to a reference motion clip. LocoMuJoCo trains it with PPO and the MimicReward class, a DeepMimic-style reward over joint positions and velocities and the root-relative positions, orientations, and velocities of the mimic sites; it is the default IL training signal for the curated humanoid mocap clips. 

SuperMimic (Liao et al., 2026). A model-based motion-tracking method: a world model is fitted to simulator transitions, and the deterministic policy is improved by back-propagating through short imagined rollouts of that model, with a TD( λ) critic bootstrap-ping the tail. In our implementation an imagined step is an ordinary environment step in which the learned dynamics replace the simulator, so observations, rewards, and ter-minations are the environment’s own functions and the algorithm is independent of the robot, the observation, and the reward. Appendix H.4 reports results on the two real-robot environments. 

> 14 LocoMuJoCo

E.2 Additional algorithms SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018). Soft Actor-Critic is an off-policy maximum-entropy actor-critic that simultaneously maximizes expected return and policy entropy. It is typi-cally more sample-efficient than PPO on continuous control and is offered as an off-policy alternative for users with a tighter sample budget. 

TD3 (Fujimoto et al., 2018). Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient is an off-policy deterministic actor-critic that mitigates value-function over-estimation via twin critics, target-policy smoothing, and delayed policy updates. Provided as a deterministic-policy counterpoint to SAC. 

S2PG (Al-Hafez et al., 2024). Stochastic Stateful Policy Gradient splits a stateful policy into a stochastic policy-state transition kernel and a stateless policy, and trains both without backpropagation through time. This avoids the gradient instability of full BPTT while still letting the policy exploit memory. Useful for partially-observable variants of the locomotion tasks. 

BPTT-PPO. A PPO variant that backpropagates the policy-gradient signal through an unrolled time horizon, supporting fully recurrent (LSTM/GRU) policies. The training loop unrolls Tbptt steps in lax.scan per gradient step. Useful when the task genuinely requires long-range memory. 

History-aware PPO. A PPO variant whose observation is augmented with a fixed-length stack of past observations and actions, encoded either by an MLP or by a Transformer block (multi-head self-attention with layer norm). This exposes short-horizon context to the policy without the optimization cost of full recurrence and is a pragmatic middle ground between standard PPO and BPTT-PPO when memory needs are moderate. 

DAgger Distillation. Dataset Aggregation implements teacher–student distilla-tion (Ross et al., 2011). At rollout time each parallel env independently picks the student or the teacher with probability β (sticky for a random number of steps), but every transition is labeled with the teacher’s action on the visited observation. The student is then trained supervised — minibatch NLL of the student Gaussian at the stored teacher action. This is LocoMuJoCo’s default distillation pipeline, used to compress per-clip or per-embodiment PPO specialists into a single multi-task generalist. 

Appendix F. Testing and Continuous Integration 

LocoMuJoCo ships a pytest suite of about 700 tests that covers the environments on both backends, the observation, reward, control, domain-randomization, and termination compo-nents, the trajectory and dataset pipeline, the retargeting pipeline, the rendering and export tools, and a short training run of every included algorithm. Trajectory-similarity measures. Return and episode length say how long a policy sur-vives, not how closely it follows the reference motion. LocoMuJoCo therefore compares each evaluation rollout with the reference clip it tracks, as two time series of one kinematic quantity, using three measures from the metrx library (Pompetzki et al., 2024). The Eu-clidean measure is the mean Euclidean distance between rollout and reference at the same time step, so it also penalizes a motion that is correct but early or late. Dynamic time warping (Sakoe and Chiba, 1978) first aligns the two series with a monotone time warp and sums the distances along the best alignment, which makes it insensitive to such timing offsets. The discrete Fr´ echet distance (Eiter and Mannila, 1994) uses the same kind of alignment but takes the largest distance along it instead of the sum, so it reports the worst deviation of the rollout rather than the average one. All three are JIT-compiled, evaluated in parallel over the evaluation environments, and averaged over rollouts; they are logged during training next to the return (Figure 8) and reported at the end of training (Figure 6, bottom).

Domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer. Because these policies are meant to run on hardware, training relies heavily on domain randomization, all of it built from LocoMuJoCo’s domain-randomization plug-in and identical for the two robots. It targets sensing and disturbances rather than the simulated plant: (i) random pushes, a velocity impulse on the root of up to 0.5 m/s horizontally and 0.78 rad/s in yaw every 1–3 s; (ii) a per-episode encoder bias of up to ±0.02 rad on the measured joint positions; (iii) uniform noise on the measured joint positions ( ±0.01 rad), joint velocities ( ±0.5 rad/s), and base angular velocity ( ±0.2 rad/s); (iv) a friction coefficient drawn from [0 .3, 1.2] for all colliding robot geometries; and (v) an offset of the pelvis center of mass of up to 5 cm per axis. Noise and bias are applied only to the actor’s observations, while the critic receives the clean values. The evaluation in Table 5 switches all of this off, so it measures tracking quality rather than robustness.

From checkpoint to robot. loco-mujoco-real exports a checkpoint to a bundle : the network weights, the controller settings, and the reference motions, which run with NumPy and MuJoCo alone. Three checks precede hardware. A validation script confirms that the bundle reproduces the observations and actions of the training environment term by term, and a rollout in plain MuJoCo tests the export. In sim-to-sim, the controller and a simulated robot then run as separate processes over the robot’s own middleware (AgiBot’s AimDK on ROS 2, or the Unitree SDK), so the controller already acts on delayed state. 

Control loop. The policy runs at 50 Hz. Its observation uses onboard sensing only: joint positions and velocities from the encoders, projected gravity and angular velocity from the pelvis IMU, the previous action, and the reference frame to track. There is no base-state estimate and no external tracking. Actions are joint-position targets, tracked by the drives’ own PD loop with the stiffness and damping of the simulated position servos. On the X2 Ultra, a policy step takes about 2 ms on state that is about 2 ms old; the controller runs on a laptop over Ethernet or on the robot’s own computer, and damps the robot on stale state, a joint past its limits, or a pelvis tilt beyond about 60 ◦.

Hardware run. Figure 11 shows simulation and robot side by side for the motion of Fig-ure 2, a 43 s routine of chained BONES-SEED (Bones Studio, 2026) dance clips. The policy is not one of Table 5: SuperMimic trained it in the same environment on all of BONES-SEED, where it completes 99.9 % of the clips in simulation after 4 .56 × 10 8 environment steps.
