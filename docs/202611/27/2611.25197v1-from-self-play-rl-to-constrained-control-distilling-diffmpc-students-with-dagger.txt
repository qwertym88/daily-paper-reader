Abstract 

Self-play reinforcement learning (RL) produces strong driving policies, but these policies are hard to verify or interpret. Differentiable model predictive control (DiffMPC) can enforce explicit con-straints, but it is usually trained by imitating logged data or with costly RL. To add constraints to an already trained policy, we keep a PPO teacher’s backbone and replace its action head with a DiffMPC that enforces road boundary half-planes and agent ellipses. We then distill the student with DAgger in the teacher’s self-play simulator. Over 9 seeds, the constrained student of a smaller teacher matches its teacher on nuPlan and beats it on CARLA and on out-of-distribution Waymo scenes. For a stronger teacher, the student has fewer failures on nuPlan and Waymo, while staying level on CARLA. Its distillation takes no more than 30% of the teacher’s training time. 

Keywords: Differentiable model predictive control, policy distillation, DAgger, self-play rein-forcement learning, autonomous driving, safety constraints 

1. Introduction 

Machine learning for robotics has made fast progress in recent years (Tang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024). While earlier work mostly relied on imitation learning (IL) (Le Mero et al., 2022), reinforce-ment learning (RL) for training and post-training has gained traction (Kiran et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; Kaufmann et al., 2023). Fast simulators such as PufferDrive (Cornelisse et al., 2026; Rowe et al., 2026) train neural driving policies over millions of kilometers in self-play and reach super-human performance (Cusumano-Towner et al., 2025). Vision-based end-to-end policies follow the same trend. They can be trained with RL only (Yin et al., 2026), post-trained with RL (Li et al., 2026), or distilled from RL-trained policies (Col et al., 2026). However, a main limitation of such pure end-to-end policies is that they are hard to verify and interpret. In safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving, safety during training, veri-fiability, and interpretability are all important (Chen et al., 2024; Brunke et al., 2022). Motivated by these challenges, a line of work in robotics research has combined learning with classic optimal control methods, such as predictive safety filters (PSFs) (Wabersich and Zeilinger, 2018) and differ-entiable model predictive control (DiffMPC) (Amos et al., 2018). These methods enforce explicit, checkable constraints. When the constraints are computed independently of the policy, e.g., from the road and other agents, they add redundancy to the system and can help in scenes the policy has not seen. They also make decisions interpretable: a failure can be traced back to the constraints, the cost function, or the targets. In this paper, our goal is to add safety constraints to an already trained, strong end-to-end policy without losing its performance. We propose to leverage DiffMPC for this goal, as it can be naturally

integrated as the action head of an end-to-end policy. DiffMPC is trained jointly with the policy, so the policy learns with the constraints. So far, DiffMPC policies have mostly been trained with imitation learning or behavior cloning on demonstrations (Huang et al., 2024; Karkus et al., 2023; Gurumurthy et al., 2025). More recent work trains DiffMPCs with RL, but this is either expen-sive (Romero et al., 2026) or has so far been demonstrated only in small navigation tasks with a sin-gle controlled robot and known obstacle motion (Wu et al., 2026). In this work, we propose to distill 

a DiffMPC student from an existing RL-trained end-to-end policy to overcome these limitations. We focus our study on autonomous driving, as it has a clear use case for verifiable constraints and well-developed simulators. Our approach, however, is not limited to autonomous driving and can be transferred to other robotics control and learning tasks. We train our policies in Puffer-Drive (Rowe et al., 2026) on maps and scenes from CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017) and nu-Plan (Caesar et al., 2021), and evaluate them on CARLA, nuPlan, and the Waymo Open Dataset (Et-tinger et al., 2021). Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose a pipeline that adds a DiffMPC layer to a trained end-to-end policy. Specifically, first, we train a neural teacher policy with PPO in self-play. Second, we keep its backbone and replace its action head with a constrained DiffMPC. Third, we distill the new architecture from the teacher with DAgger. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale distillation of a DiffMPC student from an end-to-end teacher in self-play. • We propose a constraint formulation that makes this distillation work: fixed-sum cost weights with learned node weights, route-anchored road boundary constraints, and agent ellipses. • We evaluate the pipeline on CARLA, nuPlan, and Waymo scenes with two teacher sizes and nine seeds per policy. The constrained students match or beat their teachers on every test set while adding explicit constraints, including on the out-of-distribution Waymo scenes, where they leave the road 16–26% less often than their teachers. For the larger teacher, distillation adds no more than 30% to its training time. Our code, along with videos of driving scenarios, is available at https://github.com/ lucahart/selfplay-to-mpc .

2. Related work 

Amos et al. (2018) made differentiable MPC scalable, and it has since gained traction in the robotics community (Hewing et al., 2020; Reiter et al., 2025). In our application of autonomous driving, there is a broad literature on policies trained through IL, including Huang et al. (2024); Karkus et al. (2023). DAgger distillation (Ross et al., 2011) improves other distillation methods, such as behavior cloning (Bain and Sammut, 1995), by allowing the distilled student policy to drive itself. DiffMPC RL training and imitation learning are being enabled by the latest advancements in DiffMPC solvers that can run on GPUs. Adabag et al. (2025) showed large speedups for batched solving, and Bravo-Palacios et al. (2026) scaled to long horizons. Hose et al. (2026) further im-proved solving performance for batches on the GPU, making it a perfect fit for large-scale training. The use of half-planes and ellipse constraints for MPC in autonomous driving has been covered in (Schwarting et al., 2018; Liniger et al., 2015). Recent DiffMPC setups with constraints include

Zhang et al. (2026) with track and actuator bounds, and Rickenbach et al. (2025); Trisovic et al. (2026) with track boundary constraints. Last, we discuss the literature most closely related to our work. Rickenbach et al. (2025) use imitation learning to train a short-horizon DiffMPC to match a long-horizon MPC. Romero et al. (2026) train a DiffMPC policy with actor-critic RL, and report significant training costs. Closest to our constraint formulation, Wu et al. (2026) add half-space state constraints with bounded slack to an actor-critic DiffMPC and learn its cost parameters, demonstrated on navigation of a single controlled robot among moving obstacles with known motion. This work is built upon and inspired by many of the mentioned papers. Unlike the above works, we (1) distill a DiffMPC student from an end-to-end RL teacher with DAgger, (2) train both in multi-agent autonomous-driving self-play, and (3) treat the constrained DiffMPC as a post-training step that adds redundancy to an existing policy. 

3. Preliminaries 

3.1. End-to-end policies 

We refer to end-to-end policies as networks that have been trained as a whole, from raw data inputs to final output commands. A common end-to-end policy is a feed-forward neural network, also referred to as an MLP. Such networks have shown great popularity compared to policies with sepa-rately trained components (Yurtsever et al., 2020), as the communication between components does not need to be designed by hand. End-to-end policies commonly consist of three main components: (i) an encoder, that takes the observation and outputs a latent space representation; (ii) a backbone, that represents the learned heart of the policy; and (iii) a decoder, that turns the latent space into a trajectory or control actions. There are three common ways to train an end-to-end policy: (1) Imitation learning (IL) trains a policy to imitate, e.g., human driving data. It requires no simulation and is trained with a fixed set of labels. This method suffers from poor generalizability and quickly fails in closed-loop simulation or real-world deployment (Rowe et al., 2026). (2) Reinforcement learning (RL) uses a simulation, where a policy can explore different states and learn by its actions. It is often used as a post-training step to improve a policy that was pre-trained with IL (Li et al., 2026). RL, however, often requires many steps when training a policy, making it expensive in many scenarios (Romero et al., 2026). (3) Distillation uses a policy that has already been trained as a teacher. The teacher generates labels that a student policy consumes (Hinton et al., 2015). Recent work has started using distillation for other applications than reducing model size. Col et al. (2026) first trained a model with RL in vector-space. This allowed them to achieve super-human performance at low computational cost (Cusumano-Towner et al., 2025). Afterwards, Col et al. (2026) distilled an end-to-end policy with vision encoder from the vector-space policy, achiev-ing high performance at significantly less compute. In our paper, in the context of autonomous driving, we distill by driving agents through a closed-loop simulation. At each step, the teacher generates a control, that the student uses as a label for its

We distinguish between behavior cloning (BC) (Bain and Sammut, 1995), where the teacher drives all agents at all times, and DAgger (Ross et al., 2011), where over the course of the training, students are chosen to drive agents with increasing probability 1 − pteacher . DAgger starts with BC, has a transition phase from n1,DAgger to n2,DAgger , and finally lets all agents be driven by students. 3.3. Differentiable MPC 

MPC assumes full knowledge of the system. This is often sufficiently accurate for vehicle dynamics and control limits, yet it becomes challenging when choosing tracking targets or a cost function. Amos et al. (2018) showed that an MPC can be differentiated through, using its KKT conditions and the implicit function theorem. This allows us to use differentiable MPC (DiffMPC) as an action head in end-to-end policies, where an MPC adapter converts the backbone’s hidden states to costs 

qk and targets ¯τ .This approach requires many samples to train an end-to-end policy in simulation. Hose et al. (2026) introduce WarpMPC, which can solve large batches of optimization problems on the GPU. WarpMPC is capable of computing forward passes to solve our MPC, and backward passes to train our end-to-end policy. Note that while WarpMPC significantly reduces the computational cost of solving large MPC batches, it remains more expensive than a simple MLP forward pass. Detailed differences in training time can be found in Appendix C. We refer to the entire end-to-end policy with DiffMPC action head as a DiffMPC policy . We use the unconstrained DiffMPC policy to show that DiffMPC policies match an MLP’s expressiveness. The constrained DiffMPC policy, as discussed in Section 4.2, is the main finding of our work. 

4. Methods 

Recall that our ultimate goal is to add verifiable constraints to a strong end-to-end driving policy without retraining it from scratch and without losing its performance. Our proposed pipeline to achieve this has three steps. First, we train an end-to-end teacher policy with RL in PufferDrive. Second, we transfer the teacher’s encoder and backbone to the student, and replace the teacher’s action head with an MPC adapter and DiffMPC. Third, we distill the new architecture from the teacher with DAgger. Figure 1 depicts a schematic overview of the second and third steps. We note that our DiffMPC policy is not intended to compete with the original end-to-end policy, but rather is a method to add constraints after training through an action head change and consecutive fine-tuning step. This keeps the training cost far below RL-only approaches. Section 4.1 describes the constraints we use in our constrained DiffMPC formulation. Section 4.2 details the constrained DiffMPC setup, followed by Section 4.3, which describes the MPC adapter.

4.2. Constrained DiffMPC 

We extend the unconstrained MPC (1) with the road boundary constraints and agent ellipses from (2) and (3), respectively. During operation, agents face scenarios where they cannot avoid violating constraints. This can happen before a crash or because of incorrectly set constraints. To avoid losing such gradients during training, we use slack variables. Slack variables become positive once a constraint is violated and must be higher penalized than the remaining cost term at a node. In the constrained MPC formulation, we use a normalized cost ˜qk, such that 1⊤ ˜qk = 1 . The quadratic cost-term becomes 12 if all values in τ are at their per-unit maximum of 1. A linear slack penalty is exact, i.e., the MPC keeps the constraints whenever it can, if its price exceeds the constraints’ Lagrange multipliers (Kerrigan and Maciejowski, 2000). Fixing the sum of the tracking weights keeps these multipliers bounded, so a fixed slack price stays above them in practice. Normalizing the cost has the unwanted consequence that all nodes along the MPC’s trajectory are weighted equally. This is an unwanted constraint that limits the network’s freedom. Thus, we introduce the node weight κk, which scales tracking and slack costs equally to preserve their relative weight, but allows the network to choose different weights along the MPC horizon. For the small policy, they are on par on CARLA and slightly worse on nuPlan. Thus, we conclude that the MPC head is expressive enough to replace the teacher’s action head. 

DiffMPC constraints. We now turn to our main comparison between the teacher and the con-strained student, which adds explicit constraints without losing performance. For the small policy, the constraints clearly help on CARLA by reducing failure rates from the teacher’s 0.245 to the student’s 0.180 ± 0.003 . On nuPlan the constrained DiffMPC stays on par with the teacher’s failure rates, while the other students fail more often. For the large policy, the constrained DiffMPC student has the lowest failure rates on nuPlan and matches the teacher on CARLA within one standard er-ror. Generally, the constrained DiffMPC student successfully keeps up with the teacher. Across both sizes and all three test sets, it is never significantly worse than the MLP or unconstrained students. We believe that whether the constraints help depends on the quality of the constraints compared to the teacher. The small teacher makes more mistakes than the constraints allow, so the constraints correct it. The large teacher already drives well, so the constraints rarely add information. 

Generalization. We next use the WOD data to measure the policies’ OOD performance, and therefore, their ability to generalize. For both policy sizes, the constrained students fail less often than their teachers and MLP students, mostly through less off-road driving. For the large policies, the off-road rate drops from 0.035 to 0.025 , which shows the effectiveness of the road boundary con-straints. Their effectiveness suggests that the constraints generalize better than the learned policy. While our constraints use ground-truth map data, they can be seen as an independent measurement, that increases system robustness in scenarios that are new to the end-to-end policy. 

Training cost. All students reach their best validation checkpoint within 10M to 60M labels, compared to the 1.1B and 7.9B PPO steps of the small and large teacher, respectively. Distilling the constrained student takes 2.8–4.7 h and 2.0–3.3 h on one GPU for the small and large policy, on top of the teachers’ 1 h and 11 .4 h of training. For the large policy, this adds at most 30% to the teacher’s training time. For the small policy, distillation takes longer than training the teacher, because each MPC label costs about eight to ten times an MLP label, while the small teacher con-verged quickly. Appendix C details the training times and validation curves, and Appendix D shows why the constrained DiffMPC needs the fixed-sum weights and the node weights κk.

6. Conclusion and future work 

This work presented a new pipeline that helps adding redundancy to safety critical systems with the help of DiffMPC. Besides providing a novel method for training a DiffMPC policy, we change the perspective on it from a competing architecture to a new post-training step for end-to-end MLP policies. Results clearly show that (1) unconstrained DiffMPC policies are as expressive as MLP policies (2) constrained DiffMPC adds redundancy with constraints, which leads to reduced failure rates for smaller, more failure-prone policies, and on the out-of-distribution WOD scenes. Future work should look into designing better constraints and verifying them on larger end-to-end models. Another intuitive extension is to use the new pipeline outside of vectorized simulation and showing its potential in applications beyond autonomous driving.

Appendix D. Constraint formulation 

Table 7 shows how the constrained DiffMPC developed, measured as the sum of collision and off-road rates on the 250 CARLA validation scenes. All rows are single 4x1024 runs, distilled with DAgger on CARLA maps, at their selected checkpoint. Adding the road boundary constraints and agent ellipses to the unconstrained student only at evaluation strongly degrades its performance, so the constraints have to be present during training. Training with the constraints improves the student, but it remains well below the unconstrained student. In these first constrained runs, each slack variable has a fixed linear price of 100. With the sigmoid weights in (5), the student learns weights that sum up to about 825 per node, which outweighs the slack price and effectively softens the constraints. The large weights also slow down the ADMM. Capping each weight does not help either, as the predictor can still shrink all weights. Fixing the sum of the weights and pricing the slacks at the same sum, as in Section 4.2, keeps the constraints close to hard. Without the node weights, however, the student cannot follow the teacher where its line crosses a plane, and off-road driving grows with training. Only the fixed-sum weights with the node weights κk bring the constrained student close to the unconstrained one.
