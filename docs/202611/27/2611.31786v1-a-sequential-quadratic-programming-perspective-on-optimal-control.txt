ABSTRACT 

This paper investigates the relationship between the iterative Linear Quadratic Reg-ulator (iLQR), Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), and Newton’s method for optimal control problems. Using a unified Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) framework, we introduce Newton-LQR, a Riccati-based algorithm that solves for the exact Newton step the optimal control problem. Within this framework, we establish that iLQR is a principled modified SQP method rather than merely an approximation of DDP. We show that iLQR generates a descent direction, whereas the inclusion of dynamics Hessians in DDP and Newton-LQR may lead to indef-inite quadratic subproblems and necessitate regularization. We further distinguish DDP and the proposed Newton-LQR algorithm through their treatment of costate information and second-order dynamics terms. Numerical experiments across six benchmark problems demonstrate that iLQR often exhibits superior convergence during the initial optimization phase, while Newton-LQR and DDP can exploit second-order information to accelerate convergence near a solution.

1. Introduction 

The study of iterative trajectory optimization has a long history in nonlinear control. Among the most influential methods are Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), introduced in the 1960s [16, 9], and the iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR), which later emerged as a simplified yet powerful alternative [30, 28]. DDP is acclaimed as Newton-type algorithm for trajectory optimization that exploits second-order ex-pansions of both the cost and the dynamics, achieving locally quadratic convergence near an optimum [16, 9, 10]. iLQR, by contrast, employs consistent first-order approx-imations of the dynamics, making it more numerically reliable and computationally efficient in practice [30, 28, 31]. Over the decades, these two approaches have become foundational tools for nonlinear control, and trajectory optimization [3, 32, 7, 6]. A large body of literature has analyzed the theoretical properties and limitations of both methods. Several works have emphasized the quadratic convergence of DDP under ideal assumptions [16, 9, 24, 27], but have also noted the difficulties that arise in 

> Email: abhinir@tamu.edu Email: schakrav@tamu.edu arXiv:2611.31786v1 [math.OC] 24 Nov 2026

practice when the algorithm’s backward pass combines second-order expansions with first-order terms, which can compromise stability and necessitate heavy regulariza-tion [24, 14, 4, 19, 25]. An explicit connection between DDP and Newton’s method was established in [24, 17], and it is argued in [14] that, for discrete-time optimal control, DDP can outperform Newton’s method—retaining strong local convergence while offering greater robustness and efficiency under practical problem structures. For rigid-body systems, accelerated second-order DDP has been explored [19]. Extensions such as the iterative Linear Quadratic Gaussian (iLQG) [30] and control-limited iLQR [29] have reinforced the practical importance of iLQR in stochastic and constrained settings. Recent developments in iLQR and DDP have significantly expanded their appli-cations in nonlinear trajectory optimization, particularly in robotics and aerospace engineering. In robotics, these methods have enabled efficient motion planning and control for high-dimensional dynamical systems. Notable algorithmic developments include ALTRO, which combines augmented Lagrangian methods with iLQR-based trajectory optimization [8], and Crocoddyl, which introduces a feasibility-driven DDP framework for multi-contact optimal control [15]. Further advances by Wensing and collaborators have extended DDP to hybrid dynamical systems for whole-body mo-tion planning of legged robots [13], developed computationally efficient approaches for incorporating second-order dynamics information into DDP [20], and demonstrated full second-order DDP for whole-body model predictive control on legged robot hard-ware [18]. In aerospace engineering, DDP has been applied to trajectory optimization in astrodynamics and spacecraft guidance [11, 12, 1], as well as planetary landing problems [22]. Other extensions, such as parameterized DDP, have demonstrated its applicability to complex robotic systems [23]. While effective in these contexts, these studies also highlight the algorithm’s sensitivity to problem structure and its depen-dence on strong regularization, particularly when the local quadratic models become inconsistent [24, 14]. Although iLQR is often described as an approximation of DDP obtained by ne-glecting second-order dynamic terms [30], this characterization obscures its principled nature. In this paper, we revisit the solution of optimal control problems through the lens of Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) [2, 21].The critical insight is that the Newton step in a constrained optimization problem is equivalent to solving a Quadratic Program (QP), which reduces to an LQR problem in the context of optimal control. Using this framework, we show that iLQR is not merely an approximation of DDP, but rather a principled method that guarantees cost-descent directions under mild assumptions—a property that neither DDP nor Newton’s method can ensure in general, particularly when far from an optimum [24, 14]. We also derive the exact Newton LQR formulation, and show that DDP can be interpreted as an approximation of this exact Newton step only near an optimum.To evaluate these theoretical find-ings, we conduct numerical comparisons on pendulum and cart-pole swing-up tasks, along with four additional nonlinear benchmark problems. Our analysis examines the positive definiteness of the control Hessian, the frequency and magnitude of regular-ization required to obtain descent directions, and the resulting convergence behavior. The numerical results corroborate the SQP-based analysis, demonstrating that iLQR generally maintains positive-definite quadratic subproblems, requires substantially less regularization, and achieves faster convergence than DDP across the problems consid-ered. Furthermore, by avoiding the explicit computation of dynamics Hessians, iLQR reduces the computational overhead associated with constructing the local quadratic models [32, 31]. We also see that close to an optimum, Newton LQR and DDP exhibit 2faster convergence than iLQR. These observations highlight the practical advantages of iLQR, particularly during the initial stages of optimization when the iterates are far from a stationary solution, while also motivating the switch to second-order dynamics information in Newton-LQR and DDP to accelerate convergence near optimality. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the optimal control problem and establishes the necessary notation. Section 3 presents the Sequen-tial Quadratic Programming (SQP) framework. Section 4 develops the Newton-LQR formulation and examines its relationship with iLQR, DDP, and Stagewise-Newton. Section 5 presents numerical comparisons across several benchmark problems and in-vestigates the proposed hybrid approach. Finally, Section 6 summarizes the main find-ings and conclusions. Section 6 concludes with insights drawn from these comparisons.

4. The SQP perspective on iterative perturbation feedback methods 

In Section 3, we introduced the exact Newton QP (4) and its modified counterpart (5). We now specialize these formulations to the optimal control problem (2) and demon-strate how they give rise to the Newton-LQR and iLQR algorithms, respectively. We 6subsequently examine DDP and Stagewise-Newton as related approximations to the Newton-LQR formulation. This unified SQP perspective allows us to identify the differ-ences in the quadratic subproblems solved by these algorithms and their implications for convergence. These methods, which we collectively refer to as “iterative perturba-tion feedback methods,” exploit the sparsity structure of the optimal control problem and are intended to solve the problem defined by eq. (2) and eq. (1). All of these algorithms are initialized from a nominal trajectory, (¯ xt, ¯ut), which is iteratively refined until convergence. To clarify the relationship among them, we first revisit the QP subproblem obtained from the exact Newton linear-quadratic approx-imation. This serves as the natural reference point for the discussion that follows. We then consider the modified approximation underlying iLQR, and finally examine the formulations used by DDP and Stagewise-Newton. As will become clear, although DDP and Stagewise-Newton are often interpreted as Newton-type methods, the QPs they solve differ from the exact Newton subproblem described in the next subsection and have to be understood as approximations.

As established in the preceding sections, the dynamics Hessian terms can render the local quadratic subproblem indefinite, necessitating regularization to obtain a descent direction. In contrast, iLQR omits these terms and, under suitable convexity assumptions on the cost functions, guarantees a descent direction without requiring regularization. These observations motivate a hybrid optimization strategy that exploits the com-plementary advantages of iLQR and second-order methods. The proposed approach consists of two phases. During the initial phase, iLQR is employed to exploit its favor-able descent properties and obtain a trajectory in the vicinity of a stationary solution. Once sufficient progress has been achieved, the algorithm transitions to either DDP or the proposed Newton-LQR method to exploit the additional second-order dynamics information and potentially accelerate convergence. To determine when the transition should occur, we consider the relative change in the objective function between successive iterations. Specifically, the switching crite-rion is defined as 

|J k+1 − J k|

max {|J k| , ε } < ψ, (26) where Jk denotes the objective function value at iteration k, ψ > 0 is a user-defined threshold, and ε > 0 is a small constant introduced to prevent division by zero. Once the criterion in (26) is satisfied, the iLQR backward pass is replaced by the cor-responding DDP or Newton-LQR backward pass, thereby incorporating the dynamics Hessian terms into the local quadratic model. The second-order method is retained for the remainder of the optimization. It is important to note that a small relative change in the objective function does not necessarily imply proximity to a stationary solution, as it may also indicate stagnation. Therefore, (26) is employed as a practical switching heuristic rather than a theoretical certificate of stationarity. The objective of this hybrid strategy is to combine the reliable early-stage conver-gence behavior of iLQR with the potentially faster local convergence of second-order 17 methods. In the numerical section, we investigate the stage of optimization at which activating the dynamics Hessian information becomes advantageous.

5.2. The impact of Forward-Pass 

A critical distinction between the Stagewise-Newton method and the DDP/iLQR fam-ily of algorithms lies in the forward pass, specifically in how state perturbations are propagated to compute the control update. In DDP and iLQR, the feedback term depends on the full nonlinear state perturbation, δxt. The control update is 

δut = αkt + Ktδxt, where δxt is the difference between the new and previous (nomi-20 nal) trajectories: 

δxt = f (xt, ut) − f (¯ xt, ¯ut).

Ktδˆxt, that relies on a linearized propagation of the state perturbation, δˆxt:

6. Conclusion 

In this paper, we have investigated discrete-time nonlinear optimal control problems by analyzing Newton’s method, Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), and the iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) through a unified Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) framework. The central insight is that these algorithms solve a sequence of Quadratic Programs (QPs), which reduce to Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problems by exploiting the structure of optimal control. Within this frame-work, we introduce “Newton-LQR”, a novel Riccati-based formulation for solving the exact Newton-SQP quadratic subproblem, and establish its relationship with iLQR and DDP. Our analysis reveals that DDP can be interpreted as an approximation of Newton-LQR, with their formulations agreeing near a stationary solution under appropriate conditions. However, the inclusion of dynamics Hessians can render the local quadratic subproblems indefinite, necessitating regularization and potentially producing unreli-able descent directions. In contrast, iLQR provides consistent descent directions under suitable convexity assumptions and admits theoretical convergence guarantees. Numerical experiments across six benchmark problems corroborate the theoretical findings, demonstrating that iLQR generally requires less regularization and converges faster than DDP and Newton-LQR. We further establish that heavy regularization causes the control update to asymptotically approach a scaled gradient-descent step, potentially slowing convergence. Nevertheless, our numerical investigations demon-strate that incorporating dynamics Hessians can accelerate convergence near a station-ary solution, where Newton-LQR exhibits more consistent improvements than DDP in the swing-up examples considered. These findings motivate a hybrid strategy that employs iLQR during the initial op-timization phase and transitions to Newton-LQR near optimality. Overall, our results establish iLQR as a principled SQP approach rather than merely an approximation of DDP, while highlighting the importance of balancing reliable descent directions with the local convergence advantages of second-order information. 28 References
