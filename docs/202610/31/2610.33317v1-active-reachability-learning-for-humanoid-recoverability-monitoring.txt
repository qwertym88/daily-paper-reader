Abstract— Deploying humanoid task policies requires reli-
able runtime monitors that anticipate unrecoverable falls in
time to trigger a fallback. We formulate this task as learn-
ing the stochastic, finite-horizon recoverability envelope of a
given fallback policy. Synthesizing this monitor is challenging:
existing methods assume training data naturally covers the
recoverability envelope, yet acquiring such data remains a
major bottleneck for contact-rich systems. Consequently, they
recover only a fraction of the true envelope, leaving them
prone to optimistic hallucinations for out-of-distribution (OOD)
inputs. We resolve this via a unified framework that iterates
between value learning and active exploration. Data exploration
is reframed as a reach-avoid problem, where an uncertainty-
guided explorer systematically uncovers unseen states while
avoiding confidently unrecoverable regions. This loop is further
supported by a combined Temporal Difference and Monte
Carlo objective for accurate in-distribution value prediction
and reliable uncertainty estimation. By explicitly treating the
remaining high-uncertainty regions as unrecoverable, the mon-
itor mitigates optimistic OOD hallucinations. Evaluated on
stochastic benchmarks and a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid, our
approach drastically improves monitor coverage and accuracy
and successfully transfers zero-shot to real-world hardware.
I. INTRODUCTION
The deployment of contact-rich robotic systems, such as
humanoids, holds immense potential for automating tasks
in unstructured environments. However, inherent instability
and underactuation make humanoids prone to catastrophic
failures, such as unrecoverable falls. Hence, safely deploying
versatile task policies necessitates reliable runtime monitors
that detect impending failures in advance, allowing the
system to trigger a robust fallback policy before a fall be-
comes unavoidable. In this paper, we formulate the monitor
synthesis as learning an empirical, finite-horizon recover-
ability (or liveness) envelope for a fixed fallback balancing

policy. Such reliable monitors enable rapid development and
safe deployment of arbitrary humanoid task policies while
drastically reducing the risk of hardware damage.
The synthesis of this recoverability monitor exhibits ex-
treme numerical stiffness, as the underlying liveness value
may change violently during exact rigid-body interactions.
For example, a slipping foot and a firm step may share
nearly identical states at the moment of contact, yet lead to
completely different outcomes. More fundamentally, training
requires a comprehensive mapping of the underlying liveness
set, encompassing states that are recoverable and reachable
under realistic physical disturbances. To cover these states,
we cannot uniformly sample the state space or artificially
“teleport” the robot to expand coverage, as minor kinematic
offsets often cause rigid-body penetration, triggering severe
numerical artifacts in the simulator that corrupt the training
data. Therefore, we are restricted to acquiring data via
episodic rollouts driven by dynamic controls or physical
perturbations. However, rolling out from a manually designed
initial-state distribution yields insufficient liveness set cover-
age, leaving the monitor prone to making overly optimistic
predictions for out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs.
Existing safety frameworks struggle with the compounded
dimensionality and data coverage challenges. Analytical
Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) [1]–[4] are effective for
task-space constraints but scale poorly to whole-body re-
coverability monitoring. Similarly, existing stochastic reach-
ability solvers [5], [6] are computationally restricted to
low-dimensional systems. Physics-Informed Neural Network
(PINN) reachability methods [7]–[9] handle high dimension-
ality with deep learning, but rely on uniform state-space
sampling. This renders them incompatible with contact-rich
domains where uniform sampling inevitably yields mostly
irrelevant or non-physical, corrupted samples.
On the other hand, Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based

methods learn from simulator rollouts, which respect phys-
ical constraints. RL-based CBFs [10]–[12] and RL-based
reachability approaches [13]–[17] adopt this strategy. How-
ever, because these rollouts originate from nominal initial-
state distributions, they only recover a narrow fraction of the
true recoverability envelope. This limited coverage not only
reduces the allowable operation space but also causes overly
optimistic predictions for OOD states. While recent work
attempts to mitigate this by treating high-uncertainty OOD
states as unrecoverable [18], this assumes the training dataset
naturally captured enough “OOD” states (determined by a
frozen world model) prior to learning. Standard RL explo-
ration techniques [19]–[21] also cannot fill this gap, as they
are designed to maximize task reward rather than systemati-
cally chart the boundaries of recovery failure. Consequently,
despite its critical importance for real-world deployment, the
problem of actively exploring the recoverability envelope
under non-teleporting physical constraints remains under-
explored by the community.
We tackle this challenge through a unified bi-level syn-
thesis framework. In the Inner Level (Value Learning), we
estimate the finite-horizon liveness value of the fallback pol-
icy using a combined Temporal Difference (TD) and Monte
Carlo (MC) objective, providing stable gradients for accurate
in-distribution (in-D) value learning and reliable epistemic
uncertainty estimation. In the Outer Level (Exploration), we
reframe data augmentation as a reach-avoid problem, where
an active explorer targets OOD states to expand coverage
without appending confidently unrecoverable samples. By
systematically uncovering the recoverability envelope and
treating remaining high-uncertainty regions as failures, the
monitor effectively mitigates optimistic hallucinations with-
out sacrificing allowable operation space. Finally, we validate
this framework on stochastic benchmarks and demonstrate
zero-shot transfer on a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid.
II. PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. System Dynamics
We consider a system operating within a discrete-time
Markov Decision Process under a given fallback controller
π. The dynamics form a stochastic system xk+1 ∼P(· |
xk, ak, dk), where xk ∈X is the continuous state at step
k, ak ∈A is the control action, and dk ∈D is an op-
tional artificial disturbance. Crucially, we model the system
as inherently stochastic rather than relying on traditional
worst-case bounded disturbances for liveness synthesis, be-
cause compounding worst-case disturbances across multiple
sources can easily overpower a humanoid’s control authority,
yielding impractically small recoverability envelopes.
During data collection (episodic rollouts), the system is
closed-loop under π, with ak
= π(xk) and dk
≡0.
During data exploration (novel initial state discovery), an
active explorer instead freely selects the control ak and
may additionally apply dk̸ = 0 to steer the system toward
more informative states. We assume a narrow, manually
defined distribution of initial states ρ0(x) to seed the first
episodic rollouts. As training progresses, subsequent rollouts
can optionally be initialized from states accumulated in the
replay buffer to continuously expand data coverage.

We instantiate the framework on humanoid applications,

Remark (Practical Consideration: Reach-Avoid Extension).
The liveness formulation assumes that reaching G means π
has recovered without collapsing to the ground and allows
the system to stay stationary forever. However, highly agile
recovery controllers may allow the system to temporarily
collapse then recover, and deployment may impose strict
collision-avoidance constraints to prevent damage to the sur-
rounding environment and humans. For these scenarios, our
framework naturally extends to reach-avoid formulations.
III. METHOD
A. Overview and Active Learning Loop
Because manually defining the edge of the recoverability
envelope for a high-dimensional, contact-rich humanoid is
intractable, we cast the monitor synthesis as an iterative
active-learning loop. The framework alternates between ac-
curately learning the liveness value of known states and
actively discovering novel states at the fringe of the pol-
icy’s capabilities. We begin by rolling out the given policy
π from ρ0(x) to collect an initial dataset D. Using this
dataset, we learn an accurate in-D value function alongside
an uncertainty estimator. Leveraging this uncertainty as a
reward signal, and initializing from states already in D,
we then train a disturbance explorer to actively push the
system toward novel OOD states that are at most marginally
unrecoverable. If the explorer discovers a sufficient number
of high-uncertainty states, we roll out the policy π from
these newly discovered states to augment D and return to
the value-learning phase. Once the explorer fails to find
sufficient meaningful new states, the loop terminates, and the
framework outputs the learned value function and uncertainty
estimator as the recoverability monitor. For our primary
humanoid application, during the deployment of an arbitrary
task policy, the monitor triggers the balancing policy π until a
stationary state is reached whenever either the liveness value
or uncertainty exceeds a predefined threshold (ϵV ≪ϵ). The
full active-learning loop is shown in Alg. 1.

Initial dataset and time curriculum. We construct the
initial dataset in two stages. We first roll out π from samples
of the initial-state distribution ρ0 to form the TD dataset.
We then draw a second set of initial states—from both ρ0
and the states visited in the TD dataset—and generate long-
horizon rollouts from them to form the MC dataset; the initial
dataset D is the union of the two. Importantly, accurately
learning the in-D value function is crucial for meaningful
epistemic uncertainty estimation. To stabilize temporal value
learning, we adopt a standard time-curriculum strategy [7],
in which the sampled time step k for the TD and MC targets
is expanded gradually from {K −1} to the full horizon
{0, 1, . . . , K −1}, with denser sampling near the boundary
k = K −1. Once the exploration loop begins and D grows
round by round, we remove the curriculum and fine-tune Vθ
directly over the full horizon on the expanded dataset.
C. Uncertainty- and Reachability-Based Explorer
To efficiently augment the existing dataset with novel
but potentially recoverable states, we cast exploration as
a Backward Reach-Avoid Tube (BRAT) problem. Starting
from states in the replay buffer D, we train an exploration
policy πexplore that drives the system into a target set of high
epistemic uncertainty while staying out of an avoid set of
confidently unrecoverable states.
Specifically, we first train an off-the-shelf epistemic un-
certainty estimator σ(x) (e.g., a value-function ensemble or
Random Network Distillation (RND)) on the current dataset
D to determine the high-uncertainty target set:
Gexplore = {x : gexplore(x) = ϵσ −σ(x) < 0}.
The avoid set consists of states with high predicted failure
and low uncertainty:

Both gexplore and lexplore are continuous, providing a dense
gradient signal even far from their boundaries. Consequently,
even when Gexplore is effectively empty, the learned πexplore
still moves toward relatively more novel states, and the
system’s momentum helps uncover genuinely new data.
We solve for the exploration value function Vexplore(x, t)
and its induced policy πexplore using an existing learning-
based reachability toolbox [22]. While the toolbox tradi-
tionally approximates an optimal time-conditioned policy
πexplore(x, t), we instead learn a time-independent policy
πexplore(x) to simplify the data exploration loop at a slight
cost to optimality. The training horizon for the explorer is set
as a hyperparameter Texplore. A moderate Texplore is crucial: it
prevents the ineffective exploration from a myopic horizon,
while ensuring seeds do not collapse onto a few distant high-
uncertainty peaks, as happens with overly long horizons.
Data augmentation. Each round, we roll out πexplore from
states sampled from D. Notably, the rollout horizon for these
exploration trajectories can extend beyond the explorer’s
training horizon Texplore to encourage exploration. Along
each trajectory, we extract states that successfully enter the
high-uncertainty target set to serve as novel seeds. Because
multiple valid seeds may be encountered within a single
continuous rollout, we cap the extraction to at most 5 seeds
per trajectory to prevent deep penetration into unrecoverable
OOD regions. From each extracted seed, we then execute
the fallback policy π to generate fresh TD and MC data,
which are subsequently appended to D. Value learning and
exploration alternate until a round yields fewer than Nmin
novel seeds or a predefined round budget is reached.
IV. RESULTS
We evaluate the framework across three settings. First, we
conduct case studies on two underactuated benchmarks—
a stochastic 4D planar double integrator (DI) and a 3D Du-
bins car. Both systems admit uniform grid sampling strictly
for offline evaluation purposes, allowing us to benchmark
our pipeline against near-exact ground truths and isolate
individual design choices. During monitor synthesis, only
an extremely narrow initial distribution ρ0 is provided for
initial data collection, as illustrated in Fig. 2, and new
states can only be explored by rolling out from buffered
states. Hence, the Dubins car presents severe underactuation
for exploration because rollouts are constrained by non-
holonomic dynamics with a fixed velocity and bounded turn
rate. The DI, conversely, serves as a benchmark with higher
stochasticity but lower underactuation. These case studies
are structured around three questions: (Q1) Coverage—can
our framework recover most of the recoverable states, and
which design choice performs best? (Q2) Accuracy—does the
TD+MC loss outperform TD(λ) alone or a time-agnostic TD
loss [23]? (Q3) Sensitivity—how do the MC sample budget
NMC and the explorer’s training horizon affect performance?
Second, we scale to a Unitree G1 humanoid, synthesizing a
liveness value Vθ and failure probability Pfail for a balancing
policy on complex high-dimensional dynamics. Third, we
close the loop by deploying the learned monitor on a walking
task policy in simulation and on hardware.
a) Metrics: We evaluate against “ground-truth” empir-
ical liveness values, where each value is labeled via 1000
MC rollouts. We report six metrics: recall—the fraction of
truly recoverable states within the ϵ-liveness set correctly
identified (ϵ = 0.5); precision—the fraction of predicted-
recoverable states that are genuinely recoverable, penalizing
overoptimism; F1—their harmonic mean; MSE—the mean
squared error; cumulative seeds—the total exploration seeds
collected across all rounds, indicating data efficiency; and
confident coverage—the fraction of the true recoverable
set covered with low epistemic uncertainty σ ≤ϵσ (recall
restricted to in-D predictions). We also report wall time on
an NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPU.

plorer against unbiased random-action exploration and bi-
ased random exploration that samples a constant per-episode

Evidently, the RND estimator provides a highly efficient
alternative to ensemble methods. When paired with the
reach-avoid explorer, RND matches or surpasses ensemble
performance while requiring a lower computational cost as it
eliminates the need to train multiple member networks. It ad-
ditionally provides sharper OOD detection, which effectively
bounds the augmented dataset size (cumulative seeds).
Furthermore, reach-avoid exploration demonstrates a sig-
nificant advantage over unbiased and biased random explo-
ration, particularly in highly underactuated environments. On
the Dubins car, unbiased exploration completely collapses
(0.267 coverage) and biased exploration manages only a par-
tial recovery (0.408). By contrast, the reach-avoid explorer
achieves a coverage of 0.656. On the DI system, it similarly
elevates coverage to 0.941, compared to ∼0.86 for both
random baselines. More importantly, these superior results
are obtained in fewer active-learning rounds, demonstrating
the efficiency of the reach-avoid explorer. Driven by these
margins, we adopt RND + reach-avoid as our default
configuration for the remaining experiments.

these states via 100 MC rollouts of π per state.

∼6h wall time), Vθ attains an excellent F1 score of 0.957 ±
0.001 on the validation set and maintains a highly robust F1
of 0.918±0.009 on the aggressive OOD push dataset, yield-

accuracy (F1 = 0.920 Val / 0.799 OOD) for better inter-
pretability. Notably, both F1 and coverage improve almost
monotonically across rounds. The most substantial gains
occur in OOD recall (Vθ, Fig. 6, left), surging from 0.672 to

states that are unreachable by simply rolling out π from ρ0. A
similar trend appears in the Pfail estimator, though coverage
expansion is less pronounced due to the inherently sparser
gradient signal of probability learning. While exhibiting
lower overall recall, Pfail maintains high precision—meaning
it rarely hallucinates false recoverability—which is critical
for deploying a real-world monitor.

Learning Objective Comparison. We first evaluate the
monitors trained under different learning objectives on the
healthy walking policy across varying push durations (Fig. 6,
right). Our TD+MC monitor avoids excessive conservatism,
raising zero false alarms during undisturbed walking or
gentle pushes (Tmax ≤0.05 s). In contrast, the time-agnostic
TD baseline [23] degenerates completely (triggering on >
86% of benign states), and the TD-only variant also over-
triggers on normal walking, both of which would constantly
interrupt task execution in practice. Despite this improved
precision, our TD+MC monitor achieves the lowest failure
rates across all disturbance bands, and its trigger rate strictly
bounds the actual failure rate while predicting 100% of
eventual falls in advance. Finally, even under the most severe
disturbance band, our monitor provides an average lead time
of 0.58 s between a high-confidence failure prediction (Vθ ≥
0.9) and torso-ground contact, securing sufficient margin to
potentially activate a harm-minimizing fallback.

especially on the degraded walker. This confirms both the
accuracy of the liveness value and the improved robustness
of the fallback balancing policy. Aside from minor learning
errors, the non-zero residual monitored failure rates stem
from the highly stochastic nature of our simulation envi-


ronment (aggressive domain randomization and observation
noise). However, a physical robot represents only a sin-

the actual hardware dynamics fall well within the domain
randomization bounds and physical sensor noise is more
lenient, the empirical failure probability during real-world
deployment is expected to be substantially lower. Finally,
we qualitatively demonstrate the zero-shot performance on
the G1 hardware in Fig. 1 and the supplementary video.
V. CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
In this work, we presented a framework combining a
TD+MC learning objective with uncertainty-guided reach-
avoid exploration to learn policy recoverability under
stochastic dynamics. Our approach successfully scales to
a 29-DoF humanoid, yielding an accurate and efficient
runtime monitor. To our knowledge, this is the first effort
to comprehensively map the liveness-set envelope for high-
dimensional, contact-rich systems. Despite achieving promis-
ing outcomes, the framework currently acts reactively, rather
than proactively, to OOD inputs. Furthermore, while empir-
ically accurate, the learned solutions remain uncalibrated.
Lastly, the maximum achievable coverage is fundamentally
bounded by the choice of the explorer’s action space.
Looking forward, although our framework is theoretically
amenable to any contact-rich system, its efficacy on complex
dexterous manipulation, long-horizon or extremely dynamic
tasks requires further investigation. In addition, extending it
to general reach-avoid settings, where the robot must achieve
goals while obeying safety specifications, will significantly
broaden its practical impact. Ultimately, leveraging these
learned reachability values to improve the quality and ef-
ficiency of policy synthesis stands as a natural next step.
