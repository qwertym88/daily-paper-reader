From Slices to Volumes: Comparing 2D, 2.5D, and 3D Residual
Networks for Pulmonary Nodule Malignancy-Risk Classification
Bahodir Nazarov1∗, Zineb Gharib1,
and Fatima Zazoul1
1Sichuan University, Chengdu, Sichuan, China
Abstract
Purpose: To compare 2D, 2.5D, and 3D residual networks for pulmonary nodule
malignancy-risk classification and evaluate momentum-contrast pretraining, mild augmenta-
tion, and exclusion of indeterminate ratings. Methods: A retrospectively prepared LIDC-
IDRI cohort contained 2,654 nodules labeled benign, indeterminate, or malignant using median
radiologist ratings. Patient-disjoint splits comprised 1,876 training, 382 validation, and 396
test nodules. Representations included one axial slice, five neighboring slices, and a local CT
volume. Each track evaluated a randomly initialized baseline, momentum-contrast pretraining
followed by fine-tuning, and supervised training with mild augmentation. Checkpoints were se-
lected by validation Macro-F1. The 2D experiments used three seeds; 2.5D and 3D results were
single-run estimates. A separate binary experiment excluded indeterminate nodules. Results:
Augmented models achieved test Macro-F1 values of 0.6134 ± 0.0075 for 2D, 0.6537 for 2.5D,
and 0.5403 for 3D; the 2D uncertainty denotes population standard deviation across seeds.
Corresponding accuracies were 0.6128, 0.6869, and 0.5455. Augmentation improved Macro-F1
within every track, whereas the tested pretraining recipes reduced it. Binary Macro-F1 values
were 0.8227, 0.8664, and 0.7554, respectively. Conclusion: Augmented 2.5D achieved the
highest observed performance. Differences in preprocessing, optimization, capacity, and
replication limit attribution to dimensionality. Labels reflect radiologist-assessed malignancy
risk rather than pathology-confirmed cancer. Code and experimental results are available at
https://github.com/CT-DeepLearning-Team/CT-2D-2.5D-3D-DeepLearning.
Keywords: pulmonary nodules; computed tomography; deep learning; self-supervised learning.

Introduction
CT represents anatomy as an ordered stack of slices. A pulmonary nodule can therefore be
represented by a single axial image, a short stack of neighboring images, or a local three-
dimensional volume. These alternatives provide different spatial information and impose different
learning requirements. A single image may omit through-plane variation; a neighboring-slice
representation supplies limited depth context through input channels; volumetric convolutions
can learn features across all three spatial axes. Whether the richer representation yields better
held-out performance remains an empirical question.
LIDC-IDRI provides thoracic CT images and multiple-reader nodule annotations, including
subjective malignancy ratings [1]. These ratings support research into image-based risk characteri-
zation, but their interpretation differs from a diagnosis established by histopathology. Accordingly,
this study uses the term malignancy-risk classification throughout. Its three operational classes
summarize reader assessments rather than confirmed disease status.

Residual networks provide a practical common architectural family for the comparison [2].
Self-supervised momentum contrast offers a way to initialize encoders using a larger pool
of training-patient nodules without consuming malignancy labels [3, 4].
We also examine
whether mild transformations during supervised training reduce the mismatch between fitting
the available training samples and generalizing to unseen patients. Our contributions are: (i) a
documented comparison of three spatial representations on one prepared cohort and fixed patient
assignments; (ii) within-track comparisons of direct supervision, contrastive initialization, and
mild augmentation; and (iii) an error analysis and supplementary binary experiment that retain
the distinction between uncertain ratings and the easier two-class endpoint.
The study is a comparison of implemented model tracks, rather than a strict single-variable
ablation. We report their differences explicitly. This qualification matters because training
settings, additional intensity standardization, slice centering, parameter counts, and replication
are not identical across representations.

Related Work
Prior pulmonary nodule studies have investigated different ways of incorporating local morphology
and context. Shen et al. proposed a multi-crop CNN for malignancy suspiciousness classification
using multi-scale nodule features [5]. Their use of multiple crops addresses scale and spatial
coverage; our neighboring-slice representation instead supplies a fixed short axial stack as
channels. These are related approaches to contextual information but are not interchangeable
representations.
Setio et al. used multi-view convolutional networks for false-positive reduction in a pulmonary
nodule detection system [6]. That work illustrates how complementary views can support nodule
analysis, but its detection endpoint differs from assigning risk categories to an already identified
nodule. It therefore cannot provide a directly comparable baseline for the present Macro-F1 values.
Published scores obtained under different labels, cohort selection, and evaluation partitions do
not provide directly comparable baselines for this study.
Recent work further combines spatial context with attention and auxiliary supervision. Zhu
and Fei’s Cross-ViT combines convolutional and transformer features using cross-attention, with
three neighboring slices as input [10]. Their binary experiment uses a screened LUNA16 cohort
and removes indeterminate nodules; its reader-based labeling policy differs from our median-
rating classes. Thus, a short slice stack is also compatible with more complex architectures, but
published binary performance does not establish performance on our three-class endpoint.
Jin et al.’s multitask Swin Transformer jointly predicts malignancy categories and nodule
characteristics, and uses U-Net GAN augmentation [11]. Its binary and multilevel experiments
exclude rating 3, whereas indeterminate risk remains the largest class in our primary cohort. Liu
et al.’s LNMSNet uses a 3D ResNet-18 backbone with multiscale attention and joint segmentation–
classification training [12]. Its LIDC-IDRI experiment also excludes rating 3; independent hospital
cohorts provide an additional evaluation setting. These studies motivate attention, auxiliary tasks,
and external evaluation as relevant follow-up directions, without resolving which representation
performs best under our current label and split definitions.
This study contributes a comparison of three residual-network representations with explicit
attention to uncertain ratings, pretraining transfer, and supervised regularization on a common
cohort. The central question is whether additional spatial information is beneficial under the
evaluated data and training constraints. Its contribution is comparative evidence with documented
implementation differences rather than a new network architecture.

Materials and Methods
3.1
Study design and source provenance
This retrospective computational study compares three implemented model tracks on a fixed LIDC-
IDRI-derived cohort. Each track evaluates direct supervised learning, contrastive initialization,
mild supervised augmentation, and a separate binary sensitivity analysis. Methods and results
are versioned in the accompanying repository at commit d47e716 [9]. Evaluation is internal and
patient-disjoint; no external validation cohort is included. The design emphasizes comparisons
within each representation while documenting the differences that limit cross-representation
causal inference. Reporting distinguishes reference-standard definition, checkpoint selection,
class-specific errors, and replication, consistent with the transparency principles emphasized by
CLAIM [8].
3.2
Dataset preparation and cohort
The source-only preparation pipeline inventories DICOM records, selects CT series, parses XML
annotations, matches image identifiers, reconstructs canonical volumes, creates reader masks
and consensus nodules, resamples to isotropic spacing, extracts crops, applies technical quality
control, aggregates risk ratings, and assigns patients to fixed splits. The preparation pipeline
processed 1,010 patients, inventorying 244,527 DICOM records, parsing 1,318 XML files, and
identifying 1,018 valid CT series. There were 7,385 consensus nodules, of which 2,672 were rated;
the final supervised cohort contained 2,654 eligible nodules. These counts describe different
preparation stages and should not be interpreted as independent scans or supervised examples.
The supervised cohort comprises 873 benign, 1,226 indeterminate, and 555 malignant nodules.
Table 2 gives the fixed split composition. Split separation is at the patient level: all eligible
nodules from one patient receive the same assignment. The fixed split manifests record zero
patient overlap among supervised splits. The SSL pool contains 5,133 nodules from 692 training-
assigned patients. The SSL pool includes eligible nodules without a supervised rating and is
therefore larger than the supervised training set. Validation and test patients are excluded from
SSL pretraining.

3.3
Reference labels
The median available reader malignancy rating defines the operational class: scores 1.0–2.0
are benign, 2.5–3.0 are indeterminate, and 3.5–5.0 are malignant. Half-integer medians arise
when multiple integer reader scores are aggregated. Experiments use the frozen class_index_v2
metadata column instead of independently re-deriving labels. The indeterminate category is
retained in the primary endpoint because it represents a substantial part of the prepared cohort
and a meaningful area of reader uncertainty.
The supplementary binary experiment removes indeterminate nodules while preserving orig-
inal patient assignments. It contains 1,000 training nodules (622 benign and 378 malignant),
219 validation nodules (134 and 85), and 209 test nodules (117 and 92). Binary labels use
binary_class_index. The binary task is analyzed separately because both the target and the
evaluated sample composition change.
3.4
Image representations and actual preprocessing
All source crops contain raw HU values and have shape (104, 72, 80) in (Z, Y, X) order at 1-mm
isotropic spacing. Intensities are clipped and scaled as
u(x) = min(400, max(−1000, x)) + 1000

(1)
The common window maps the retained intensity range to [0, 1] and suppresses extreme values
associated with acquisition and padding.
The 2D loader uses axial slice 52, producing a 1 × 72 × 80 tensor. It subsequently standardizes
using training-only statistics: mean approximately 0.365105 and standard deviation 0.314459.
The implemented 2.5D loader determines the central slice from the median z coordinate of
positive mask voxels and extracts z −2 through z + 2, producing 5 × 72 × 80. It uses HU
scaling without additional standardization. Masks are therefore used for 2.5D slice selection,
although they are not input channels. The 3D loader supplies the complete scaled crop as
1 × 104 × 72 × 80, without the 2D standardization step. Validation and test inference use no
stochastic augmentation.
The shared dataset and HU window do not imply identical preprocessing. Mask-dependent
centering in 2.5D, fixed centering in 2D, and additional 2D standardization are retained as
characteristics of the evaluated implementations and considered when interpreting cross-track
differences.
3.5
Network architectures
Both planar tracks use randomly initialized torchvision ResNet-18 architectures with the first
convolution adapted to one or five channels. The input convolution has 64 output channels,
kernel size 7 × 7, stride 2, and padding 3. Native crop dimensions are retained. The 2D classifier
uses a 512-dimensional pooled feature, dropout, and a linear head. The 2.5D classifier replaces
the standard final layer with a three-output head. The three-class models have 11,171,779 and
11,184,323 parameters, respectively.
The final volumetric model is a lightweight 3D ResNet-18, with residual-block counts [2, 2, 2, 2],
channel widths 16, 32, 64, 128, adaptive global-average pooling, dropout 0.20, and a linear head.
It has 2,073,747 three-class parameters. Although the volumetric network has fewer parameters,
its larger input and spatial operations create a different computational and optimization problem.
Equal architectural family does not imply equal capacity, receptive fields, or training cost.

3.7
Momentum-contrast pretraining
MoCo learns representations by comparing two augmented views of the same nodule against a
queue of representations from other samples [3]. For normalized query q, positive key k+, queued
keys kj, and temperature τ, the contrastive loss is

The key encoder follows the query encoder by an exponential moving average. A projection head
is used during contrastive learning and is discarded when the encoder transfers to classification.
The planar 2D implementation follows a MoCo v2-style recipe with an MLP head and stronger
view augmentation [4].
The 2D run uses SGD momentum 0.9 and cosine scheduling; 3D also uses cosine scheduling.
The projection MLP is 512 →512 →128 in 2D and 128 →128 →128 in 3D; the 2.5D output
dimension is 128. These substantially different pretraining budgets mean that the comparison
tests specific track recipes rather than a uniform SSL configuration. The SSL pool is restricted
to training-assigned patients, and risk labels are ignored during pretraining.

3.8
Training augmentation
The 2D mild recipe includes horizontal and vertical flips, affine rotation up to 15◦, translation
parameters up to 0.06 in the implemented normalized grid, scale 0.9–1.1, intensity scale 0.9–1.1,
intensity shift ±0.05, and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.02. Its affine transform is
sampled with probability 0.8; flips have probability 0.5 each, intensity jitter 0.5, and noise 0.3.
Augmentation occurs before standardization.
The 2.5D recipe applies the same flips and intensity/noise settings jointly across all five
channels, preserving their order and correspondence. It does not use the 2D affine transform.
The 3D recipe uses rotations up to 5◦around each axis, translations up to three voxels, intensity
scale 0.95–1.05, shift ±0.03, and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.01. Values are clipped
back to [0, 1]. These representation-specific recipes should be interpreted through within-track
A–C contrasts.

Augmentation and contrastive initialization
The A–C changes in test Macro-F1 are +0.0075 for 2D, +0.0462 for 2.5D, and +0.0475 for 3D. The
positive direction is consistent across tracks; the small 2D difference should not be interpreted as

established superiority given seed variation. The 2.5D report records a train–validation Macro-F1

5
Discussion
5.1
Spatial context and generalization
The strongest observed three-class result is achieved by augmented 2.5D, not by the full-volume
network. A short stack may provide useful local depth context while retaining planar feature
extraction. However, this explanation is a hypothesis about the present implemented tracks. The
baseline 2D and 2.5D scores are almost identical, and several aspects besides spatial context
differ. The comparison does not establish that five slices are intrinsically optimal or that 3D
information is harmful.
The 3D model sees far more input voxels while using fewer parameters and smaller batches.
11


Its learning dynamics are affected by these design choices, normalization, and available labeled
examples. More context can introduce additional variation that a limited cohort does not support
learning robustly. Conversely, another 3D architecture, training budget, crop extent, or regular-
ization recipe might produce a different ranking. The results support selecting representations
empirically under the intended data budget.
5.2
Regularization and self-supervised transfer
The consistent positive A–C direction suggests that mild supervised augmentation is useful in
these experiments. The larger observed changes in 2.5D and 3D, and the reduced reported 2.5D
train–validation gap, support further study of regularization. Because transformations differ
by representation and only 2D has repeated-seed results, the data do not quantify a universal
augmentation effect.
The negative A–B changes are useful evidence about the specific pretraining recipes. Optimizing
instance discrimination does not guarantee that features support the downstream class definitions.
View strength, queue composition, optimization budget, temperature, and fine-tuning can
influence transfer. Multiple nodules from one patient may also enter a queue without being
recognized as related examples. This is a plausible issue for future experimentation, not a
demonstrated cause of the present results. The study cannot conclude that self-supervised
learning in general is ineffective.
5.3
Uncertainty and interpretation of the endpoint
The binary analysis indicates that the intermediate-risk category contributes to a difficult three-
class decision problem. It does not isolate label ambiguity as the only explanation: the binary
cohort is smaller, has a different class balance, and uses two output classes. The 2.5D C error
profile further shows that high indeterminate recall can accompany low benign recall. A strong
aggregate score must therefore be read together with class-specific errors.
The endpoint is agreement with an aggregated reader rating. It is not a measurement of cancer
sensitivity or specificity, prospective benefit, or clinical utility. The three-class labels also have
an ordering that the nominal softmax classifiers do not explicitly encode. Future work could
compare ordinal objectives, retain distributions of reader ratings, or model disagreement directly.
These alternatives would require new experiments rather than reinterpretation of the current
saved scores.
5.4
Limitations and next experiments
First, this study uses one fixed patient partition and no external dataset. Acquisition variation
and domain shift remain untested. Second, repeated-seed reporting is unequal, and nodules
within patients are correlated. Patient-cluster uncertainty estimation and matched repeated runs
are needed before claiming statistical superiority. Third, mask-based centering in 2.5D, additional
2D standardization, unequal loss weighting, and distinct optimization and augmentation recipes
confound a dimensionality-only interpretation.
Fourth, early exploratory runs and parameter sweeps are retained in the repository. Although
checkpoint selection uses validation Macro-F1, the committed artifacts alone cannot establish
complete independence of final design decisions from prior test observations. Any future confir-
matory comparison should preregister its protocol and reserve a new evaluation cohort. Fifth,
the repository lacks redistributed checkpoints and imaging arrays, and some scripts contain
machine-specific paths. Reproduction requires separate dataset access and path configuration.
Finally, radiologist-derived labels are subjective and lack universal pathology confirmation.
12


A stronger follow-up would harmonize centering and intensity processing, standardize the
downstream loss and selection policy, compare computational budgets, repeat every track across
matched seeds, and report patient-level paired differences on an untouched evaluation set. These
steps would distinguish the effect of representation from the behavior of the current three
implementations.
5.5
Scope of comparison with recent methods
The current experiments do not include direct matched evaluations of Cross-ViT, MTST, or
LNMSNet [10, 11, 12]. Our three representation tracks are residual-network implementations,
not three distinct state-of-the-art algorithms. Published numbers from the recent studies are not
used as experimental baselines because their cohort selection, risk aggregation, excluded ratings,
supervision, and test partitions differ. This is a methodological reason to avoid a numerical
cross-study ranking; it does not replace evaluation of those methods on the same test patients.
A matched benchmark would require adapting their classification heads to the three median-
rating classes, preserving the frozen patient manifests, defining which auxiliary annotations
are available during training, and retraining under a documented selection and replication
policy. The existing saved experiments do not provide those comparisons. Accordingly, our
conclusions concern the reported residual-network tracks and training recipes. They neither
establish competitive superiority over recent methods nor attribute the observed ranking solely
to input dimensionality.
6
Conclusion
On the fixed LIDC-IDRI-derived patient-disjoint cohort, augmented 2.5D achieved the highest
observed three-class test Macro-F1 (0.6537). Mild augmentation improved the reported mean
or single-run Macro-F1 across all tracks, while the tested MoCo recipes did not. Removing
indeterminate nodules produced substantially higher scores on a separate binary endpoint. The
findings show that full-volume modeling did not outperform the planar alternatives under the
evaluated data and training constraints. They motivate carefully harmonized and replicated
comparisons, without establishing universal superiority of 2.5D or clinical diagnostic performance.
