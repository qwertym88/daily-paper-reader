Spiking Neuromorphic Control for Periodic Orbits Stabilization in
Underactuated Robots: A Greedy Lyapunov-Based Approach
Surov M.1 and Sumenkov O.1
Abstract— This work investigates the use of event-based
spiking controllers, implemented as spiking neural networks,
for trajectory tracking in underactuated robots. Specifically, we
address the problem of stabilizing a prescribed periodic orbit.
It is well known that, by applying the method of transverse
linearization to the robot dynamics in a neighborhood of
a periodic trajectory, the dynamics can be approximately
described by a linear time-varying system with periodic
coefficients (a periodic LTV system). The problem of orbital
stabilization can therefore be reduced to stabilizing the trivial
solution of this LTV system. We show that a previously proposed
Lyapunov-based greedy design, originally developed for LTI
systems, can also be extended to the corresponding linearized
transverse dynamics, which take the form of a periodic LTV
system. This enables the construction of a feedback controller
for orbital stabilization of the robot. The effectiveness of the
proposed method is demonstrated on the trajectory stabilization
problem for the butterfly robot.
I. INTRODUCTION
Neuromorphic
control
is
an
emerging
paradigm
in
control systems engineering, inspired by the event-driven
information processing of the human brain. Although
the underlying principles of spike-based computation and
neuromorphic engineering date back to the 1990s [1], [2],
their application to control systems has gained renewed
attention in recent years, with successful experimental
demonstrations reported, for example, in [3], [4]. Thus,
spike-based control shows potential for energy-efficient,
robust, and computationally simple control, motivating
further investigation of its use in control systems.
Despite this progress, a significant gap remains between
practical implementations and the theoretical justification
of
neuromorphic
control
systems.
Several
studies
in
neuromorphic control [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12],
among others, have addressed this gap by developing stability
analysis tools for event-driven systems.
In particular, for LTI systems, several approaches have
recently been proposed. The emulation-based approach [8],
[13],
[11]
uses
integrate-and-fire
neurons
with
fixed-
amplitude spikes and provides practical stability guarantees
for the resulting closed-loop system. An alternative, referred
to as the greedy Lyapunov-based approach [10], [12],
generates neuron spikes according to a local minimization
principle, enabling Lyapunov-based stability analysis of the
resulting hybrid closed-loop dynamics.
Building on recent results for LTI systems [12], we extend
spiking neuromorphic control, in which the control input

This control problem arises in many robotic and engineering

be approximated by a hybrid system expressed in local

original problem to the stabilization of an auxiliary hybrid
LTV system. To stabilize this auxiliary system, we employ
the greedy Lyapunov-based approach originally developed
for LTI systems [12]. Building on this framework, we provide
a constructive design procedure for spiking neuromorphic
controllers and establish practical stability of the reference
orbit. The approach is experimentally validated on a butterfly
robot testbed.

In this work, we address practical orbital stability,
which involves boundedness of solutions near the orbit
and attractivity of a neighborhood Uρ(x⋆). While practical
(asymptotic) stability of an equilibrium is a well-established
notion in nonlinear control (see, e.g., [22], [23]), there
is, to the best of our knowledge, no universally adopted
definition of practical orbital stability. We therefore introduce
the following definition.
Definition 1. We say that the trajectory x⋆(t) of the equation

(2)

following conditions hold:
1) For any ε ≥ρ, there exists δ = δ (ε) > 0 such that
any solution of (2) with initial condition x(t0) ∈Uδ(x⋆)
satisfies x(t) ∈Uε(x⋆) for all t ≥t0;
2) Any solution of (2) eventually reaches the tube Uρ(x⋆)

We consider practical orbital stability in a local sense:
for initial conditions far from the orbit, the requirements

of Rn containing the orbit.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
The proposed control law is evaluated on the Butterfly
robot [30], a well-known benchmark for motion control. The
robot represents a planar mechanical system: a figure-eight-
shaped link is mounted to a fixed base through a single
revolute joint and rotates in the vertical plane under a DC
motor, while a small spherical ball rolls passively along
its surface. The link’s angular position is measured by an
encoder, and the ball’s position is estimated in real time from
a vision system.
Assuming ideal rolling, perfect spherical symmetry of
the ball, and rotational symmetry of the mechanism,
the configuration space is topologically a torus. It is
parameterized by two angles: ϑ, the orientation of the link
relative to the base, and ϕ, the ball’s position in a polar
frame attached to the link (Fig. 1). The DC motor generates
the control torque u at the link’s revolute joint axis.
The robot is an underactuated mechanical system with
two degrees of freedom and one control input. Its dynamics
are described by the Euler–Lagrange equations [24], which
can be represented in the control-affine form (1) with
state x ≡(ϑ,ϕ, ˙ϑ, ˙ϕ)⊤and scalar control input u. Exact
expressions for the functions f and g are lengthy and
omitted here for brevity; they can be found in [31], and
the physical parameters are available at https://gitlab.
com/-/snippets/4807338.
Following the algorithm for computing feasible periodic
trajectories for the Butterfly robot [24], we obtained the
trajectory illustrated in Fig. 2. Due to rotational symmetry,
the coordinates ϑ,ϕ advance by the angle π over one
period T = 2.38 sec, and the system returns to an identical
configuration.

V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper has provided a theoretical justification that
neuromorphic control can solve the problem of periodic
orbit tracking. In particular, we have shown that dynamics
of an underactuated robot under event-based spiking control
can be represented as a linear hybrid system with periodic
coefficients. This simplification allowed us to formulate the
firing conditions and establish practical orbital stability of
the reference trajectory.
We have also demonstrated that the proposed controller
design approach is not merely of theoretical interest.
Experiments on the physical robot confirm that the resulting
controller can be implemented in practice, computed in real
time, and remains effective in the presence of disturbances,
measurement noise, and delays — none of which are
accounted for in the analysis. Together, the theoretical and
experimental results establish both the feasibility and the
practical applicability of the proposed neuromorphic control
approach.
The presented results can be readily extended to systems
with m > 1 control signals. A complete theoretical proof of
stability and robustness of the nonlinear system, as well as
the investigation of different firing strategies, is left for future
work.
