Certifying Distributionally Robust Decisions from Projection Observations
Pouya Ahadi 1 Kamran Paynabar 1
Abstract
A scalar measurement can determine a robust de-
cision without identifying the latent covariance
or every decision risk. We study finite Gaussian
projection experiments with a fixed mean-plus-
standard-deviation target. The observation laws
define a set of indistinguishable moment models.
We exhibit decisions that agree throughout that
set despite unidentified risk values, and give a
confidence-set certificate that returns a decision
or an unresolved outcome. We prove soundness,
necessary abstention for indistinguishable models
with incompatible optima, and a fixed-checkpoint
certification guarantee. A scalar profile reduces
nonlinear risk comparisons to convex inner prob-
lems. Across seven synthetic instances, jointly
calibrated moment regions give sufficient budgets
21 to 52 times explicit expected-sample informa-
tion lower bounds. Separately measured stopping
times are lower, with zero decision errors in 1,900
simulated streams. These comparisons do not
constitute matching bounds, and the guarantees
require Gaussian replication.
1. Introduction
In some experiments, a decision produces one scalar re-
sponse while the random factors behind that response re-
main hidden. We study the case in which the response is
a known projection of a fresh latent vector. The aim is
to select a decision with the smallest mean-plus-standard-
deviation risk. A measurement can be informative about this
decision even when it cannot recover the latent covariance
matrix or the risk of every decision.
The observation law is closely related to heteroskedastic
linear experimental design. Weltz et al. (Weltz et al., 2023)
study Gaussian linear responses with an unknown quadratic
variance and transductive best-arm identification. We retain

new Gaussian observation model or a new general principle
that decisions can be identified under partial parameter iden-

decision-region active learning already express that broader
principle (Kasy, 2016; Javdani et al., 2014).
Our question is specific: given a set of scalar projections,
when is the minimizing risk decision determined by their
laws, and when can finite data certify it? Three distinctions
matter. First, identifying the best decision can require fewer
projections than identifying all decision risks. Second, a
certificate that has not fired does not establish nonidentifia-
bility. Third, some restricted query sets admit two models
with identical response laws and different unique optima;
on those instances an honest procedure must retain an unre-
solved outcome.
We develop the identification geometry for this projection
model, a confidence-set certificate with an explicit unre-
solved output, and a fixed-checkpoint stopping guarantee for
classes where the worst-case risk contrasts can be bounded
globally. The certificate and its Gaussian coverage are stated
later. Closed-form contrasts cover the studied examples. For
affine moment models with convex confidence sets, a one-
dimensional profile also gives globally boundable nonlinear
contrasts; its cost beyond the small cases tested here remains
unmeasured. Across seven identifiable Gaussian instances,
one jointly calibrated moment region gives sufficient check-
point budgets 21 to 52 times explicit information lower
bounds. These compare different sample-complexity quan-
tities and do not constitute a matching theorem. Instance
definitions, calibrations, scripts, outputs, and source hashes
are included in the reproducibility archive described in the
appendix.

ranking analysis (Kasy, 2016) already shows that a decision

7. Computational and finite-sample results
7.1. Computing Nonlinear Contrasts
The scalar profile was checked on exact fibers of dimensions
three and five and on an all-unknown confidence set with
seven queries. Valid interval lower bounds and independent
feasible multistart checks agreed to the reported tolerances.
Appendix B gives the four numerical intervals, solve counts,
and qualifications. These calculations use floating-point
solvers and do not establish large-dimensional scaling.
7.2. Certification budgets and information comparisons
Table 1 reports seven identifiable Gaussian instances, in-
cluding a multidimensional-fiber case. All use δ = 0.05 and
predeclared checkpoint schedules. The sufficient budget
is the first checkpoint at which a deterministic good-event
bound makes the certificate positive. The observed mean
stopping time is measured separately by Monte Carlo on the
same schedule. The information column uses (17) for an
explicit opposite-optimum alternative, so the ratio compares
different sample-complexity quantities.
Every identifiable stream certified: 1,900 runs, no decision
errors, and no unresolved outcomes. The observed means
were 2.0–5.3 times below the sufficient budgets. Dividing
these Monte Carlo means by the explicit-alternative informa-
tion bounds gives 5.5–20.5; this is a descriptive comparison,
not a matching bound. On the restricted b = 0.9 witness,
two models with opposite unique optima have identical
query laws. The certificate stayed unresolved in all 300
paired streams under both models, consistent with the struc-
tural abstention result. The observed study uses exact nested
Gaussian sufficient-statistic draws for replicated queries; its
seeds, scripts, and stopping distributions are identified in
the appendix.
Within the four restricted two-query rows, the ratio falls as
∆S shrinks: at larger gaps the rule stops at an early fixed
checkpoint, where calibration and checkpoint overhead are

7.3. Allocation Diagnostic
On one 10-query mixed-projection family, 100 paired
streams gave mean stopping budgets of 6,000 for a strong
fixed design and 5,920 for adaptive tracking. Their paired
difference was −80 ± 113 (standard error), so no reliable
adaptive advantage was observed. Sampling all queries
uniformly cost 30,720 observations on average. This distin-
guishes selecting informative queries from adapting their
allocation; it is not a universal design theorem. Appendix C
gives the full comparison.
8. Discussion and limitations
The projection laws determine a fiber of moment models.
A robust decision can be fixed across that fiber even when
individual decision variances and risk differences vary. The
certificate tests this decision claim against a finite-data con-
fidence set and returns unresolved when the claim cannot
be proved. Theorem 2 shows that unresolved output is
sometimes necessary for a restricted query set. It does not
follow that every unresolved finite-data case is structurally
nonidentifiable.
The computational result is scoped to affine moment models
with a convex parameter and confidence set. It reduces a
nonlinear pairwise risk contrast to a scalar global search
with convex inner problems. The r = 3 confidence-set
probe shows this calculation can work beyond an exact one-
dimensional fiber. The numerical inner and outer bounds
use standard floating-point solvers; they are not interval-
arithmetic proofs of the reported decimal places. Runtime
and bound quality at larger M, r, and decision sets remain
unmeasured. A zero competitor-variance floor needs end-
point handling beyond the positive interval in Proposition 2.
The statistical guarantees require finite queries, fresh Gaus-
sian draws, replication, and simultaneous coverage at the
permitted checkpoints. The fixed-allocation stopping theo-
rem does not establish an advantage for response-adaptive
sampling. The new all-unknown r = 3 instance needs
far more observations than its explicit information lower
bound, so computability alone does not make the cer-
tificate sample-efficient. The coordinatewise confidence
set pays for simultaneous errors in several queried mo-
ments. The calibrated joint region reduced sufficient bud-
gets on all seven identifiable instances, but its scales re-
quire Gaussian replication, and its offline Monte Carlo
calibration is part of the randomized coverage guarantee.
The approximation and numerical solver costs at larger de-
cision sets remain unmeasured. Stacking raw Gaussian
sample means and chi-square sample variances is not it-
self an exact χ2
2|S| statistic. The empirical study is syn-
thetic and small; it does not validate the method on an
industrial deployment. We screened structural-dynamics,
taxi-trajectory, and appliance-meter datasets, but none di-
rectly supplies selectable projections with a known response
map and replicated stationary Gaussian query laws; the
audit is archived in icml_experiments/research/
observed_stopping_20261019/.
The two-node
spring network is a physics-based illustration with simu-
lated loads, not a real-data validation of this certificate. Mix-
tures, heavy tails, unknown feature maps, and data-driven
selection of the DRO radius are outside the theorem.
The robust value here comes from a centered-moment ambi-
guity set. For linear loss, its worst-case value depends only
on the allowed mean shift, even though the set also con-
strains second moments. The ambiguity radius expresses
a chosen risk level; the statistical confidence intervals ex-
press uncertainty about the latent moments. These have
separate roles. The observed lack of adaptive gain over a
strong fixed design in one family should not be read as a
universal allocation theorem. The distinction supported by
the current evidence is that choosing useful queries matters,
while the tested response-adaptive tracker added little once

Impact Statement
This work studies when scalar projection observations sup-
port a reliable risk-based decision and when the available
information requires an unresolved output. The experiments
use synthetic data and do not establish safety or reliability
in an industrial deployment. Such use would require check-
ing the observation model, distributional assumptions, and
numerical certificate in the intended application.
