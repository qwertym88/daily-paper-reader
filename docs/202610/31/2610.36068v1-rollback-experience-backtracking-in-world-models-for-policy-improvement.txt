Abstract
Action-conditioned world models offer a scalable alternative to costly real-world interaction for
improving robot policies. However, existing world-model-based methods largely treat world
models as forward-only surrogates of the physical environment, leaving untapped a capability no
physical environment affords: rewinding to past decision points to explore counterfactual futures.
In this work, we introduce Rollback, a policy improvement paradigm that turns world models from
passive simulators into counterfactual engines: it rolls back to critical decision points, branches
into alternative actions, and contrasts their outcomes to attribute success to individual decisions.
This concentrates imagination where it matters most and distills counterfactual outcomes into
fine-grained, action-level supervision without a separately learned value model. Furthermore, we
build a scalable training system that unifies distributed world-model search with asynchronous
real-robot execution, forming a self-reinforcing cycle: real-world experience continually sharpens
the world model, and a sharper world model in turn drives further policy improvement. We term
the world-model side of this cycle test-time physics grounding, a capability that hand-built physics
simulators lack: while the world model serves as the training environment, real transitions from the
policy it trains correct its dynamics through a lightweight LoRA pipeline. Experiments on three
challenging real-world tasks show that Rollback improves success rates over the strongest baseline
by an absolute 10.67%, 11.11%, and 11.85% on block extraction, ball tossing, and tabletop curling,
respectively, while achieving more stable improvement throughout training. Although initialized
from teleoperated demonstrations far below human level, Rollback surpasses the average success
rate of human players on both ball tossing and tabletop curling within 8 hours of training.

Introduction
Action-conditioned world models have provided simulated environments for robot policy training, offering a
scalable complement to real-world interaction (Hafner et al., 2018, 2019, 2020). By predicting the consequences
of policy-generated actions, they allow policies to collect experience and learn from imagined outcomes without
physical deployment (Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). Recent video world models extend this
paradigm to diverse robotic tasks, supporting policy evaluation (Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b)
and improvement through synthetic experience (Jang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). These
advances further extend to general-purpose robot policies, which can be refined using imagined interactions and
task feedback (Xiao et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Moving exploration and optimization into learned environments
makes policy learning less bound to physical trials.
Despite this progress, existing world-model-based policy learning largely retains the trajectory-centric interaction
protocol of real-world training, using the world model primarily to generate forward rollouts whose intermediate
contexts are discarded afterward. This overlooks a capability unavailable in physical RL: the ability to “rewind and
reconsider.” While a physical environment cannot generally return to an identical intermediate state after execution,
a world model can restore a cached temporal context, rewind to an earlier decision point, and explore alternative

Key insight
Our key insight is therefore to redesign world-model-based policy learning around a training-time ex-
perience backtracking primitive, repeatedly rewinding to and reconsidering decisions for informative data
generation. The world model becomes not merely a surrogate simulator, but a rewindable training environment.
action continuations from the same history. This enables the policy to reconsider what it could have done differently
at a particular decision rather than restarting exploration from scratch.
Fully exploiting this capability, however, requires addressing three key challenges as shown in Figure 1. (i) Limited
informative exploration. Not all decisions are equally worth revisiting: along a trajectory, a few critical decisions
determine the outcome, while alternatives to most others lead to similar results. Independent rollouts cannot identify
these consequential decisions or adaptively allocate exploration among them (Browne et al., 2012; Ecoffet et al.,
2020). (ii) Sparse decision-level supervision. Trajectory-level feedback does not reveal which intermediate actions
determine the outcome (Andrychowicz et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2025). Treating continuations independently preserves
this credit-assignment problem, whereas learning an additional value model introduces extra modeling assumptions
and potential estimation bias (Fujimoto et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2025; Schulman et al., 2015). (iii) Biased world-model
predictions. Branching from policy-selected contexts may shift interactions beyond the world model’s training
distribution, causing prediction errors to accumulate or be exploited during optimization (Chen et al., 2026; Janner
et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020, 2021). Without real-world grounding, imagined gains may not transfer to real robots.
To operationalize training-time experience backtracking and address these challenges, we present Rollback, a policy
improvement paradigm that turns world models from passive simulators into counterfactual engines. Rollback
comprises three key components: (i) Experience-backtracking tree search. Starting from successful and failed
real-world trajectories, Rollback rolls back to intermediate decision points and branches into alternative action
chunks. Monte Carlo tree search adaptively allocates simulation budget toward decisions whose alternatives lead
to divergent outcomes, avoiding exhaustive branching (Browne et al., 2012; Hubert et al., 2021; Silver et al., 2016).
(ii) Tree-structured value estimation. Since sibling branches share a decision context, contrasting their downstream
outcomes isolates the effect of individual actions. Subtree-level outcome aggregation yields fine-grained, action-
level advantage supervision without an additional learned value model. (iii) World model–policy co-evolution. A
scalable training system unifies distributed world-model search with asynchronous real-robot execution. Real-world
experience continually refines the world model, which in turn provides more faithful imagination for search and
policy improvement. We call this test-time physics grounding: after pretraining, while the world model serves as the
training environment for a policy, real transitions collected by that policy correct its predicted dynamics in the states
2


the policy actually visits. Most existing action-conditioned world models are trained offline and then used as fixed
simulators (Guo et al., 2025a; Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024), which suffices for evaluation
but not for policy learning, because an improving policy moves into exactly the states the simulator has seen least.
We evaluate Rollback on three challenging real-world robotics tasks: block extraction, ball tossing, and tabletop
curling. Across these tasks, Rollback improves success rates over the strongest baseline by an absolute 10.67%,
11.11%, and 11.85%, respectively. Under matched interaction budgets, Rollback achieves faster and more consistent
improvements in task success rates. Within 8 hours of training with one physical robot, Rollback surpasses the
average success rate of human players on both ball tossing and tabletop curling, although it is initialized from
teleoperated demonstrations far below human level. Our project website is available at rollback-wm.github.io.
In summary, our contributions in this work can be summarized as follows:
• We propose Rollback, a policy improvement paradigm that exploits experience backtracking to turn world
models from passive simulators into counterfactual engines, supported by a training system unifying distributed
world-model search with asynchronous real-robot execution.
• We design an experience-backtracking search that concentrates imagination on critical decision points, and a
tree-structured value estimation that distills counterfactual outcomes into fine-grained, action-level supervision
without a separately learned value model.
• Extensive experiments on three challenging real-world robotics tasks demonstrate that Rollback achieves higher
success rates and more stable improvement.

Related Work
Robot Policy Learning from Interaction.
Robot policies are commonly improved for downstream tasks and
deployment conditions using task-specific data and interaction feedback (Guo et al., 2025b; Kim et al., 2025;
Physical Intelligence et al., 2025a). For generalist policies including RT-2, OpenVLA, and the π series (Black
et al., 2024; Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025b), supervised fine-tuning learns
task-specific behaviors; OpenVLA-OFT improves adaptation and inference efficiency through optimized action
representations and objectives (Kim et al., 2025). Reinforcement learning further exploits execution outcomes:
iRe-VLA alternates reinforcement and supervised learning for stability (Guo et al., 2025b); VLA-RL learns a
process reward model (Lu et al., 2025); RIPT-VLA uses dynamic sampling and leave-one-out advantages to learn
from sparse success without a value network (Tan et al., 2025); and SimpleVLA-RL scales training through efficient
sampling and parallelization (Li et al., 2025a). For real-world adaptation, RECAP combines demonstrations,
autonomous experience, and expert corrections through advantage-conditioned training (Physical Intelligence et al.,
2025a). However, reinforcement learning on physical robots remains resource-intensive due to costly interactions
and labor-intensive environment resets (Yang et al., 2026; Zang et al., 2026).
World Models for Robotics.
World models support robot learning and planning by predicting the consequences of
candidate actions before physical execution (Hafner et al., 2018, 2019; Yang et al., 2023). Recent advances span
general-purpose world simulation and models tailored to robot interaction. Cosmos-Predict2.5 and LingBot-World
advance controllable video generation and interactive simulation (NVIDIA et al., 2025; Robbyant Team et al.,
2026). UniSim, IRASim, EnerVerse-AC, and Ctrl-World model action-conditioned visual dynamics to support
simulated interaction and policy development (Guo et al., 2025a; Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhu et al.,
2024). DreamDojo further transfers interaction knowledge from large-scale human videos to robot world modeling
through latent actions (Gao et al., 2026). WorldEval, WorldGym, and the Veo robotics simulator study generated
rollouts as proxies for real-world policy evaluation (Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Quevedo
et al., 2025). Beyond simulation, EnerVerse, Genie Envisioner, and LingBot-VA connect future visual prediction
with action generation for robot control (Huang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2025). DreamGen uses
generated videos and inferred actions to construct synthetic training trajectories (Jang et al., 2025), while V-JEPA
2-AC supports robotic planning through action-conditioned prediction in latent space (Assran et al., 2025).
World-Model-Based Policy Improvement.
Recent work increasingly uses action-conditioned world models as
interactive environments for policy improvement (Jiang et al., 2026; Sharma et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2025; Yang
et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025). World-Env combines a video simulator with a VLM-guided reflector that supplies
task feedback and termination signals (Xiao et al., 2025), while WMPO optimizes policies through interactions with
a learned world model (Zhu et al., 2025). World-Gymnast uses VLM-evaluated imagined rollouts for reinforcement
learning and investigates iterative world-model updates using real-world experience (Sharma et al., 2026). To
obtain informative supervision, RISE combines a controllable dynamics model with a learned progress value model
that provides advantages for policy improvement (Yang et al., 2026). WoVR instead emphasizes the reliability
3


of imagined interaction through keyframe-initialized rollouts and world model–policy co-evolution (Jiang et al.,
2026). However, these methods struggle to improve steadily, as forward-only rollouts rarely revisit informative
decisions and learned values may be biased.
Physics Grounding of World Simulators.
Action-conditioned world models are typically trained offline on large
collections of robot or human videos and then evaluated or deployed as fixed simulators (Gao et al., 2026; Guo
et al., 2025a; Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). Their fidelity is measured on held-out data from
the training distribution, and how a simulator should remain physically grounded once a policy starts exploring
it has received little attention. Physics engines offer a contrast: their dynamics are specified by hand, and system
identification can only tune the parameters of a fixed model. A learned world simulator can instead absorb new real
transitions directly, so its physics can be corrected while it is in use. World-Gymnast and WoVR update the world
model with real experience as part of their training loops (Jiang et al., 2026; Sharma et al., 2026). We isolate this
update as a capability of the simulator itself, test-time physics grounding, and show that it matters for steady policy
improvement in world-model-based reinforcement learning.
Tree Search for Policy Improvement.
Monte Carlo tree search allocates a limited simulation budget by balancing
the exploitation of promising branches with the exploration of under-visited ones (Browne et al., 2012). AlphaGo
and Expert Iteration distill search-improved decisions back into a policy network (Anthony et al., 2017; Silver
et al., 2016), MuZero plans with a learned latent dynamics model (Schrittwieser et al., 2019), and Sampled MuZero
extends search to complex and continuous action spaces through action sampling (Hubert et al., 2021). Go-Explore
further shows that returning to previously visited states before exploring substantially improves exploration in
hard-exploration problems (Ecoffet et al., 2020). However, these methods rely on exact simulators or compact
latent models, leaving the ability of video world models to restore past contexts and branch counterfactual futures
unexplored for real-world robot policy improvement.

Preliminaries
3.1
Problem Formulation
Given a language instruction g and an observation ot comprising multi-view images and proprioception, the policy
πθ generates an action chunk at ∈RH×da with horizon H and action dimension da. The action-conditioned world
model Wϕ predicts the next observation and updates its temporal context through (ˆot+1, mt+1) ∼Wϕ(· | mt, at, g),
where mt denotes the retained temporal context. The predicted observation ˆot+1 contains both the multi-view images
and the proprioceptive state, so the policy receives the same inputs in imagination as on the real robot. Our objective
is to improve the policy’s real-world task success by learning from imagined world-model interactions and real-world
experience. A real-world episode τ = (o0, a0, o1, a1, . . . , oT ) ends with a binary outcome yτ ∈{0, 1} indicating
task success, so the objective amounts to maximizing J(πθ) = Eτ∼πθ[yτ] with as little physical interaction as
possible. Real episodes are expensive since each occupies the robot and needs a manual reset, whereas GPU
compute scales with hardware.

4.1
Experience-Backtracking Tree Search
Independent rollouts expend substantial computation on repeatedly generating shared trajectory prefixes, yet provide
limited comparisons of alternative actions from critical intermediate decision contexts. World models enable these
comparisons by restoring saved contexts and simulating alternative continuations from previously explored decision
points. Building on this capability, Rollback organizes reusable decision contexts into a search tree, allowing
exploration to branch from selected nodes without regenerating their shared prefixes.
Decision Context Construction.
Experience-backtracking search requires decision contexts for exploring alter-
native continuations. Using GE-Sim 2.0 as the world model (Qiu et al., 2026), we construct a reference path by
sampling a seed trajectory from offline demonstrations or collected real trajectories and retaining observations at
action-chunk boundaries. Given an action chunk, GE-Sim 2.0 jointly predicts the future frames of the three camera
views and, through its pose-expert head, the robot’s proprioceptive state (seven arm joints and one gripper per arm);
together they form the imagined observation from which the policy proposes the next chunk. Without the predicted
proprioceptive state, the policy would have to infer the arm configuration from generated images alone, which is
too coarse for the precise contacts and release timing that our tasks require. Each non-terminal node serves as a
branching point where the policy proposes alternative actions and the world model simulates their consequences
from the corresponding real observation. The outcome of the sampled trajectory is backed up along the reference
path, so the real attempt itself acts as the first evaluated branch of the tree. We cache the world-model context at each
imagined node, enabling further continuations from the same decision point without regenerating the shared prefix.
MCTS-Guided Exploration.
Building on these reusable decision contexts and inspired by AlphaGo (Silver et al.,
2016), we use Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) (Browne et al., 2012) to concentrate a limited imagination budget
on promising regions of the decision space. By propagating rollout outcomes back to earlier decisions, MCTS uses
evidence from completed explorations to guide subsequent branch selection. Branches with higher observed success
rates receive further exploration, while an exploration bonus preserves opportunities for less-visited alternatives.
This adaptive allocation helps discover successful continuations and examine alternative actions around them,

Policy-Guided Expansion.
Since action chunks are continuous and high-dimensional, the children of a node cannot
be enumerated. We use the policy itself as the proposal distribution: each expansion restores the context of the
selected node and samples a chunk from the highest-bin policy πθ(· | ov, g, b = K) with a fresh noise seed, where
ov is the real or imagined observation at v and b = K is the highest of the K value bins defined in Section 4.2, so the
search explores alternatives that the current policy considers plausible, in the manner of Sampled MuZero (Hubert
et al., 2021). The search stops once the number of simulated action chunks reaches the imagination budget B.
Disagreement-Driven Refinement.
A pair of sibling actions provides a training signal only when their outcomes
differ, and a difference observed from one or two rollouts can arise by chance. Whenever sibling subtrees fall into
different provisional value bins (Equation 4), we push their parent into a priority queue and allocate further rollouts
to the least-visited sibling until every sibling has received at least nconf visits. This concentrates the budget on
comparisons that produce supervision and reduces label noise before they are used for training; Algorithm 2 in
Appendix A gives the complete procedure.
4.2
Tree-Structured Value Estimation
Tree structure connects an action to multiple downstream outcomes and allows alternative actions to be compared
within a shared decision context. We use this structure to obtain chunk-level quality labels without fitting an
additional value function. Because labels are computed relative to sibling actions, decision contexts of different
difficulty contribute comparable supervision.
Chunk-Level Value Estimation.
We estimate the value of each action chunk from the outcomes of its downstream
continuations. Each chunk ai connects a parent node v to an imagined child ui, whose subtree contains the
continuations explored after that action. We obtain their outcomes using the world judge of GE-Sim 2.0 (Qiu
et al., 2026), a reward model that we fine-tune for each task on annotated robot videos with binary cross-entropy
supervision. Given visual observations and the task instruction, the model predicts task completion, which is
converted into a binary outcome yℓ∈{0, 1} using task-specific success criteria and temporal consistency checks.

Test-Time Physics Grounding.
We call the capability that this adaptation provides test-time physics grounding.
Here test time refers to the world model, not the policy: it is the period after the world model’s own pretraining in
which it serves as the environment for policy learning. The initial world model is fine-tuned on expert data, which
covers the contacts and object motions that teleoperators produce but few of those produced by a policy that fails in
new ways or succeeds along new routes; an error there, for example a block that stays upright when it should topple,
can be exploited by search and labeled as success. Grounding needs no extra annotation, since each real transition
(o≤t, at, ot+1) supplies its own target under Equation 6. A LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022) keeps each update small
enough to repeat throughout training without retraining the full model, and DMD restores few-step sampling for
search. The capability is specific to learned simulators: a physics engine has hand-specified dynamics, and system
identification can only tune its parameters. It is also a general requirement for any learned simulator used to train
policies, because an improving policy drifts toward the states that the simulator’s training data covers least. The
frozen-world-model ablation in Section 6.3 disables this grounding.
5
Asynchronous Real–Imagination Training System
Search in a video world model is computationally heavy, whereas real-robot interaction is slow and cannot be
parallelized beyond the number of available robots. A synchronous loop that alternates data collection, search,
and training would leave each resource idle while the others work, so Rollback runs them as concurrent processes.
The three processes also run at different rates: a real episode lasts as long as the robot needs to complete or fail
the task and be reset, a search tree lasts until it spends its imagination budget, which takes different amounts of
time for different seeds, and the learners advance at the rate of their gradient steps. None of them waits for another:
they exchange only data and versioned model snapshots. Section 5.1 describes where each process runs, Section 5.2
how search is scheduled around cached world-model contexts, and Section 5.3 the resulting training loop.

5.1
Cloud–Edge–Device Infrastructure
Figure 3 divides the system into three tiers. All GPU-intensive work, namely search, data storage, and training, runs
in the cloud. Edge sites are located next to the robots and run policy inference, so the cloud is not part of the robot’s
control loop. Devices only sense and actuate.
Distributed Search Pool.
Each search GPU hosts a policy replica, a world-model instance, and a cache of world-
model contexts. The Elastic Orchestrator assigns each worker seed trajectories drawn from offline demonstrations
and collected real trajectories, and the worker builds the tree for a seed without communicating with other workers.
Policy sampling, world-model prediction, and context restoration all take place on the same GPU, so no intermediate
rollout state is sent over the network during search; a worker sends only the supervision extracted from each finished
tree. Because search workers share no state, the throughput of imagination grows with the number of GPUs.
Experience Fabric.
All data exchanged between producers and learners passes through the Experience Fabric. It
stores real trajectories and tree supervision in separate streams because they are used differently: offline demon-
strations and real trajectories seed search and provide direct policy supervision, tree samples provide chunk-level
quality labels, and world-model adaptation uses real trajectories only. Policy batches are sampled from all three
sources to form Dmix and prefetched to the learners. Robots and search workers write whenever data is ready,
learners read whenever they need a batch, and no process waits for another; this shared pool is the only place where
processes exchange data.
Co-Evolution Learners.
The Policy Trainer optimizes Equation 5 continuously with distributed data parallelism
(DDP) on batches prefetched from the Experience Fabric. Concurrently, the WM Trainer performs the test-time
physics grounding described in Section 4.3: it repeatedly adapts the world model to real trajectories with LoRA
SFT followed by DMD, using 400 and 100 steps per adaptation in our experiments. Gradients are synchronized
only within each trainer, and neither trainer waits for the other or for search. Both trainers hand their weights to
the Publish component, which stores them as versioned snapshots and sends policy snapshots to search workers
and edge sites, and world-model snapshots to search workers.
Edge Sites and Devices.
Each edge site runs a Policy Runtime, a trajectory buffer, and a weight cache. A robot
streams observations to the Policy Runtime and executes the returned action chunks with its own low-level controller.

5.2
Search Scheduling and Context Reuse
Worker-Local Contexts.
Experience backtracking branches from states that earlier rollouts have already reached.
Regenerating the prefix of each new branch would cost as many world-model calls as the depth of the branching
node. Each worker therefore keeps its trees and the world-model contexts of their nodes in GPU memory, and a new
branch starts by restoring the cached context of the selected node (Section 4.1). A tree stays on the worker that
holds its contexts until it is finished, and the orchestrator sends new seeds to idle workers.
Version-Pinned Search Jobs.
Trees take different amounts of time because their seed trajectories, depths, and
refinement steps differ. If search paused for every model update, each update would wait for the slowest tree, and the
search GPUs would sit idle while the learners train. Search and training are therefore fully decoupled: workers keep
searching while both trainers keep training. Each search job is pinned to the latest policy and world-model snapshots
available when its tree starts and keeps them until the tree is finished; snapshots published in the meantime are
picked up by the worker’s next tree. Pinning keeps every tree internally consistent: all of its branches are proposed
by one policy and simulated by one world model, so sibling outcomes remain comparable and the cached contexts
stay valid for the whole tree. Supervision from a finished tree enters the Experience Fabric immediately and is
mixed into subsequent policy batches.
Cost of Imagination.
Two mechanisms reduce the GPU time spent on each tree. Context reuse removes the
recomputation of shared prefixes, and DMD reduces the number of sampling steps needed to generate each
new action chunk (Section 4.3). Network traffic is limited to seed dispatch, finished supervision, and weight
synchronization, since all tree operations stay on the worker. Search capacity can therefore be increased by adding
GPUs, whereas real data grows only with the number of robots. As search GPUs are added, the Policy Trainer and
the Experience Fabric must process proportionally more tree supervision, so they eventually limit throughput.
5.3
Training Process
As shown in Figure 4, Rollback runs real-robot execution, world-model search, policy training, and world-model
adaptation concurrently, exchanging information only through the Experience Fabric and asynchronous transfer
of versioned snapshots. The robot continually executes the latest policy it has received and uploads each labeled
trajectory to Dreal; new weights take effect only between episodes, so physical data collection never waits for
9


training. Meanwhile, GPU workers continually run experience-backtracking search from offline demonstrations
and the growing Dreal, and the Policy Trainer continually updates the policy on Dmix with Equation 5, so tree
supervision is used as soon as it is produced. In parallel, the WM Trainer repeatedly adapts the world model to the
accumulated real trajectories (Section 4.3) and publishes each adapted model to the search pool. A policy snapshot
thus reaches the robot at its next episode boundary and each search worker at its next tree, and the trajectories it
collects reach the world model at the next adaptation. Algorithm 1 gives the complete training procedure.
6
Experiments
We evaluate Rollback on three real-world manipulation tasks to assess policy performance and data efficiency. We
investigate the contributions of experience-backtracking search (through the comparison with RISE w/ GE-Sim2,
our reproduction of RISE on the same world model without backtracking), tree-structured supervision, and
world-model adaptation to iterative policy improvement. Specifically, we ask whether Rollback outperforms
existing baselines in the real world, how much each component contributes, whether it improves stably and reaches
human-level performance, and how faithful its world model is.
6.1
Experimental Setup
Tasks and Evaluation.
We evaluate our proposed Rollback and baselines on the AGIBOT Genie-02 robot (AGIBOT,
2026) across three real-world tasks spanning contact-rich manipulation, precise control, and competitive interaction:
• Block Extraction. The robot removes wooden Jenga blocks one at a time from a stacked tower without collapsing
it, testing precise manipulation under stability constraints.
• Ball Tossing. The robot throws a ball into a bucket, with success determined by whether the ball lands inside.
This task tests precise control of the throwing motion and release timing.
• Tabletop Curling. A human and the robot each deliver two stones. Later shots can displace earlier stones, and
the winner is determined by the score based on proximity to the target center. This task tests strategic selection
and precise execution against an opponent.
The policy observes three RGB views, from one head-mounted camera and two wrist cameras, together with the
robot’s proprioceptive state, and controls 16 action dimensions, comprising seven arm joints and one gripper per
side, at 30 Hz. For each task, we select six initial positions (nine for Tabletop Curling) and run 15 trials per position
(25 for Block Extraction); per-position results are in Appendix D.
Implementation Details.
(1) World Model Training. We initialize the world model from pretrained GE-Sim 2.0 (Qiu
et al., 2026) and train it in two stages: supervised fine-tuning followed by distribution matching distillation (Yin
et al., 2024). We fine-tune the model on task-specific data for 10K steps using 32 NVIDIA H200 GPUs with a
global batch size of 128. We then perform DMD on the same dataset for 2,500 steps using 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs
with a global batch size of 16. (2) Policy Warm-up. We initialize the policy from π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al.,
2025b) and pre-train it on task-specific expert-collected data using RECAP-style quality conditioning (Physical
Intelligence et al., 2025a). We use five quality bins, assigning successful trajectories to the highest bin 5 and failed
trajectories to the lowest bin 1. Training runs for 100K steps on 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs with a global batch size
of 64. (3) Reward Model Training. We use the world judge of GE-Sim 2.0 (Qiu et al., 2026) as the reward model
and fine-tune it on task-specific robot videos annotated with success labels. Conditioned on visual observations
and the task instruction, the model predicts task completion using a binary classification head trained with binary
cross-entropy loss. (4) Policy Improvement in World Models. We run policy improvement with one physical robot
and 48 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, allocating 40 GPUs to world-model-based tree search and 8 to policy training with
DDP. Following world-model warm-up, all processes run asynchronously; each world-model adaptation adds newly
collected real trajectories to the replay dataset and adapts the world model through 400 steps of LoRA-based (Hu
et al., 2022) SFT followed by 100 steps of DMD.
Baselines.
We compare Rollback with imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and world-model-based baselines.
All methods use the same π0.5 initialization and comparable compute budgets; RECAP and RISE w/ GE-Sim2 are
trained for a similar duration of about 8 hours with one physical robot and a similar number of NVIDIA H200 GPUs.
Execution-level details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendices B and C. (1) SFT. We fine-tune π0.5 (Physi-
cal Intelligence et al., 2025b) on task-specific expert demonstrations using supervised learning. (2) RECAP. We adapt
RECAP (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025a) to π0.5, using a learned value model to estimate advantages for condi-
tioned policy training on collected real-world data. (3) RISE w/ GE-Sim2. RISE (Yang et al., 2026) learns from imag-
ined interactions with a compositional world model that couples action-conditioned dynamics with a learned progress

6.2
Main Results
Table 1 compares Rollback with the baselines across three real-world tasks. Rollback achieves the highest success
rate on all tasks, reaching 69.33% on Block Extraction, 40.00% on Ball Tossing, and 64.44% on Tabletop Curling.
Rollback’s average success rate exceeds SFT, RECAP, and RISE w/ GE-Sim2 by 17.63%, 13.71%, and 11.21%,
respectively. Compared with RISE w/ GE-Sim2, which also learns through interaction with the same world model,
Rollback improves success rates by an absolute 10.67%, 11.11%, and 11.85% on the three tasks. This corresponds
to a relative improvement of 24.0% in average success rate over RISE w/ GE-Sim2. On average, real-world RL
with RECAP adds 3.92% over SFT and forward world-model rollouts with RISE w/ GE-Sim2 add another 2.49%,
whereas experience backtracking with the same world model adds 11.21% on top of RISE w/ GE-Sim2, more than
the two previous gains combined. The largest relative gain is on Ball Tossing (38.5% over RISE w/ GE-Sim2), where
the outcome is decided by the release, so comparing sibling actions from the same context directly identifies better re-
leases. These gains span contact-rich manipulation, precise throwing, and competitive interaction, indicating that the
benefits of search-based training transfer to physical execution without requiring world-model search at deployment.
6.3
Ablation Studies
We conduct ablations on Block Extraction to examine the contributions of world-model adaptation and tree-structured
value estimation; frozen-world-model results on the other two tasks are reported in Appendix D. We report three
metrics over all evaluation trials: Target Contact measures whether the pushing motion hits the target block; Task
Success measures complete extraction without collapsing the remaining structure; and Consecutive Extractions
counts the average number of blocks extracted in a row before failure. (1) Frozen World Model. We retain the
world model obtained after initial task-specific training and disable subsequent updates from real interaction, which
disables test-time physics grounding. (2) Direct Reward Supervision. We replace subtree-aggregated values with
individual rollout rewards for quality-conditioned policy training while retaining tree search. All variants use the
same policy initialization and computation budget. As shown in Table 2, removing either component degrades all
three metrics; direct reward supervision lowers task success most (59.33%), and a frozen world model reduces
consecutive extractions from 2.60 to 1.90. Target contact also falls from 89.33% to 78.67% with a frozen world
model and to 77.33% with direct reward supervision. With direct reward supervision, each label depends on a single
stochastic rollout rather than a subtree average, and with a frozen world model, prediction errors accumulate over
the long sequences of contact-rich decisions that block extraction requires.

6.4
Training Dynamics
Figure 5 tracks the real-world success rate of Rollback and RISE w/ GE-Sim2 on Ball Tossing and Tabletop Curling
over 8 hours of training. Starting from the RECAP policy, Rollback improves Ball Tossing steadily from 25.56% to
40.00%, whereas RISE w/ GE-Sim2 fluctuates and ends at only 28.89%. This is a relative improvement of 56.5%
within 8 hours using a single physical robot, while RISE w/ GE-Sim2 first drops to 24.44% after 2 hours. Keeping
the world model frozen instead yields only 26.67%, indicating that continually refining the world model with real
experience is essential for sustained improvement. On Tabletop Curling, Rollback rises from 51.11% to 64.44%
over the same period, while RISE w/ GE-Sim2 stays close to its initialization and ends at 52.59%, only 1.48%
above its starting point. On Tabletop Curling, the gap between the two methods widens at every evaluation; on Ball
Tossing, it widens steadily after 4 hours.
6.5
Qualitative Results
Figure 7 visualizes experience-backtracking search on Tabletop Curling: panel (a) shows the search tree, and panels
(b, c) show continuations from a branch point inside imagination and from a rollback on a real attempt. Rather
than restarting from the initial state, Rollback rolls back to two intermediate decision points of a real attempt and
branches from the restored contexts. At step 200 (control steps at 30 Hz), during the second push, the recorded
push brings the red stone into the house, whereas an imagined alternative from the same context leaves it short
of the house. Comparing continuations that share everything up to this point isolates the second push as the decision
that determines the outcome. Branching also happens inside imagination: from the same imagined state at step
50, both continuations push the first stone alike, but one releases the second stone fast enough to reach the house
and the other releases it slowly, so it stops in front. More examples for all three tasks are provided in Appendix E.
6.6
Surpassing Human Performance
We compare Rollback with human players performing each task by hand and with teleoperated data collection, both
under the same initial positions and success criteria; each is averaged over three people. On Ball Tossing, human
players and teleoperation achieve 31.48% and 16.53%, respectively, and Rollback surpasses the human average
after 6 hours of training (33.33%), reaching 40.00% after 8 hours (Figure 6). On Tabletop Curling, human players
and teleoperation achieve 58.39% and 38.94%, and Rollback surpasses humans after 4 hours, reaching 64.44% after
8 hours. Although Rollback is initialized from teleoperated demonstrations far below human level, it surpasses
human players on both tasks by learning from counterfactual imagination. At the end of training, Rollback exceeds
the human average by 8.52% and 6.05% on the two tasks, and teleoperation by 23.47% and 25.50%. The RECAP
initialization already exceeds teleoperation on both tasks (25.56% vs. 16.53% and 51.11% vs. 38.94%), but only
training with Rollback lifts it above human players. The policy never observes the human players, so the margin
over them comes from comparing the robot’s own alternative actions in imagination.

7
Conclusion
We presented Rollback, a policy improvement paradigm that exploits experience backtracking in world models.
By revisiting decision contexts and aggregating the outcomes of alternative continuations, Rollback concentrates
imagination on informative comparisons and obtains chunk-level supervision without a learned value model. A
unified training system couples this search with real-robot execution, jointly improving the policy and the world
model through real-world feedback. This test-time physics grounding keeps the world model accurate in the states
the policy visits. Experiments on three challenging real-world tasks demonstrate higher success rates and more
stable improvement than competing baselines. Over RISE w/ GE-Sim2, our reproduction of RISE on the same
world model, Rollback improves the success rate by an absolute 10.67%, 11.11%, and 11.85% on block extraction,
ball tossing, and tabletop curling, and it surpasses the average human player on the latter two tasks after 8 hours of
training with one robot.

A
Detailed Training Algorithm
Algorithm 1 summarizes the overall training procedure. Real-robot execution, tree search, policy training, and
world-model adaptation run as concurrent processes that never wait for one another. They exchange data only
through the shared pool of the Experience Fabric (Dreal and Btree) and models only as versioned snapshots; each
search tree is pinned to the snapshots that are current when it starts. Algorithm 2 details the experience-backtracking
search, and Algorithm 3 gives the exact rule used to extract tree-structured supervision.

Trial Initialization and Termination.
Before each trial, the robot is returned to a task-specific reset configuration
sampled from a pool constructed from the beginning of successful demonstration segments. This avoids evaluating
every policy from a single fixed arm pose while keeping the reset distribution identical across methods. Object
configurations are then randomized according to the task protocol. Block-extraction episodes have a hard limit of
700 control steps (23.3 s), whereas ball-tossing episodes are limited to 120 steps (4.0 s). An episode terminates early
once the outcome is determined or a safety stop is issued. A tabletop-curling trial ends after the human and robot
have each completed two shots, and the winner is determined from the final stone configuration.
Outcome Annotation.
Real-robot trajectories are assigned binary success labels from their observed physical
outcomes. Block extraction is successful only when the target block is removed while the tower remains intact; a
collapse or an unlabeled termination is treated as failure. Ball tossing is successful only when the ball lands in the
bucket, and all other terminations are failures. Curling is scored only after all four shots so that displacement by
later shots is accounted for. The task-specific GE-Sim 2.0 world judge is used to evaluate imagined world-model
rollouts only; it does not determine the reported real-world success rates.

Figure 15: Typical failures on Ball Tossing. Stroboscopic composites of unsuccessful throws sampled from
real-robot data collected during training.
Figure 15 shows the four failure types we observe on Ball Tossing. Short and overshooting throws come from
errors in release velocity, and rim bounces from small errors in release direction; these three types account for
most failures. Failed releases, where the gripper opens too late and the ball drops near the robot, are less frequent.
All four types are decided within a short window around release, so a single action chunk largely determines the
outcome of the whole episode.

Limitations and Future Work
Rollback relies on a task-specific reward model and on world-model fidelity over long contact-rich horizons. The
fidelity analysis in Section 6.7 shows that prediction error grows along a rollout, and each policy in this work is
24


trained for a single task with one physical robot. Future work includes learning outcome detectors jointly with the
world model and extending Rollback to multi-task policies. Another direction is to scale the system to multiple
robots that share one search cluster.
