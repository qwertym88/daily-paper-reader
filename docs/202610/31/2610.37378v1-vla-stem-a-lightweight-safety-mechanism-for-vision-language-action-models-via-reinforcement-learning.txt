Abstract— Vision-Language-Action
(VLA)
models
have
emerged as a prominent paradigm for end-to-end robotic control,
integrating high-level reasoning with low-level action execution.
However, most existing VLA models are primarily designed
for static manipulation tasks in unconstrained environments,
failing to account for safety-critical scenarios that involve
spatial constraints or dynamic obstacles. Fine-tuning VLAs
on obstacle-avoidance data is a straightforward solution, but
collecting such data is costly, especially for dynamic scenarios,
and may degrade their original task capabilities. To address these
limitations, we propose VLA-Stem, a lightweight, reinforcement-
learning-based safety mechanism for VLA systems. Without
fine-tuning the underlying VLA policy, VLA-Stem converts
its action chunks into motion references and uses trajectory-
level guidance to preserve their spatial intent while allowing
flexible motion and timing adjustments. Conditioned on these
references, robot proprioception, and local obstacle information,
the Stem policy adaptively modifies execution at inference time,
preserving the original task intent while enabling responsive
adaptation to spatial constraints and dynamic obstacles. To
achieve robust and sample-efficient policy learning in complex
environments, we adopt a teacher–student distillation framework
combined with curriculum learning. We evaluate VLA-Stem
on reference following, static spatial constraints, and dynamic
obstacle avoidance. Across six constrained simulation settings,
VLA-Stem achieves 52.8% average collision-free task success
versus 23.7% for the strongest safety baseline, while preserving
base-policy performance in obstacle-free settings; real-world
experiments further show consistent collision reductions under
static and dynamic obstacles. These results demonstrate that
VLA-Stem couples high-level VLA task intent with rapid, reflex-
like responses to immediate physical risks, providing a brainstem-
inspired mechanism for robust robot execution.
I. INTRODUCTION
Embodied intelligence aims to integrate robotics with
artificial intelligence for general real-world interaction, with
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models emerging as a promi-
nent direction. State-of-the-art VLA models [1] adopt end-
to-end architectures that combine high-level capabilities with
low-level action execution. This resembles human behavior,
where deliberate reasoning guides bodily actions to accom-
plish diverse real-world tasks. However, we argue that this
paradigm overlooks another fundamental aspect of biological
intelligence: basic physiological and sensorimotor functions,
such as postural regulation and rapid reflexive responses,

Therefore, VLA policies should not only learn what to do
and how to do it, but also remain responsive to the safety

Building on this perspective, we aim to endow VLA
systems with the ability to react to immediate physical risks
while remaining coordinated with their task-directed behavior
(Fig. 1). Existing approaches mainly follow two directions.
One is to learn collision-aware manipulation behaviors from
large-scale avoidance data [2]; however, collecting such data
is labor-intensive and costly, especially in dynamic environ-
ments, and adapting a pretrained VLA requires additional
training of the task policy itself. Another direction is to
augment a frozen VLA with a conventional safety module. For
example, VLSA [3] introduces control-theoretic constraints
to modify unsafe VLA actions during execution. While
this modular design provides an explicit mechanism for
satisfying local geometric constraints, it primarily focuses
on action-space safety and ignores the coordination between
safety responses and the task-directed behavior of the VLA,

leading to restricted corrective behaviors, reduced task success,
and limited adaptability in complex dynamic environments.
This motivates a mechanism that does not treat safety
adaptation independently of VLA task intent, but instead
tightly integrates safety responses with VLA task execution.
To address these limitations, we propose VLA-Stem, a
lightweight, brainstem-inspired RL-based safety mechanism
that couples VLA task intent with rapid responses to phys-
ical risks. Specifically, VLA-Stem uses the short-horizon
trajectory generated by the base VLA as a reference for
its intended motion, and jointly conditions on this reference
trajectory, robot proprioception, and local obstacle information
to generate executable actions. To support such flexible
execution, we introduce a trajectory-level guidance objective
that preserves the spatial intent of the motion reference while
relaxing strict temporal alignment. We further adopt teacher–
student distillation with curriculum learning to facilitate
structured exploration and improve the learned policy’s
recovery capability under safety-induced deviations. As a
result, VLA-Stem improves execution safety and robustness
without sacrificing task performance or incurring additional
training cost for the base policy.
We evaluate VLA-Stem across multiple manipulation tasks
and scenarios, including obstacle-free execution, static spatial
constraints, and dynamic obstacle avoidance. Results show
that VLA-Stem reduces safety violations while maintaining
base-policy task performance, demonstrating its potential as
an inference-time safety mechanism for VLA policies. Our
main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose VLA-Stem, a lightweight, reinforcement-
learning-based safety mechanism for VLA models that
directly integrates VLA task intent with runtime safety
adaptation to physical constraints, without fine-tuning
the underlying policy.
• We design a trajectory-level guidance objective that
uses the base VLA trajectory as a reference motion,
allowing flexible local motion and timing adjustments
while preserving task behavior. We further introduce a
teacher–student distillation framework with curriculum
learning to improve recovery from obstacle-avoidance-
induced deviations.
• We conduct extensive experiments across multiple manip-
ulation scenarios in both simulation and the real world,
demonstrating improved collision-free task success and
direct sim-to-real transfer of the Stem policy without
real-world training or fine-tuning.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Vision-Language-Action Models
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as
an end-to-end paradigm for robot manipulation, conditioning
action generation on visual observations and language instruc-
tions [4], [5]. Representative systems, including OpenVLA [6],
RT-X [7], [8], RDT [9], π0 [10], and π0.5 [1], have continued
to improve the scale, generalization, and action-generation
capability of robot policies [11], [12].
Although VLA models demonstrate strong generalization,
they remain constrained by high-quality data requirements
and limited robustness in out-of-distribution scenarios. Recent
work has explored reinforcement learning for VLA adaptation,
such as VLA-RL [13] and π∗
0.6 [14]. While these methods
improve task-level robustness, they still primarily optimize
policies for task completion and adaptation, without explic-
itly treating execution safety in challenging manipulation
scenarios as a central objective.
B. Safety for Manipulators
Safe trajectory generation has been widely studied through
sampling-based planners such as RRT [15] and PRM [16],
optimization-based methods such as CHOMP [17], Tra-
jOpt [18], and STOMP [19], as well as MPC. Although
widely used, these methods often require substantial manual
effort due to their complexity and the need for extensive
retuning across different scenarios. Learning-based meth-
ods [20], including Motion Planning Diffusion [21], Deep
Reactive Policy [2], and URPlanner [22], improve efficiency
and generalization through demonstrations or reinforcement
learning [23]. However, they typically act as standalone
trajectory generators and are difficult to directly integrate with
VLA policies without disrupting the task-directed behavior
of the base policy.
Beyond trajectory generation, recent work has also explored
safety mechanisms for end-to-end policies. CBF-based safe
RL methods [24], [25] enforce safety through constrained
policy optimization, with SafeVLA [26] extending this
direction to VLA policies, while safety filters such as
RAIL [27] augment frozen policies with external safety
modules. However, filtering-based corrections can become
less reliable in complex or dynamic environments and may
compromise base-policy performance on challenging tasks.
C. Test-Time VLA
Test-time VLA [28] is a recent paradigm that adjusts VLA
actions during deployment, avoiding costly fine-tuning. VLA-
Reasoner [29] improves actions through online MCTS, while
V-GPS [30] selects candidate actions using a pretrained value
function. However, these methods evaluate multiple candidate
action sequences at inference time, boosting performance at
the expense of real-time efficiency. In contrast, our method
performs lightweight VLA-conditioned execution with a
compact Stem policy, enabling efficient safety augmentation
without candidate search or VLA fine-tuning.

B. Trajectory-Level Guidance for Spatial Intent Preservation
Many runtime safety mechanisms operate by correcting
the current nominal action at each control step. Such local
correction can satisfy immediate physical constraints but
does not account for how the corrected robot state affects
the remaining VLA action sequence. The same issue arises
when step-wise action or waypoint alignment is directly used
to guide RL exploration and sampling. The resulting robot
state may therefore become misaligned with subsequent time-
indexed actions or waypoints along the reference trajectory.
Repeated step-wise correction or alignment can therefore
produce discontinuous corrections and accumulate deviation
from the VLA-directed motion. This accumulated mismatch
can further shift closed-loop execution away from the state
distribution expected by the base VLA.
To address this issue, we introduce trajectory-level guid-
ance. Instead of aligning execution with a fixed action or
waypoint at each time step, we measure its deviation from
local segments of the VLA reference trajectory. This preserves
spatial consistency with the task-directed motion at the
trajectory level. We further preserve forward progression
while relaxing strict temporal correspondence.

such states with reinforcement learning alone is challenging,

the reference trajectory. The third stage introduces moving
obstacles, requiring the policy to jointly adjust local motion
and execution timing, including waiting when necessary
before resuming task progression.

For each task, we construct three scenarios: normal, static,
and dynamic. The normal scenario contains no additional
obstacles and evaluates whether VLA-Stem preserves base-
policy task performance. The static scenario uses two obstacle
placement strategies. The first places obstacles near the
target object or placement region to constrain grasping and
placement. The second samples points along the nominal
object-to-placement path and applies random lateral offsets
to generate obstacles around the transfer route. Placements
are checked for workspace validity and overlap.
In the dynamic scenario, moving obstacles cross the robot’s
path during either approach or object transfer. We sample a
crossing region along the task motion and generate an obstacle
trajectory with a randomized starting position, direction, and
constant speed. Obstacle crossing times are sampled within
a temporal window corresponding to the robot’s expected
passage through this region, inducing interaction during
execution. This setting requires the policy to adapt both its
motion and execution timing (Fig. 3).
We compare VLA-Stem with three types of baselines: (1)
the base VLA policies, π0 and π0.5, which reflect the original
performance without an additional safety mechanism; (2)
VLA-finetune, which fine-tunes a separate VLA policy for
each task and scenario using RRT-based obstacle-avoidance
trajectories, with an additional wait primitive in dynamic
scenarios. We evaluate VLA-finetune under multiple demon-
stration budgets and report the best-performing setting in
the main results. In our experiments, performance peaks at
1,500 demonstrations, while increasing the data to 2,000
leads to a decline; and (3) VLSA, a plug-and-play safety
baseline using the same π0.5 checkpoint and ground-truth
obstacle information as VLA-Stem. VLSA applies CBF-based
corrections to the nominal end-effector motion converted from
VLA joint actions, leaving gripper commands unchanged. We
evaluate VLSA-EE with end-effector constraints and VLSA-
ARM with additional constraints on collision-prone arm links.
For each task and scenario, we tune the CBF gain, geometric
scales, and optimization weights on validation episodes and
report the configuration achieving the highest SUCC.
We report two metrics: (1) SUCC, the proportion of
episodes completed without constraint violations or colli-
sions; and (2) COLL, the proportion of episodes with such
violations. For each trained policy, every task–scenario setting
is evaluated over 3,200 episodes.
Training details. Both teacher and student use MLP actor–
critic networks. For trajectory following, PPO uses 256
parallel environments, a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, 8 update
epochs, and a minibatch size of 3,200. For the static and
dynamic stages, the corresponding values are 192, 1.5×10−4,
12, and 19,200. All stages use a rollout horizon of 100 steps,
a discount factor of γ = 0.8, and the Adam optimizer. Using
GPU-parallel simulation, the stage-wise training budgets for
the evaluated checkpoints are approximately 38.0M, 23.7M,
and 20.6M simulated environment transitions per seed for
trajectory following, static, and dynamic training, respectively,
including both teacher and student optimization.

Safety–Task Trade-off. Under static and dynamic con-

Increasing obstacle-avoidance supervision through VLA-
finetune improves some constrained settings, but its per-
formance remains inconsistent across tasks. Our failure
analysis suggests that a single VLA policy has difficulty
simultaneously maintaining fine-grained task execution and
acquiring reliable safety responses. Even in relatively low-
risk cases where no substantial avoidance maneuver is
required, the fine-tuned policy can fail on behaviors that the
raw VLA performs successfully, particularly during precise
manipulation; for example, our failure analysis frequently
observes grasp failures on irregular objects such as the carrot
in Object Stowing, where the original π0.5 policy often
succeeds. Meanwhile, substantial collision rates remain in
several static and dynamic settings despite the additional
avoidance supervision. Together, these observations indicate
that directly incorporating obstacle-response behavior into
the VLA can interfere with its original manipulation behavior
without consistently establishing reliable safety adaptation.
VLSA exhibits a different type of limitation. Its explicit
safety optimization can substantially reduce collision rates,
but in practice, lower collision rates do not necessarily
translate into successful task execution. On PickCube-Static,
for example, VLSA-ARM reduces COLL to 0.2253, slightly
below the 0.2316 of VLA-Stem, yet achieves only 0.1516
SUCC compared with 0.5219 for VLA-Stem. To better
understand the gap between collision reduction and task
success, we inspect the collision-free failures of VLSA.

motion and lead to grasp failures; when large obstacles
block the transfer path, geometric avoidance can instead push
execution away from the VLA’s nominal motion distribution,
making subsequent task recovery difficult. In dynamic scenes,

C. Real-World Evaluation
We evaluate VLA-Stem across two real-world manipulation
tasks, object stowing and stacking, on a real UR7e robotic
arm equipped with two RealSense D435i cameras for VLA
observations. For each task, we collect 50 teleoperated
demonstrations to fine-tune the real-world VLA policy. During
execution, obstacle poses are estimated using AprilTag [35]
with an additional RealSense D455 camera and converted into
local obstacle representations. The safety policy is trained
only in simulation and directly deployed on the real robot,
aided by low-dimensional inputs with smaller domain gaps
than raw visual observations.
We use the same SUCC and COLL metrics as in simulation
and conduct 15 trials per task and scenario. Fig. 5 reports
results on both object stowing and stacking. In normal settings,
VLA-Stem retains the observed base-policy success rates.
Under static and dynamic obstacles across both tasks, VLA-
Stem reduces collision rates from 73–100% to 20–40% and
increases task success from 0–20% to 47–60%. These results
further demonstrate the effectiveness of the simulation-trained
Stem policy in real-world deployment.
V. CONCLUSION
We propose VLA-Stem, a lightweight RL-based safety
mechanism that couples frozen VLA motion references
with responsive obstacle avoidance. Trajectory-level guidance
allows local spatial and timing adjustments while preserving
the intended motion progression, and teacher–student training
supports recovery from execution deviations. Simulation
and real-world experiments demonstrate improved collision-
free task success under static and dynamic obstacles while
retaining base-policy performance in obstacle-free settings.
Direct deployment of the simulation-trained Stem policy on
the real robot further demonstrates its practical potential.
The current evaluation focuses on structured manipulation
tasks and collision avoidance using low-dimensional obstacle
representations. Future work will extend the framework to
longer-horizon tasks and contact- or force-related safety
objectives, while integrating robust visual perception to
improve reliability under noisy obstacle observations.

2) Policy Architecture: The deployable Stem policy adopts
an MLP-based actor–critic architecture with three independent
feature encoders. The VLA reference trajectory is flattened
and processed by a three-layer MLP with hidden dimensions
(256, 256, 256). The proprioceptive and obstacle inputs are
encoded by two-layer MLPs with dimensions (256, 256) and
(64, 64), respectively. ReLU activations are used between
layers.
The encoded features are concatenated into a 576-
dimensional representation shared by the actor and critic.
Both heads contain three hidden layers of dimensions
(1024, 1024, 512) with ReLU activations. The actor parameter-
izes a seven-dimensional diagonal Gaussian action distribution
with a learned, state-independent log standard deviation.
Actions are passed through a tanh transformation, while
deterministic inference uses the tanh-transformed action mean.
The critic outputs a scalar state-value estimate.
The deployable Stem policy contains approximately 4.56
million trainable parameters. During teacher–student train-
ing, the privileged teacher additionally receives task-related
state information, resulting in a 23-dimensional state input,
whereas the deployed student uses only the 16-dimensional
proprioceptive input.

D. Baseline Implementation Details
1) Base VLA Policies: We report the raw π0 and π0.5
policies as unmodified baselines. For each simulation task,
the corresponding base VLA is fine-tuned using 100 demon-
strations for 30,000 optimization steps; its checkpoint is then
held fixed when comparing runtime safety mechanisms and
training Stem. This ensures that Stem and the safety-layer
baselines are evaluated with the same task-level VLA policy.
2) VLA Fine-Tuning Baseline: The VLA-finetune baseline
trains separate policies using planner-generated obstacle-
avoidance demonstrations. We generate these trajectories
using RRT-based planning; dynamic scenarios additionally
employ a waiting primitive. We vary the number of obstacle-
avoidance demonstrations and report the best-performing con-
figuration in the main comparison. The evaluated performance
peaks at 1,500 demonstrations, while increasing the budget
to 2,000 results in a decline, as discussed in the main paper.

We use batched PyTorch-based QP solvers. VLSA-EE

c) Parameter Tuning: For each task and scenario, we
tune the CBF gain, geometric scales, and optimization weights
on validation episodes. The configuration achieving the
highest collision-free task success (SUCC) is selected for
the corresponding setting.
d) Dynamic Obstacle Handling: In dynamic scenarios,
the obstacle geometry is updated online using the current
ground-truth obstacle observations. Each obstacle is repre-
sented by its center, full dimensions, orientation, and presence
indicator, forming an 11-dimensional feature vector, with up
to three obstacle slots.
At each control step, VLSA reconstructs the obstacle
ellipsoids and updates the corresponding CBF constraints
using their current poses. The implementation does not esti-
mate obstacle velocities or predict future obstacle trajectories.
It therefore performs reactive safety correction based on
instantaneous geometric information rather than predictive
dynamic-obstacle avoidance.
