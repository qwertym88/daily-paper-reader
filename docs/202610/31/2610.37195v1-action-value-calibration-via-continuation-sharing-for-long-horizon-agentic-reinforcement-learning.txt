Action-Value Calibration via Continuation Sharing for Long-Horizon
Agentic Reinforcement Learning
Yingjian Chen1,2, Lang Zhou1,2, Zixin Zhao1,2, Pingfan Li1,2,
Weishi Zheng1,2, Zhilin Zhao1,2*
1Sun Yat-sen University, 2Shenzhen Loop Area Institute
Abstract
Reinforcement
learning
for
long-horizon
agents relies on feedback from sequences of
interactions, making credit assignment to indi-
vidual actions difficult. Existing methods such
as GiGPO compare actions at shared states
to compute relative advantages, but estimate
each action’s value from a single sampled con-
tinuation. The resulting local credit remains
sensitive to decisions made later in the trajec-
tory.
To address this problem, we propose
ConCal (Continuation Calibration), a recur-
sive continuation-value estimator that incor-
porates continuation evidence already present
in a rollout batch.
ConCal matches visits
from the same task by their incoming state-
action-state transitions, combines shared con-
tinuation evidence with each visit’s own tran-
sition estimate, and propagates calibrated val-
ues backward to refine earlier action-value es-
timates. This reduces dependence on individ-
ual sampled continuations and mitigates local
credit distortions without additional rollouts.
Held-out return diagnostics show lower error
in within-group value differences and higher
agreement with reference action orderings than
estimates based on individual continuations.
With Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, ConCal improves
success rates over GiGPO by 3.39 and 5.86
percentage points on ALFWorld and WebShop,
respectively. Our code is available at https:
//github.com/Yingjian-Chen/ConCal.

The rollout batch also contains evidence beyond
each individual continuation. Existing approaches
reuse cross-trajectory structure through transition-
level advantage averaging and graph-based credit
estimation (Li et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026; Yao
et al., 2026). For a given task, repeated transitions
with the same starting state, executed action, and
resulting state link that action to several observed
continuations, as illustrated in Figure 1(c). The
visits following these transitions provide shared ev-
idence about subsequent returns, while each visit
records a particular reward and successor within
its own trajectory. A single pooled value removes
these differences from the estimates assigned to
matched visits. Our insight is to calibrate action
values by combining shared continuation evidence
with each visit’s own transition estimate and propa-
gating the resulting estimates backward to earlier
decisions.
Accordingly,
we
propose
Continuation
Calibration (ConCal),
a recursive estimator
of continuation values.
ConCal matches visits
by their incoming state-action-state transitions
and blends the transition estimate of each visit
with the mean estimate of matched visits. This
partial sharing retains a separate estimate for
each visit. Recursive updates carry the calibrated
continuation values backward along recorded
trajectories, allowing earlier action-value estimates
to incorporate evidence from other rollouts. Each
action is evaluated from its own observed reward
and the calibrated continuation value of its actual
successor, without collecting additional rollouts.
The illustrative held-out diagnostics in Figure 2
show lower error in relative values and greater
local credit consistency than estimates based on
individual continuations.
In summary, our contributions are as follows: (1)
Analysis of Local Credit Assignment. We ana-
lyze the dependence of return-based local credit on
individual sampled continuations through credit-
conflict measurements and held-out relative-value
diagnostics. (2) Calibrating Local Action Val-
ues. We introduce ConCal, which combines shared
continuation evidence from matched visits with
each visit’s own transition estimate through recur-
sive updates to refine local action-value estimates
within the existing rollout batch. (3) Empirical
Evaluation. With Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Con-
Cal improves success rates over GiGPO by 3.39
and 5.86 percentage points on ALFWorld and Web-
Shop, respectively, and achieves higher reported
mean success rates than GraphGPO on both bench-
marks. Ablations support recursive sharing and
retention of individual transition estimates, while
diagnostics examine relative-value magnitudes and
action orderings.

Related Work
Reinforcement learning for language agents.
Reinforcement
learning
(RL)
has
been
ap-
plied to align language models with human
preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022) and train
agents through interaction with external environ-
ments (Carta et al., 2023). Multi-turn RL enables
agents to learn sequential decision-making from
environment feedback (Zhou et al., 2024), with ap-
2


plications spanning device control (Bai et al., 2024),
web navigation (Wei et al., 2025), and search-
augmented reasoning (Jin et al., 2025). Recent
frameworks further support long-horizon training
across diverse interactive environments (Xi et al.,
2026). Our work focuses on credit assignment in
long-horizon agentic RL.
Group-based local credit assignment.
Group-
based RL estimates relative advantages from sam-
pled rewards without a learned value baseline (Ah-
madian et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024). Subse-
quent work improves optimization and sampling
to address training bias and instability (Liu et al.,
2025; Yu et al., 2025). For multi-turn agents, lo-
cal comparisons extend from shared state anchors
to groups that account for historical context (Feng
et al., 2025; He et al., 2026). Our work focuses
on calibrating the action-value estimates entering
these local comparisons through shared continua-
tion evidence from matched visits.
Cross-trajectory
credit
estimation.
Shared
structure across trajectories provides additional
evidence for step-level credit assignment. Tree-
GRPO adopts tree-search sampling and computes
relative advantages within and across trees (Ji
et al., 2026). SALT averages trajectory-level advan-
tages over matched state-action-state transitions (Li
et al., 2026). GraphGPO aggregates rollouts into a
state-transition graph and derives step-level credit
from shortest-path distances to successful termi-
nal states (Cheng et al., 2026). GRAFT estimates
state values through Bellman updates on a trajec-
tory graph and derives step-level credit from these
values (Yao et al., 2026). PGPO augments step-
level advantages by incorporating differences be-
tween state potentials estimated from grouped re-
turns (Zheng et al., 2026). In this paper, we in-
troduce ConCal to calibrate local action values
by recursively combining shared continuation evi-
dence with visit-specific transition estimates, with
an in-depth analysis revealing how calibration and
normalization shape action preferences and local
credit.

5.6
Case study
We present a WebShop case study of local credit
assignment for web agents (Figure 8). The task
requires Zest color and size 5X, but the agent has
selected only the color. GiGPO estimates each ac-
tion’s value from its own sampled continuation. It
assigns negative step advantage to Select 5X be-
cause its continuation ends in task failure, while
favoring Repeat Zest, whose trajectory succeeds,
yielding a value gap of ∆G = −6.9834. ConCal
calibrates action-value estimates using evidence
from multiple continuations and reverses this pref-
erence, with ∆eQ = +0.6452. It assigns positive
step advantage to Select 5X despite its failed sam-
pled continuation, favoring completion of the re-
quired size selection. This case illustrates how
ConCal uses shared continuation evidence to as-
sign positive local credit to useful actions despite
failures in their own sampled continuations.
6
Conclusion
We introduce ConCal, a recursive continuation-
value estimator for long-horizon agentic reinforce-
ment learning. ConCal combines evidence from
matched visits with the transition estimate of each
visit and propagates calibrated continuation val-
ues to earlier decisions without additional rollouts.
Experiments on ALFWorld and WebShop show im-
proved task performance, with ablations supporting
both recursive evidence reuse and visit-specific es-
timation. Further analyses show that shared contin-
uations calibrate preference strength and improve
agreement with action orderings derived from held-
out returns. These findings support calibrating rel-
ative action-value estimates with shared continua-
tion evidence from existing rollout groups in the
evaluated settings.
Limitations
The matching rule of ConCal relies on observed
incoming transitions and does not enforce agree-
ment in the full interaction history, hidden state,
or remaining interaction budget. Such differences
can introduce bias into the shared estimates incor-
porated into subsequent recursive updates. The
extent of continuation reuse also depends on the
availability of matching visits within the rollout
batch. Future work could refine matching with
relevant historical information while maintaining
sharing coverage, and account for uncertainty in
continuation estimates.

Appendix
A
Benchmark Details
A.1
WebShop
WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) is a simulated shop-
ping environment in which agents purchase prod-
ucts that satisfy natural-language requests. Fol-
lowing the WebShop setting of GraphGPO (Cheng
et al., 2026), we adopt the text-rich interface, which
presents page content and identifies interactive el-
ements. We conduct experiments in the 1,000-
product environment with programmatically gen-
erated goals. Goals with indices 0–499 form the
evaluation pool, and the remaining goals form the
training pool. Each episode allows up to 15 en-
vironment interactions. The environment assigns
the purchased product a match score in [0, 1] based
on its type, attributes, selected options, and price
relative to the request. We report task score as the
mean match score multiplied by 100 and success
rate as the percentage of episodes with a match
score of 1. Task score captures partial satisfaction
of the requirements, whereas success rate measures
complete task fulfillment.
A.2
ALFWorld
ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) provides house-
hold tasks that require agents to follow natural-
language instructions through sequential interac-
tions. The six task categories are Pick, Clean, Cool,
Look, Heat, and Pick2. These tasks involve locat-
ing objects and completing prerequisite actions in
the appropriate order. Training tasks are drawn
from the 3,553 games in the training split, and eval-

held-out seen split. Each episode allows up to 50
environment interactions. An episode is successful
when all task goal conditions are satisfied within
this limit. We report success rates for each task
category and an overall success rate (All) computed
across all evaluated episodes.

Discussion with Related Methods
G.1
GraphGPO
GraphGPO (Cheng et al., 2026) constructs step re-
wards from shortest-path distances to successful
terminal states. ConCal focuses on calibrating the
action-value differences used for local credit. It
recursively combines shared continuation evidence
with each visit’s own transition target, then evalu-
ates actions using their recorded rewards and actual
successors. Its credit therefore reflects estimated
discounted returns, while GraphGPO’s credit re-
flects distance to success on the merged graph. For
actions from the same state with equal transition
costs and equally distant successors, GraphGPO
assigns identical distance-based step rewards. Con-
Cal can still distinguish their action values through
differences in recorded rewards and sampled con-
tinuation evidence, with partial sharing retaining
each visit’s contribution.
G.2
SALT
SALT (Li et al., 2026) averages trajectory-level ad-
vantages across matched transitions to assign step-
level credit. ConCal instead recursively calibrates
continuation values by partially pooling targets
r+γ bV across visits matched by their incoming tran-
sitions, retaining each visit’s own transition contri-
bution. It constructs action values from recorded
rewards and calibrated continuation estimates at
their actual successors, then derives local advan-
tages through state-group centering.
Recursive
15


propagation also reaches earlier actions whose suc-
cessor visits have no direct cross-trajectory match.
SALT focuses on refining trajectory-level advan-
tages into step-level credit, whereas our study cen-
ters on continuation-value estimation before local
credit is formed. We provide an in-depth empiri-
cal analysis of how calibration changes preference
strength and action ordering, and how normaliza-
tion preserves or suppresses the resulting value
gaps in local credit.
G.3
GRAFT
Concurrent work GRAFT (Yao et al., 2026) ex-
plicitly constructs a trajectory graph and estimates
shared node values through empirical Bellman iter-
ation. It derives step advantages from node-value
differences and further refines them with Graph
GAE. ConCal instead partially shares continua-
tion evidence across visits matched by incoming
transitions while retaining a separate estimate for
each visit (Equation 9). Its recursive updates op-
erate directly on recorded visits without explic-
itly constructing a transition graph. Action values
combine recorded rewards with calibrated contin-
uations at actual successors, with the state-group
mean providing the local baseline. GRAFT fo-
cuses on graph-based state-value recovery and step-
advantage construction, whereas our study cen-
ters on partial continuation calibration and its ef-
fects on relative action values. Using held-out re-
turns, our in-depth empirical analysis distinguishes
preference-strength calibration from action-order
changes: Gap-matched reproduces most of Con-
Cal’s centered-MSE reduction relative to Own-MC
through magnitude adjustment, while ConCal also
improves ordering agreement.
