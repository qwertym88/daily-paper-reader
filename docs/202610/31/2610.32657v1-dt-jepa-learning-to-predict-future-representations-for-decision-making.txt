ABSTRACT
Decision Transformer (DT) has demonstrated strong performance in various of-
fline reinforcement learning (RL) tasks by conditioning action generation on ex-
pected future returns and historical trajectories. However, treating expected future
returns as the condition provides an incomplete description of the future, as tra-
jectories with different state transitions and behaviors may share similar returns.
Moreover, expected future returns inherently depend on task-specific reward func-
tions, which makes the policy learning reliant on reward-labeled datasets and re-
stricts its scalability to tasks with similar but unseen rewards. Recently, Joint Em-
bedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) has emerged as a promising paradigm of
self-supervised learning-based world models, which learns predictive representa-
tions of future dynamics in latent space rather than reconstructing future states in
the original space. Inspired by JEPA, we propose DT-JEPA, an offline pretraining
and online finetuning framework that learns general knowledge from reward-free
offline trajectories and achieve excellent adaptation to the unseen tasks. Instead
of predicting a single deterministic future embedding, we design a novel prob-
abilistic predictor to model the conditional distribution of future representations
in the latent space, thereby capturing both predictive information and uncertainty
about future dynamics. The predicted future representation is then utilized to
guide DT’s action generation. To generalize to tasks with unseen reward ob-
jectives, we further develop a critic-guided fine-tuning mechanism to efficiently
adapt the pretrained policy to unseen reward functions with limited online inter-
actions. Theoretically, we show that 1) the JEPA objective seeks to maximize a
variational lower bound on the mutual information between historical representa-
tions and future trajectories; and 2) predictive future-conditioned policy learning
admits a finite-sample performance bound with an O(N −1/4) estimation term.
Experiments on D4RL benchmarks demonstrate that DT-JEPA achieves efficient
adaptation across varying offline RL tasks, outperforming traditional DT-based
and other offline RL methods.

INTRODUCTION
Complex decision-making domains such as robotic control, autonomous driving, and embodied
intelligence often involve large-scale offline datasets collected across diverse tasks and policies.
Learning general decision knowledge from such heterogeneous data and efficiently adapting it to
new downstream tasks is becoming increasingly important (Levine et al., 2020). Motivated by the
success of pretraining–finetuning paradigms in computer vision (Chen et al., 2020; He et al., 2022)
and natural language processing (Devlin et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020), reinforcement learning
(RL) research has recently focused on leveraging offline trajectories to learn reusable policies and
representations that can generalize across tasks. Offline RL provides a natural foundation for this

direction, as it enables policy learning entirely from previously collected experience without requir-
ing additional interaction with the environment. More recently, inspired by the strong sequence
modeling capability of Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017), subsequent studies have reformulated
offline policy learning as a conditional sequence modeling problem (Chen et al., 2021), offering a
new perspective for offline RL.
Within the CSM formulation, policy inference is conditioned on a specific target together with the
historical context. Following this paradigm, the representative Decision Transformer (DT) condi-
tions action generation on desired future returns and has demonstrated strong performance across a
wide range of offline RL tasks. Despite its effectiveness, return-oriented CSM methods still suffer
from several limitations. On the one hand, expected future returns provide an ambiguous descrip-
tion of future behaviors, since distinct future trajectories with different state transitions and action
patterns may share similar cumulative returns. On the other hand, return conditioning inherently
relies on task-specific reward functions, restricting policy pretraining to reward-annotated trajecto-
ries and thereby limiting scalability and transferability. To alleviate these limitations, Generalized
DT (GDT) extends return conditioning to generalized hindsight statistics extracted from future tra-
jectories (Furuta et al., 2022), while Pretrained DT (PDT) encodes future trajectory segments into
embeddings for future-conditioned action generation (Xie et al., 2023). However, these approaches
still rely on realized future trajectory information during pretraining and require appropriate future
conditions to be specified or selected at deployment. This raises an important question: How can
we explicitly learn the distribution of future dynamics in the latent space solely from observable
history, while ensuring the predicted representations preserve sufficient information about the future
to guide DT’s action generation?
Recent advances in offline RL have begun to explore policy learning under reward-free and unsu-
pervised pretraining paradigms (Touati & Ollivier, 2021; Park et al., 2024; Bagatella et al., 2026).
Since the transition dynamics p(s′ | s, a) characterize how the environment evolves under differ-
ent actions, agents can still extract substantial decision-relevant knowledge from offline trajectories
without explicit reward supervision. World models provide a natural framework for capturing such
dynamics by learning predictive models of environment transitions (Hafner et al., 2023; 2019). How-
ever, conventional generative world-modeling approaches often rely on observation reconstruction
or generation objectives, which may require modeling fine-grained details that are not directly rel-
evant to downstream decision making. Recently, Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA)
have emerged as a promising alternative for predictive representation learning (LeCun et al., 2022;
Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024). Rather than reconstructing future observations in the orig-
inal input space, JEPA predicts their latent representations from observed context, encouraging the
model to capture abstract information that is predictive of future dynamics.
Inspired by this paradigm, we propose DT-JEPA, a generalizable offline pretraining and online fine-
tuning framework that learns transferable decision knowledge from reward-free offline trajectories
and efficiently adapts to unseen downstream tasks. First, to capture the predictive information and
uncertainty about future dynamics, we design a novel probabilistic predictor to model the conditional
distribution of future representations in the latent space. Second, we incorporate the predicted future
representation into DT through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) (Peebles & Xie, 2023), dy-
namically modulating historical representations based on the future-predictive information to guide
DT’s action generation. Finally, to adapt the pretrained policy to a new task with an unseen reward
function, we develop a critic-guided online fine-tuning mechanism. The DT-JEPA policy and critic
are jointly optimized on newly collected trajectories, where the critic estimates action values un-
der the target reward and provides Q-value guidance for adapting the policy toward the new task
objective.
Theoretically, we show that 1) JEPA objective function maximize a variational lower bound on mu-
tual information between historical representations and future trajectories; and 2) predictive future-
conditioned policy learning admits a finite-sample performance bound with an O(N −1/4) estimation
term. Empirically, we evaluate DT-JEPA on the standard D4RL benchmark (Fu et al., 2020). Exper-
imental results demonstrate that DT-JEPA consistently achieves superior performance over existing
DT variants and competitive offline RL methods. In particular, DT-JEPA shows strong generaliza-
tion to the unseen tasks with unseen reward functions, achieving efficient online adaptation with
limited interactions.

2.2
JOINT-EMBEDDING PREDICTIVE ARCHITECTURE
The Joint Embedding Prediction Architecture (JEPA) is a self-supervised representation learning
framework based on latent-space prediction (LeCun et al., 2022; Assran et al., 2023). Its core idea
is not to directly reconstruct the original observed data, but rather to predict the latent-space embed-
dings of missing target data. Given an input sample, JEPA constructs a context view xC and a target
view xT , which are processed by a context encoder fθ and a target encoder fθ′, respectively. This
produces the latent representations zC = fθ(xC) and zT = fθ′(xT ). The parameters of the target
encoder fθ′ are updated using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the context encoder’s pa-
rameters, i.e., θ′ ←αθ′ + (1 −α)θ, where α ∈[0, 1) is the momentum coefficient (Grill et al.,
2020). Building on this, JEPA uses a predictor gϕ to predict the target latent representation based
on the context representation zC and target-related auxiliary information ξT , i.e., ˆzT = gϕ(zC, ξT ).
The model is trained by minimizing the distance between the predicted representation and the target
representation:
LJEPA = ∥gϕ (fθ(xC), ξT ) −fθ′(xT )∥2

that is relevant to and predictive of the target xT .

METHOD
In this section, we present DT-JEPA, an offline pretraining and online finetuning-based predictive
sequence-modeling framework for generalizable decision making. During the pretraining phase,
a variational JEPA model is designed to infer future representations from multi-task offline data
without reward supervision. Subsequently, the predicted future latent guides DT’s action genera-
tion by effectively modulating transformer’s hidden representations. During the finetuning phase,
task-specific critics is designed to efficiently adapt the pretrained policy toward unseen reward ob-
jectives with limited environment interaction. Theoretically, we analyze the effectiveness of the
predictive representation learning objective and derive a finite-sample performance bound for the
future-conditioned policy learning.

information I(Rt; τ≥t) between the conditional variable RTG and the future trajectory. To address
this issue, drawing on the concept of the JEPA world model, we seek to learn ˆFt as the future latent
representation predicted by JEPA based on historical trajectories, which is expected to preserve
richer predictive information about future trajectories beyond what can be represented by a scalar
RTG.

EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments systematically evaluate the proposed DT-JEPA framework centered on the follow-
ing three questions:
• RQ1: Can DT-JEPA learn transferable knowledge from reward-free datasets to support
downstream policy adaptation?
• RQ2: Can DT-JEPA effectively transfer the knowledge acquired during the pretraining
phase to unseen new tasks?
• RQ3: How do the latent future representation ˆFt learned by JEPA and online fine-tuning
mechanism contribute to the DT-JEPA’s performance?
4.1
BASELINES
We compare DT-JEPA with a variety of offline RL and DT-based methods. TD3-BC (Fujimoto &
Gu, 2021), a widely used offline RL method that combines value-based policy optimization with
behavior cloning regularization; TD-JEPA (Bagatella et al., 2026), which learns long-term latent-
predictive representations from reward-free offline transitions for zero-shot downstream RL; QT
(Hu et al., 2024), which augments Transformer-based conditional sequence modeling with Q-value
regularization; ODT (Zheng et al., 2022), an online DT that adapts the pretrained sequence policy
through limited environment interaction; Prompt DT (Xu et al., 2022), which conditions DT on
trajectory demonstrations to support few-shot policy generalization across tasks; VDT (Zheng et al.,
2025), which incorporates value guidance into DT to improve policy optimization; LSDT (Wang
et al., 2025), which jointly models long-term and local trajectory dependencies to enhance sequence-
based decision making; and PDT (Xie et al., 2023), a method that conditions policy learning on true
future trajectory information and subsequently adapts the pretrained policy to downstream tasks.

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduced DT-JEPA, a predictive representation learning framework that integrates
JEPA with DT for transferable decision making. DT-JEPA learns probabilistic future representations
from offline trajectories and incorporates them into action generation through AdaLN. We further
developed a critic-guided fine-tuning mechanism to adapt the pretrained policy to unseen reward
objectives with limited interaction. Theoretically, we analyze the predictive learning objective and
derive a finite-sample performance bound for future-conditioned policy learning. Experiments show
that DT-JEPA achieves strong performance and efficient adaptation, validating predictive future rep-
resentations as an effective condition information for generalizable sequence decision making.

A
RELATED WORK
A.1
DECISION TRANSFORMERS AND CONDITIONAL SEQUENCE MODELING
Reinforcement learning algorithms face the challenge of long-term credit assignment, which is typ-
ically addressed through temporal-difference (TD) learning (Kaelbling et al., 1996). Transform-
ers (Vaswani et al., 2017), originally developed for sequence modeling, provide a powerful mecha-
nism for capturing long-range dependencies across trajectories. Decision Transformer (DT) (Chen
et al., 2021) builds on this perspective and formulates reinforcement learning as a conditional se-
quence modeling problem, representing trajectories as sequences of returns, states, and actions and
predicting actions conditioned on desired returns. Prompt-DT (Xu et al., 2022) further introduces
trajectory demonstrations as prompts to encode task-specific information for few-shot policy gener-
alization. Generalized DT (GDT) (Furuta et al., 2022) interprets conditional policy learning through
hindsight information matching and extends the conditioning signal beyond returns to future states
and other trajectory statistics. Meta-DT (Wang et al., 2024) further applies conditional sequence
modeling to offline meta-RL by learning task representations through a disentangled world model
for few-shot and zero-shot adaptation. These studies suggest that the conditioning information is
central to the generalization ability of sequence-based policies.
A.2
JEPA-BASED REPRESENTATION LEARNING FOR RL
Future latent prediction has been widely used in reinforcement learning to learn decision-relevant
representations and improve sample efficiency without reconstructing raw observations (Schwarzer
et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2020). More recently, JEPA provide a general framework for predictive
representation learning by predicting the latent representation of a target from an observed context
rather than reconstructing the target in the original input space (LeCun et al., 2022). This work has
been later extended to video representation learning through V-JEPA, where models learn by predict-
ing latent representations of masked spatiotemporal regions (Bardes et al., 2024). In reinforcement
learning, several representative methods have explored latent prediction from different perspectives.
Recent methods such as TD-JEPA (Bagatella et al., 2026) further study reward-free representation
learning through policy-conditioned temporal prediction, emphasizing long-term dynamics that can
support zero-shot control. Kenneweg et al. (Kenneweg et al., 2025) further combine JEPA with PPO,
jointly optimizing action-conditioned latent prediction and actor–critic objectives for reinforcement
learning from visual observations.
A.3
REWARD-FREE PRETRAINING AND ZERO-SHOT RL
Reward-free RL focuses on policy learning from unlabeled datasets without relying on task-specific
reward supervision. Forward-Backward (FB) representations learn state-action occupancy repre-
sentations that support zero-shot policy construction for downstream rewards (Touati & Ollivier,
2021; Touati et al., 2023). HILP instead learns a temporally structured latent space for discovering
and composing goal-directed behaviors from reward-free data (Park et al., 2024). RLDP explores
reusable policy representations learned from offline trajectories to facilitate generalization to unseen
reward specifications (Jajoo et al., 2026). These approaches highlight different ways of extract-
ing task-agnostic structure from reward-free experience for downstream control. These methods
demonstrate that reward-free RL methods can acquire reusable behavioral knowledge without spe-
cific reward supervision.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
C.1
EXPERIMENTAL TASKS AND DATASETS
Environments.
We evaluate DT-JEPA on diverse D4RL benchmarks.
For MuJoCo locomo-
tion tasks, the experiments include four sequential control environments: Hopper, HalfCheetah,
Walker2d, and Ant. These environments cover different levels of dynamical complexity, ranging
from low-dimensional planar locomotion to high-dimensional three-dimensional quadrupedal con-
trol.
Figure 5: Illustration of four MuJoCo environments utilized in the paper.
• Hopper. Hopper is a planar locomotion environment in which a single-legged robot must
maintain balance while repeatedly hopping forward. The observation is 11-dimensional
and contains the configurations and velocities of the robot body and articulated joints. The
action space is 3-dimensional, with each action specifying the torque applied to one actu-
ated joint. Due to its small action space but sensitive balance dynamics, Hopper provides a
relatively compact benchmark for evaluating temporal decision making.
• HalfCheetah. HalfCheetah considers a planar articulated robot whose objective is to gen-
erate coordinated motions for fast forward locomotion. Its observation space contains 17
dimensions describing joint configurations and velocities, while the 6-dimensional contin-
uous action controls the torques applied to the actuated joints in the front and rear limbs.
Compared with Hopper, HalfCheetah requires coordination across a larger set of coupled
joints and admits a wider range of locomotion behaviors.
• Walker2d. Walker2d is a two-dimensional bipedal locomotion task in which the agent
controls two articulated legs to move forward while keeping the torso upright. The envi-
ronment has a 17-dimensional observation space and a 6-dimensional action space. The
actions correspond to torques applied to the thigh, leg, and foot joints of the two legs. Suc-
cessful control requires both forward progression and stable coordination between the two
legs, making the task particularly sensitive to errors in long-horizon action prediction.
• Ant. Ant is a three-dimensional quadrupedal locomotion environment with four articulated
legs and eight actuated joints. The agent must coordinate all four legs to produce stable
forward motion. In the D4RL MuJoCo setting, the observation is 111-dimensional, incor-
porating joint configurations, velocities, and contact-related information, while the action
space is 8-dimensional and specifies the torques applied to the eight controllable joints.
Its substantially higher state and action complexity makes Ant a challenging benchmark
for evaluating whether learned representations can capture long-horizon and multi-joint
dynamics.
For Maze2D navigation tasks, we consider 3 environments with increasing difficulty: Umaze,
Medium, and Large.
In Maze2D, the agent controls a point mass to navigate through a two-
dimensional maze toward a fixed goal. The environment has a 4-dimensional state space describing
the position and velocity of the agent, and a 2-dimensional continuous action space specifying its
acceleration. The reward is determined by the distance between the agent and the goal. The four
20


environments mainly differ in maze structure and navigation horizon, providing progressively more
challenging settings for evaluating long-horizon decision making.
• Umaze. Umaze introduces a compact U-shaped maze that requires the agent to navigate
around obstacles rather than moving directly toward the goal. Compared with Open, suc-
cessful control requires the policy to account for the maze structure and coordinate a se-
quence of actions over a longer navigation horizon.
• Medium. Medium contains a larger and more complex maze with longer feasible paths
between the initial position and the goal. The agent must make appropriate route choices
while maintaining stable motion through multiple corridors, increasing the demand for
long-term trajectory reasoning.
• Large. Large is the most challenging Maze2D configuration, featuring the largest maze
and the longest navigation horizon. Reaching the target requires the agent to coordinate
decisions over extended trajectories and avoid locally attractive but globally ineffective
movements, making it a challenging benchmark for long-horizon navigation.
For AntMaze navigation tasks, we consider three maze configurations: Umaze, Medium, and Large.
AntMaze extends Maze2D by replacing the point-mass agent with a quadrupedal Ant robot, resulting
in a more challenging control problem that combines locomotion and long-horizon navigation. The
environment has a 27-dimensional state space and an 8-dimensional continuous action space. The
three configurations mainly differ in maze size and navigation complexity, providing progressively
more challenging settings for evaluating long-horizon decision making.
Figure 6: Illustration of four AntMaze environments utilized in the paper.
• Umaze. Umaze is the smallest and simplest AntMaze configuration. The agent controls the
Ant robot to navigate through a compact U-shaped maze toward the target location. Due to
the relatively short navigation distance and simple maze structure, Umaze provides a basic
setting for evaluating whether the learned policy can combine low-level locomotion control
with goal-directed navigation.
• Medium. Medium uses a larger maze with longer navigation paths and more complex
route choices than Umaze. The agent must maintain stable quadrupedal locomotion while
navigating through multiple maze corridors toward the target. The increased spatial scale
requires the policy to capture longer-term dependencies between local movements and the
overall navigation objective.
• Large. Large is the most challenging of the three AntMaze configurations, featuring the
largest maze and the longest navigation horizon. Successful control requires the agent to
coordinate continuous locomotion over a long sequence of decisions while following an ap-
propriate route through the maze. This setting therefore provides a demanding benchmark
for evaluating long-horizon planning and the ability to exploit predictive representations
over extended trajectories.
Offline datasets.
For each locomotion domain, we consider three commonly used D4RL dataset
qualities: medium, medium-replay, and medium-expert. These datasets differ not only in behavioral
quality but also in the diversity and consistency of the trajectories they contain.
21


The medium dataset is collected using a partially trained policy with moderate task performance. It
therefore mainly contains coherent but suboptimal behaviors. The medium-replay dataset consists
of transitions stored in the replay buffer throughout the training process of the medium-level policy.
As a result, it contains a substantially broader mixture of behaviors, including early-stage, low-
quality, and partially improved trajectories. The medium-expert dataset combines trajectories from
medium and expert policies, producing a heterogeneous dataset that contains both suboptimal and
near-optimal behaviors.
For the AntMaze domain, we consider four commonly used D4RL dataset configurations: umaze,
umaze-diverse, medium-play, and medium-diverse. These datasets differ in both maze complexity
and the diversity of navigation behaviors contained in the offline data.
The umaze dataset uses the smallest maze layout with a fixed starting location and a fixed goal, and
therefore contains relatively consistent navigation behaviors. The umaze-diverse dataset uses the
same maze layout but introduces more diverse navigation trajectories through varying navigation
goals and starting conditions. For the larger Medium maze, medium-play is collected using a set
of hand-designed starting and goal locations, producing structured but heterogeneous navigation
behaviors. In contrast, medium-diverse uses more broadly distributed navigation goals, resulting
in a more diverse offline dataset with a wider range of trajectories. These configurations therefore
progressively increase the difficulty of learning reusable long-horizon navigation behaviors from
offline experience.
For the Maze2D domain, we evaluate on three D4RL environments: umaze, medium, and large.
Unlike AntMaze, these configurations mainly differ in maze scale and navigation complexity rather
than in the data-collection strategy. The umaze environment contains a compact U-shaped layout and
relatively short navigation paths. The medium environment introduces a larger maze with longer
routes and more complex spatial dependencies, while large further increases the maze size and
navigation horizon. The corresponding offline datasets contain continuous navigation experience
toward different goals, making the larger layouts increasingly challenging for learning long-horizon
dependencies and stitching useful behaviors across trajectories.
C.2
BASELINES
We provide a more detailed introduction to the baselines used in our experiments in this section.
• TD3-BC (Fujimoto & Gu, 2021) is a widely used offline reinforcement learning method
built upon Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3). It combines value-
based policy optimization with a behavior cloning objective that regularizes the learned
policy toward actions contained in the offline dataset. This simple modification reduces
the tendency of the policy to exploit out-of-distribution actions while still allowing policy
improvement through critic guidance. TD3-BC provides a representative non-sequence-
modeling offline RL baseline for evaluating whether trajectory-level sequence modeling
offers advantages over conventional actor–critic methods.
• TD-JEPA (Bagatella et al., 2026) is a reward-free zero-shot RL method based on temporal-
difference latent prediction.
It learns state and task encoders together with a policy-
conditioned multi-step predictor from offline reward-free transitions, encouraging the la-
tent representations to capture long-term policy-dependent dynamics. The learned predic-
tor approximates successor features in the latent space and is used to train a family of
latent-conditioned policies. At test time, downstream reward functions are mapped into the
learned task space to retrieve corresponding policies without further policy training. Since
TD-JEPA also exploits JEPA-style latent prediction for reward-free representation learning,
it provides a closely related baseline for evaluating the predictive representation learning
mechanism of DT-JEPA.
• ODT (Zheng et al., 2022) extends DT to the offline-to-online reinforcement learning set-
ting. It first learns a return-conditioned sequence policy from offline trajectories and sub-
sequently adapts the pretrained policy through limited online environment interactions. In
contrast to the deterministic action prediction commonly used in the original DT, ODT
employs a stochastic policy and incorporates an entropy-regularized objective to encour-
age exploration during online fine-tuning. We include ODT as a representative sequence-
22


modeling baseline for evaluating the effectiveness of directly adapting a pretrained DT with
online reinforcement learning.
• QT (Hu et al., 2024) is a Transformer-based offline RL method that combines conditional
sequence modeling with Q-value regularization. QT learns an action-value function using
an n-step Bellman objective and incorporates the learned Q-values into policy optimization,
while retaining the original DT objective as behavior regularization. This allows the policy
to favor high-value actions while remaining close to the behavior distribution, thereby im-
proving trajectory stitching from suboptimal offline data. During inference, QT generates
candidate actions under multiple return conditions and selects actions according to their
estimated Q-values. We include QT to compare DT-JEPA with a value-guided sequence-
modeling approach that explicitly improves policy optimization and trajectory stitching.
• Prompt DT (Xu et al., 2022) introduces trajectory prompts into DT to improve general-
ization across tasks. Instead of relying solely on return-to-go and historical state–action
sequences, Prompt DT conditions the policy on short trajectory demonstrations collected
from the target task. These demonstrations provide task-specific contextual information
that allows the Transformer to infer the desired behavior through in-context sequence mod-
eling. Prompt DT therefore serves as an important comparison for evaluating whether
explicit task demonstrations are necessary for cross-task adaptation.
• VDT (Zheng et al., 2025) augments the DT framework with value-based guidance. While
standard Decision Transformer mainly learns actions through supervised sequence predic-
tion conditioned on return-to-go, VDT additionally exploits value information to provide
a more informative signal for policy optimization. By incorporating value estimation into
sequence-based decision making, VDT aims to alleviate the limitations of purely behavior-
cloning-style training and improve the quality of generated actions. We use VDT to com-
pare DT-JEPA with a value-guided variant of D.
• LSDT (Wang et al., 2025) enhances DT by jointly modeling long-term and local depen-
dencies in offline trajectories. Rather than relying on a single temporal representation to
capture all historical information, LSDT explicitly considers complementary dependencies
at different temporal scales, enabling the policy to better exploit both long-horizon behav-
ioral structure and short-term transition information. This design is particularly relevant for
continuous-control problems where current actions may depend simultaneously on recent
dynamics and longer-term trajectory context. LSDT is included to assess whether explicitly
improving temporal dependency modeling is sufficient to achieve strong generalization.
• PDT (Xie et al., 2023) is a closely related reward-free sequence-model pretraining method
based on future conditioning. During pretraining, PDT encodes a succeeding trajectory
segment into a latent future representation and conditions the policy on this latent variable
for action prediction, allowing the model to learn diverse behaviors from reward-free and
potentially suboptimal trajectories. A future prior is additionally learned to generate latent
future representations from the current state. During downstream adaptation, PDT asso-
ciates future representations with returns through a return prediction model and uses this
information to select desirable futures for policy execution. Since PDT explicitly exploits
future trajectory information without requiring reward labels during pretraining, it provides
one of the most directly related baselines for evaluating DT-JEPA.

G.1
EFFECT OF PREDICTED FUTURE REPRESENTATIONS ON THE ACTION DISTRIBUTIONS.
To examine whether the JEPA-predicted future representations effectively participate in decision
making, we visualize the action distributions induced by different sampled future latents. We con-
duct this analysis on the Walker2d task using a DT-JEPA agent pretrained on the corresponding
offline dataset, and visualize the action distributions under different future representations sampled
from the probabilistic JEPA predictor. Given the same historical context, we sample three future rep-
resentations from the probabilistic JEPA predictor and use them to condition the DT policy through
AdaLN. As shown in Figure 7, different JEPA latents induce clearly distinguishable action distri-
butions across multiple action dimensions at both timesteps 1000 and 2000. Since the observable
history is kept unchanged, the resulting variation can be attributed to the predicted future represen-
tation, confirming that ˆFt is actively used by the policy rather than being ignored or collapsed into
an ineffective conditioning signal.
More importantly, the differences are structured across action dimensions rather than appearing as
uniform perturbations. This suggests that different samples from the predictive distribution encode
distinct possible future evolutions, which are translated by AdaLN into different control preferences.
In this sense, the probabilistic JEPA predictor does not merely provide an additional stochastic input,
but induces a family of future-conditioned action distributions under the same observed history.
These observations provide evidence that the predicted latent future serves as a meaningful decision
variable linking future-dynamics prediction to action generation in DT-JEPA.
G.2
EFFECT OF PREDICTED FUTURE REPRESENTATIONS ON THE ATTENTION SCORE.
To further understand how different future-conditioning strategies affect historical information uti-
lization, we visualize the attention scores of four variants in Figure 8. Here, DT adopts the con-
ventional RTG token as the conditioning signal; RTG-AdaLN retains RTG but injects it through
AdaLN; JEPA-Token replaces RTG with the predicted future representation ˆFt while preserving
token-based conditioning; and DT-JEPA combines predictive future representations with AdaLN
modulation.
A key observation is that the attention patterns become progressively more concentrated around the
main diagonal from DT to DT-JEPA. This phenomenon is particularly meaningful for HalfCheetah,
whose dynamics exhibit a strong local Markov structure: the state and action at the current step
are primarily determined by temporally nearby transitions, while distant historical tokens are gener-
ally less informative for immediate action prediction. Therefore, an attention pattern concentrated
around the diagonal indicates that the model assigns larger weights to temporally adjacent and dy-
namically relevant trajectory information rather than distributing attention broadly over the entire
history.
The original DT exhibits a relatively diffuse attention pattern, suggesting that the RTG-conditioned
Transformer does not sharply distinguish between locally relevant and less informative historical to-
kens. Introducing AdaLN with the same RTG condition already increases the locality of attention, as

G.3
HYPERPARAMETER SENSITIVITY
G.3.1
SENSITIVITY TO λJ DURING TRAINING
We further investigate the sensitivity of DT-JEPA to the training-stage weight λJ of the JEPA objec-
tive by varying it over {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}. For the MuJoCo locomotion tasks, all experiments
are conducted on the medium datasets. For navigation tasks, AntMaze is evaluated on umaze, while
Maze2D includes the umaze, medium, and large configurations.
As shown in Fig. 9, the preferred value of λJ varies across environments. Hopper performs best
around λJ = 0.1, whereas Walker2d, AntMaze, and Maze2D-Large favor a relatively stronger JEPA
objective around λJ = 1. In contrast, HalfCheetah and Maze2D-Umaze achieve better performance
with smaller values of λJ. Despite these differences, DT-JEPA remains relatively stable over a
moderate range of λJ, indicating that its performance does not depend on a narrowly tuned value.
The observed variation also reflects the different roles of predictive representation learning across
tasks. A small λJ causes training to be dominated by behavior cloning, limiting the amount of fu-
ture dynamics information preserved in the learned representation. Increasing λJ strengthens the
predictive objective and can be particularly beneficial in tasks requiring longer-term temporal rea-
soning, as suggested by the preference for larger values in AntMaze and Maze2D-Large. However,
an excessively large λJ shifts the optimization too far toward latent prediction and may reduce the
emphasis on accurate action generation. Simpler or more locally determined tasks therefore do not
necessarily benefit from stronger JEPA supervision.
G.3.2
SENSITIVITY TO λ′
J AND λQ DURING DEPLOYMENT
We further analyze the sensitivity of DT-JEPA to the two training-stage coefficients λJ and λQ
on four MuJoCo environments. As shown in Fig. 10, the preferred hyperparameter values vary
across environments, indicating that the balance between predictive representation learning and
value-guided policy optimization is task-dependent.
For λ′
J, moderate values generally provide better performance, while excessively small values
weaken the contribution of JEPA-based future representation learning and overly large values
may place too much emphasis on the auxiliary predictive objective. In particular, Hopper favors
λJ = 0.1, whereas Walker2d, HalfCheetah, and Ant perform well with λJ around 1 or 0.1.
For λQ, the main difference is reflected in the adaptation dynamics. A relatively large λQ can
accelerate early-stage adaptation in some environments, while a smaller value may provide more
stable improvements at later stages. For example, Walker2d exhibits a clear late-stage advantage
with λQ = 0.1, whereas Ant benefits more from the milder critic guidance induced by λQ = 0.01.

G.4
ANALYSIS ON TRAJECTORY STITCHING ABILITY
As shown in Table 4, removing the top high-return trajectories makes the offline datasets substan-
tially more fragmented and reduces the availability of complete high-quality behaviors. Under this
setting, DT-JEPA maintains strong performance across most locomotion and navigation tasks, indi-
cating that its policy does not rely solely on imitating complete successful trajectories. Instead, the
learned future representations appear to provide a useful predictive structure for connecting locally
effective behavior segments that may originate from different trajectories.
This advantage is particularly evident on the long-horizon navigation tasks. DT-JEPA performs con-
sistently well across the AntMaze variants and the more challenging Maze2D configurations, where
successful behavior often requires combining useful sub-trajectories over extended horizons. This
suggests that predicting future latent dynamics helps the policy reason beyond the immediate local
transition and identify behavior fragments that are compatible with a desirable future evolution. In
contrast, methods that mainly rely on sequence imitation or fixed future conditioning are generally
more affected when coherent high-return trajectories are removed, since the offline data provide
weaker direct supervision for complete decision sequences. QT remains highly competitive on sev-
eral datasets, which is consistent with the benefit of value-based trajectory stitching: its learned Q-
function can favor locally high-value actions even when they are observed in different trajectories.
DT-JEPA, however, shows a more consistent advantage across the degraded datasets, suggesting that
predictive future representations provide a complementary way to recover long-horizon behavioral
structure without explicitly relying on value-based action selection.

number of Transformer blocks LP ∈{1, 2, 3, 4} while keeping the JEPA encoder, policy backbone,
and all other training configurations fixed. As shown in Table 5, the predictor depth has a clear
influence on downstream performance. Increasing LP from one to two blocks improves the average
normalized return from 79.7 to 85.3, while using three blocks further increases it to 87.4. This
suggests that a very shallow predictor provides insufficient capacity for modeling the conditional
evolution of future latent representations. In contrast, increasing the depth from three to four blocks
does not yield further overall improvement, with the average return slightly decreasing from 87.4
to 86.9. Therefore, the benefit of increasing predictor capacity gradually saturates once a moderate
depth is reached.
The effect of predictor depth also varies noticeably across task families. For the locomotion bench-
marks, performance improves relatively smoothly from one to three blocks. Hopper and Ant con-
sistently favor a three-block predictor, whereas HalfCheetah reaches comparable or slightly better
performance with only two blocks. This behavior is consistent with the relatively smooth dynamics
of HalfCheetah, for which additional predictor capacity brings limited benefit. Walker2d exhibits
a slightly different pattern, where four blocks provide marginal improvements over three blocks.
However, the differences between LP = 3 and LP = 4 remain small, suggesting that the additional
capacity is not essential for these locomotion tasks.

SENSITIVITY TO THE SEQUENCE LENGTH
We further study the sensitivity of DT-JEPA to the sequence length K, which determines the tem-
poral range available for predictive representation learning and sequence-based policy modeling.
As shown in Table 6, increasing K from 5 to 20 consistently improves performance across almost
all environments. In particular, the average normalized return increases substantially as the con-
text grows from K = 5 to K = 10 and further to K = 20. This trend suggests that very short
sequences provide insufficient temporal information for DT-JEPA to infer reliable future dynamics
and establish useful dependencies between historical observations and subsequent behaviors.
The benefit of increasing K, however, gradually saturates once the context reaches a moderate
length. Among the twelve MuJoCo locomotion datasets, K = 20 achieves the best performance
on almost all tasks, while K = 30 provides only marginal additional benefit on Walker2d-medium-
expert. This indicates that locomotion control mainly requires a moderate temporal window: once
sufficient recent dynamics are captured, extending the sequence further contributes little additional
decision-relevant information. Conversely, the navigation environments exhibit a somewhat differ-
ent pattern. Most AntMaze tasks and the larger Maze2D configurations slightly favor K = 30,
suggesting that longer contexts can be beneficial when decisions depend on longer-term spatial and
temporal dependencies. This effect becomes more apparent as the navigation horizon and maze com-
plexity increase, whereas the simpler Maze2D-Umaze task already reaches its best performance at
K = 20. Importantly, further increasing the sequence length to K = 40 does not lead to additional

G.7
ADDITIONAL ABLATION RESULTS
Table 7 further provides a controlled comparison on the same unseen downstream tasks as Table 2,
aiming to determine whether the generalization advantage of DT-JEPA can be mainly attributed
to AdaLN-based conditioning or critic-guided adaptation. Compared with RTG-based condition-
ing, PDT already achieves substantially stronger performance, suggesting that incorporating future-
oriented information provides a more informative basis for adapting the policy to reward objectives
that are not observed during pretraining.
Applying AdaLN to PDT leads to a consistent but relatively modest improvement. This trend is con-
sistent with the main ablation results, where replacing conventional RTG conditioning with AdaLN
alone only provides limited gains. These observations indicate that AdaLN improves how the con-
ditioning information interacts with the policy, but does not fundamentally determine the quality of
the conditioning signal itself. In other words, a more flexible modulation mechanism is beneficial
only when the underlying representation contains sufficiently informative task-relevant structure.
A more pronounced improvement is observed when critic-guided adaptation is introduced to
PDT. This shows that downstream value information is important for aligning a pretrained future-
conditioned policy with an unseen reward objective. Combining critic guidance with AdaLN further
improves PDT across the four environments, indicating that the two mechanisms play complemen-
tary roles: AdaLN facilitates the utilization of conditioning information inside the policy, whereas
critic guidance provides reward-oriented supervision that steers the policy toward desirable behav-
iors during adaptation.
Nevertheless, a clear performance gap remains between PDT-AdaLN-CG and the full DT-JEPA
on all evaluated unseen tasks. More importantly, when considered together with Table 2, PDT-
AdaLN-CG still performs below DT-JEPA without critic guidance. This comparison is particularly
informative because the strengthened PDT baseline has already been equipped with both AdaLN and
the same critic-guided adaptation mechanism used by DT-JEPA. Therefore, the remaining advantage
cannot be explained solely by the modulation architecture or downstream critic supervision. Instead,
it points to the importance of the future conditioning representation itself.
The key distinction is that DT-JEPA does not rely only on a generic future embedding as the con-
ditioning variable. It explicitly predicts future latent representations from the observed trajectory
history and models their conditional distribution through JEPA learning. As a result, the condition-
ing signal is directly grounded in the current behavioral context and captures predictive information
about how the trajectory may evolve. Such history-dependent future information can provide a more

why DT-JEPA retains an advantage even after the alternative baseline is equipped with comparable
