Abstract 

Humanoid whole-body controllers can now track a diverse set of dynamic motions, but they are typically trained offline and then frozen, so teaching such a controller a new skill tends to erode the skills it already mastered. We study continual learning for humanoid whole-body motion , where a single controller must acquire skills from a sequential task stream without revisiting past data. We introduce Similarity-guided LoRA-PNN , a progressive neural net-work (PNN) policy that prevents catastrophic forgetting by construction while reusing knowledge across skills through lightweight low-rank adaptation. A two-level motion-similarity measure, built from dynamic time warping aggregated by optimal transport, decides which prior skill to build on and how much new capacity to allocate, yielding strong forward transfer and large efficiency gains. Across six sequentially learned skill categories, our similarity-guided LoRA pol-icy attains the best forward transfer ( 0.125 vs. 0.079 ) and the highest average accuracy among all methods, while saving up to 94 .5% of trainable parameters and 40 .8% of training time. The resulting controller reaches 96 .13% sim-to-sim transfer and is deployed on a physical Unitree G1. Our code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/continual-humanoid-learning-35D3. 

Keywords: Continual learning, Humanoid robots, Whole-body motion control, Low-rank adaptation, Sim-to-real transfer

# 1 Introduction 

Whole-body control underpins humanoid behavior: robust locomotion requires coordi-nating the legs, torso, and arms to manage balance and momentum while supporting task-relevant motion. Driven by learning-based motion tracking with sim-to-real pipelines that exploit large-scale human motion, a single policy can track or generalize across a broad repertoire of agile whole-body skills [1, 4, 6, 13, 19]. Yet such poli-cies are trained offline and deployed as essentially static controllers, making it hard to incrementally acquire new motions. The obvious workaround—fine-tuning on the new motion set—triggers catastrophic forgetting, often causing severe regressions on previously mastered behaviors [31, 35]. This contrasts sharply with human learning, where new skills are added while old ones are retained. Continual learning (CL) explic-itly targets this stability–plasticity dilemma and is well studied in computer vision and natural language processing [25], but in robotics it remains under-explored and is demonstrated mostly in manipulation and navigation rather than legged whole-body control [10, 17, 30]. To our knowledge, continual learning for humanoid whole-body motion has not been systematically studied. Such continual acquisition can also extend generalist controllers [14], offering a practical route toward an ever-growing generalist. We therefore target this gap: enabling one policy to sequentially acquire whole-body skills (Fig. 1) with strong transfer at low cost. Building on Progressive Neural Networks (PNN) [21], we instantiate a frozen sub-network per task, which isolates prior competencies and makes zero forgetting a structural guarantee rather than something we must optimize for. The challenge, and our focus, is to make this expansion reuse knowledge across skills while staying parameter- and computation-efficient. We grow each new sub-network with parameter-efficient LoRA adapters [8] instead of training it from scratch, and we guide expansion with a two-level motion-similarity measure that selects the most related prior skill to inherit from and sets each adapter’s capacity from the expected transfer. Across a continual suite of six skill categories, this yields the best forward transfer among strong baselines, while cutting trainable parameters and training time substantially, with zero forgetting by design.

# 2 Related Work 

Humanoid whole-body locomotion. Learning-based control has rapidly expanded the repertoire of humanoid whole-body skills, from real-world RL locomotion driven by proprioceptive history [19] and expressive upper-/lower-body coordina-tion [1] to unified controllers that expose fine-grained command spaces [34]. Aparticularly active direction is whole-body motion tracking , where a single policy imi-tates large libraries of reference motions [4, 6] and, more recently, scales to generalist controllers that track thousands of clips [14]. Despite this progress, such controllers are typically trained offline and then deployed as fixed models: incorporating a new skill requires retraining or fine-tuning, which degrades previously mastered behaviors—in stark contrast to how humans accumulate skills over time. 

Continual learning. Continual (or lifelong) learning studies how an agent can incrementally acquire tasks from a non-stationary stream while balancing the stability–plasticity trade-off and mitigating catastrophic forgetting [31]. Classical strategies fall into three families: regularizing important weights ( e.g ., EWC [9]), parameter isolation via pruning or masking ( e.g ., PackNet [16]), and dynamic archi-tecture growth such as Progressive Neural Networks [21]; more recent work adapts large foundation models with lightweight low-rank modules to limit interference [11]. In robotics, continual learning has been explored mainly for manipulation and mobile platforms— e.g ., continually improving mobile manipulation [17], growing skill libraries through unsupervised discovery [30], online neurogenesis for lifelong legged control [27], self-evolving VLA skill spaces with expert routing [32], and language-augmented skill composition [18]. To the best of our knowledge, continual learning has not been inves-tigated for humanoid whole-body motion. We address this gap with Similarity-guided LoRA-PNN , a progressive policy that continually acquires humanoid whole-body skills with zero forgetting and strong, low-cost transfer. 33 Method 

## 3.1 Problem Setup 

We study continual learning for humanoid whole-body motion, where a single con-troller must incrementally acquire a growing set of whole-body skills ( e.g ., walking, jumping, and dancing) from a sequential curriculum in which skills are introduced one after another. Let πθ map humanoid observations (proprioception, root state, contact cues, and optional task commands) to actions, and let {D 1, . . . , DK } be the ordered task stream, where Dk holds the demonstrations for task k. A continual-learning algorithm ACL updates the network online across skills via 

θk = ACL (θk−1, Dk) , (1) seeking plasticity on the newly introduced behavior while preserving stability on previ-ously learned ones. Our approach removes the stability bottleneck altogether, so that mastering a new skill never degrades those already learned. This shifts the central question from whether the controller forgets to how quickly it transfers to new skills and how efficiently it scales as its repertoire grows.

## 3.4 Motion Similarity 

Parent selection and rank allocation both rely on a distance between two sets of motions, Dl = {An}Nn=1 and Dk = {Bm}Mm=1 , where each motion is a variable-length sequence (typically 3–5 minutes after retargeting) that may contain transitions (pauses, start/stop, activity changes). The distance must be robust to (i) temporal misalignment and (ii) unequal sequence lengths, and it must not be driven by how many motions each set happens to contain. We therefore use a two-level comparison :motions are first decomposed into short windows and compared as distributions of local behaviors, and sets are then compared as distributions of motions. Similarity is computed hierarchically with dynamic time warping (DTW) [24] inside windows and optimal transport (OT) [28] across windows and across motions.

## 3.5 Policy Training Objective 

We train each column as a distinct actor with an on-policy objective that imitates the reference motion: at stage k only column k is optimized while all earlier columns are frozen, no past-task replay is used, and all actors share a single critic. The per-step reward is a weighted sum of tracking and regularization terms:

Following the adaptive scheme of [33], each tracking term is an exponential of a normalized error, ri =exp( −∥ ei∥/σ i), covering root-velocity, anchor (key-body) position/orientation, and key-body linear/angular-velocity tracking, with the same terms and weights shared across all tasks. Regularizers [1] penalize action rate, joint velocity/acceleration, foot slip, and limit/collision violations, and episodes terminate when the reference motion ends or safety constraints are violated. 

Teacher–student distillation. To obtain a deployable policy, we distill a privi-leged teacher into a student that uses only deployable observations. The teacher is 9trained in Isaac Gym [15] with privileged inputs (base velocity, key-body poses, con-tact states, and domain-randomization parameters) unavailable in MuJoCo [29] and on hardware. The student is trained with DAgger [20], predicting actions from deploy-able observations augmented with a history encoder while minimizing ∥atea − astu ∥2.Both policies use a shared encoder over the deployable proprioceptive history; this encoder is trained jointly with the teacher and then kept frozen during student distilla-tion, so distillation only updates the student’s action prediction network. ## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Task suite and continual protocol. We study continual humanoid whole-body motion learning under a task-incremental protocol with a provided task identifier. Let 

K = {1, . . . , K } be the task set, trained in order 1 → 2 → · · · → K. After learning task k, we evaluate the policy πk on all tasks i ≤ k, forming a stage-by-task matrix 

A ∈ RK×K with Ak,i = Perf( πk, i ). Our suite contains K = 6 tasks curated from 

LAFAN1 [5] and the Kungfu motion datasets [4, 33] to span a range of difficulty, from easiest to hardest: walk , run , jump , dance , fight , and fall and up . All methods share the same per-task optimization budget (50K iterations, 1024 parallel environ-ments) and evaluation budget (100 episodes per task). Key implementation settings are summarized in Table 3; the complete PPO, network, domain-randomization, and reward settings are listed in Appendix B.

Baselines. Since no continual-learning baseline exists for humanoid whole-body control, we (i) adapt three strong multi-motion humanoid imitation methods—

## 4.4 Efficiency 

Beyond accuracy, similarity-guided LoRA makes progressive expansion efficient (Table 8). Replacing full fine-tuning with LoRA cuts trainable parameters by up to 94 .5% (2 .4M → 132K) and training time by 40 .8% (2 .94 → 1.74 h per 5k iterations), while even the best-accuracy linear rank variant still saves 90 .9% of trainable param-eters. The four variants thus trace an accuracy–efficiency trade-off in which the cost of a new skill scales with its novelty rather than a fixed per-column budget: a skill close to an already-learned one is acquired with a small low-rank update, whereas a dissimilar skill receives a larger rank, so new parameters are spent where transfer is weakest. These savings also bring scalability: although PNN’s lateral connections grow as O(K2) in the number of tasks, similarity-guided inheritance, LoRA, and parallel training of disjoint columns keep the per-task cost low and make substantially longer task streams practical. 16 Dance Fall & Up Fight Run Fig. 6 Qualitative sim-to-sim comparison of the single distilled student on dance , fall and up ,

## 4.5 Real-World Deployment 

We validate deployment through a two-stage transfer pipeline: sim-to-sim from Isaac Gym to MuJoCo with the distilled student, then the same student on a physical Unitree G1 (23 actuated DoF; wrists locked for safety). The student attains a 96 .13% sim-to-sim success rate (50 episodes per motion, 1500 trials) with only modest tracking degradation (Table 10); Fig. 6 shows the same motions executed consistently in both simulators. 

Continual distillation. Distilling a continual teacher into a continual student pre-serves transfer: it reaches 96 .13% sim-to-sim success, versus 94 .07% when task-specific teachers are merged into one student (Table 11), confirming that the progressive policy distills into a single compact controller without losing fidelity. 

Real-robot results. We deploy the single continually trained student on the Uni-tree G1 across 30 motions spanning all six skill categories, attaining 90 .33% success over 10 episodes per motion under the same criterion. Fig. 7 shows representative frames of the deployed controller executing walking, running, dancing, and fight-ing motions on the physical robot; the full executions are in the supplementary video (Supplementary Video 1). To our knowledge this is among the first continually learned humanoid whole-body controllers deployed on hardware across its sequentially acquired skill set.
