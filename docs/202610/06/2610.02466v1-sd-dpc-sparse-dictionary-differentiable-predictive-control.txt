# SD-DPC: Sparse Dictionary Differentiable Predictive Control 

Ali Reza Daneshvar Garmroodi 1 and Jan Drgoˇ na 2

Abstract — We present sparse dictionary differentiable pre-dictive control (SD-DPC), a framework for learning sparse, interpretable feedback policies for nonlinear systems from data. A prediction model is first identified by rollout-based sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy), building on gradient-based and multistep formulations. The policy is then parameterized as a sparse combination of dictionary functions and trained by differentiating a constrained finite-horizon predictive-control objective through this model, so that its terms are selected by closed-loop performance rather than by imitating a previously trained controller. The result is an explicit feedback law with only a handful of terms. Across three benchmark control problems, SD-DPC satisfies the constraints in all test scenarios, outperforms a policy distilled onto the same terms by up to an order of magnitude, and requires orders of magnitude less memory and online computation than an optimization benchmark, while admitting explicit sensitivity bounds. 

I. INTRODUCTION Model predictive control (MPC) optimizes closed-loop performance under state and input constraints, but requires solving a constrained optimization problem at every sampling instant [1]–[3], which can be prohibitive for fast nonlinear systems or restricted hardware. Explicit MPC moves the op-timization offline, although the number of regions describing the solution can grow rapidly with the problem dimension [4]. Learned policies scale better, either by approximating a precomputed MPC law [5]–[7] or by directly minimizing a predictive-control objective through differentiable closed-loop rollouts [8], [9]. The latter approach, differentiable predictive control (DPC), yields an explicit policy that is in-expensive to evaluate online. However, these learned policies are commonly deep neural networks, which distribute the control law across many coupled parameters and are difficult to interpret, verify, modify, and adapt after deployment [10]. Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) rep-resents system dynamics as a sparse combination of candi-date functions [11]. Extensions to controlled systems enable compact models for model-based control [12], [13], while ensemble, weak-form, gradient-based, and trajectory-based formulations improve robustness to noise and avoid point-wise derivative estimates [14]–[18]. The same dictionary structure can also represent a feedback law, which is the idea pursued here.

Sparse and symbolic representations have also been used for interpretable control policies. One line of work opti-mizes the policy structure through model-free reinforcement learning [19]–[21], where constraints enter only through the reward. Another trains a neural policy first and then fits a sparse policy to its actions [22], so the result is selected for agreement with the teacher rather than for closed-loop performance. Sparse polynomial feedback laws have also been learned by minimizing an averaged closed-loop cost with an ℓ1 penalty [23], but for known control-affine dy-namics with a constant input matrix and without state or input constraints. To our knowledge, the terms of an explicit sparse policy have not been selected under a constrained finite-horizon predictive-control objective evaluated through a model identified from data. In this work, we train a sparse dictionary policy directly through DPC. The prediction model and the feedback law are both sparse combinations of candidate functions, learned by the same sequentially thresholded gradient procedure, so the policy terms are selected by the constrained closed-loop objective itself rather than by an intermediate imitation step. The contributions of this paper are as follows.  

> •

Closed-loop sparse policy learning framework: SD-DPC represents both the prediction model and the feedback policy as sparse dictionary expansions and, to our knowledge, is the first to select the policy terms under a constrained predictive-control objective through a model identified from data [16]–[18]. arXiv:2610.02466v1 [eess.SY] 1 Oct 2026 • Structural guarantees: we show finite termination of the thresholded training with a bound on the cost of each prune, and derive a Lipschitz bound of the policy from its retained coefficients that bounds the closed-loop deviation caused by pruning or model mismatch.  

> •

Closed-loop validation: on three benchmarks evaluated on the true plants, SD-DPC satisfies all constraints with orders of magnitude less memory and evaluation time than online optimization, whereas distilling a deep neural policy onto the same terms increases the tracking error by up to an order of magnitude and violates the obstacle constraint. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews SINDy and DPC, Section III develops SD-DPC, its training algorithm, and its structural properties, Section IV details the case studies, Section V presents the results, and Section VI concludes. IV. N UMERICAL EXAMPLES 

Three numerical case studies demonstrate the flexibility of SD-DPC: constrained reference tracking of a nonlinear system, stabilization of a nonlinear oscillator at its unstable origin, and an obstacle-avoidance reaching task. All exam-ples are implemented in NeuroMANCER v1.5.3 [27], and all computations are performed on a MacBook with an Apple M1 processor and 8 GB of RAM. The code is available at 

https://github.com/SOLARIS-JHU/SD-DPC .In each case study, SD-DPC is compared against three baselines. The first is an optimization-based benchmark solved with IPOPT [28] through CasADi [29], which min-imizes the same stage and terminal costs as the policy objective, unless stated otherwise in the case study, and uses the true dynamics, and therefore has exact model knowledge. For the two-tank system, it is a receding-horizon MPC whose horizon starts at 50 steps, shrinks toward one step as the end of each reference segment approaches, and is reset when the reference changes. For the Van der Pol oscillator and the 2-D integrator, the full N -step problem is solved once per scenario and the optimal input sequence is applied in open loop. Because this benchmark is computed for each scenario separately, whereas a learned policy is a single feedback law shared by all scenarios, it serves as a performance reference rather than as a competitor. NN-DPC is a deep neural policy trained by (3) on the same identified model and represents the standard DPC parameterization; in all cases, it is a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 32 

units and ReLU activations. Finally, NN-Distilled follows the distillation approach of SINDy-RL [22]: a sparse dictionary policy is regressed onto the state–action pairs generated by the trained NN-DPC policy. Its terms are fixed to those retained by SD-DPC, so the two differ only in how the coefficients on this common support are obtained, either by regression onto the neural policy’s actions or under the closed-loop objective (9), which isolates the effect of the training objective. All policies are trained on rollouts of the identified model 

FΞ, whereas all controllers are evaluated on the true plants (13), (15), and (17), so that the reported results include the effect of model mismatch. Since state constraints, including the obstacle, are only penalized, their satisfaction is assessed on the evaluation scenarios of Section V.

A. Two-Tank System 

For the two-tank system, SD-DPC retains six terms across the two input channels (Fig. 3(a)), tracks each reference segment, and satisfies the level constraints on every scenario. Its tracking loss is about twelve times lower than that of NN-DPC, a gap well beyond the scenario-to-scenario spread of either policy, so the sparse parameterization costs nothing in accuracy here; both learned policies remain well above the IPOPT benchmark, which optimizes each scenario online. The distilled policy, which uses the same six terms, also satisfies the constraints, but its coefficients, fitted to the actions of NN-DPC, leave a steady offset, and its tracking loss is about seventeen times that of SD-DPC. B. Forced Van der Pol Oscillator 

In the Van der Pol case, all controllers stabilize the origin and satisfy the constraints on every scenario. The benchmark optimizes each scenario individually and therefore reaches a loss below 10 −5, whereas both learned policies settle more slowly, with a final-10-step loss of about 4–5 × 10 −2. The loss of SD-DPC is comparable to that of NN-DPC, whereas the distilled policy on the same terms reaches about twice that of SD-DPC. What the sparse parameterization adds here is structure. C. Two-Dimensional Integrator with Obstacle Avoidance 

This case study shows why the policy must be trained in closed loop, with the constraints enforced during optimiza-tion. SD-DPC retains twelve terms (Fig. 3(c)), steers around the obstacle, and reaches the setpoint from every sampled initial condition, with cumulative control effort within 0.6% 

of the minimum attained by IPOPT. SD-DPC and NN-DPC are both trained through closed-loop rollouts that include the obstacle penalty, and both satisfy the constraint in every scenario. The terminal error of SD-DPC is higher than that of NN-DPC, the price of a twelve-term parameterization, remains well within the reaching task. The distilled policy violates the obstacle constraint in 60 .8% of the scenarios, with a maximum violation depth of 1.61 , and its terminal error is about thirteen times that of SD-DPC. This work presented SD-DPC, a framework in which both the prediction model and the feedback policy are sparse combinations of candidate functions, learned by the same thresholded gradient-descent procedure, so that the policy terms are chosen by the constrained closed-loop objective rather than by imitating a pretrained controller. Across three case studies, the learned SD-DPC policies retained only a few terms, satisfied the constraints in all test scenarios, and reduced the task loss of a policy distilled onto the same terms by up to an order of magnitude, whereas the distilled policy violated the obstacle constraint. The closed-form policies require about two orders of magnitude less memory than deep neural policies and far less memory and evaluation time than the optimization-based benchmark. The sparse policies also admit explicit sensitivity bounds and, in specific cases, direct stability analysis, as shown for the Van der Pol oscillator. Constraint satisfaction is currently promoted through penalty terms and verified empirically. Future work will focus on exploiting the symbolic structure of SD-DPC for stronger closed-loop guarantees. This includes hard fea-sibility through safety filters, systematic region-of-attraction certification, robustness bounds under identification error, and sufficient conditions under which sparsification preserves stability and feasibility.
