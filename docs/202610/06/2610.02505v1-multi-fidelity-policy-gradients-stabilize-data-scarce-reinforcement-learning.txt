## ABSTRACT 

Policy gradient methods for on-policy reinforcement learning (RL) can become unstable when expensive, scarce target-domain data yield noisy gradient esti-mates. We address this challenge by complementing limited high-fidelity (HF) 

target-domain data with abundant, cheap, but biased low-fidelity (LF) data, e.g., from a simplified simulator. Most existing methods directly optimize biased ob-jectives based on LF data. In contrast, the recently introduced multi-fidelity policy gradient (MFPG) framework uses LF data solely to construct a control variate that reduces variance and improves HF data efficiency without biasing the policy gra-dient estimator. However, published work on MFPG is limited to REINFORCE on small-scale simulation tasks. We develop MFPG for modern actor-critic learning in GPU-parallel simulation and on a physical robot. Our analysis and experiments show that naive extensions to proximal policy optimization (PPO) can lose cross-fidelity correlation or inflate variance. Our MFPG-PPO addresses these failures by redesigning the sampling, advantage estimation, and control variate construction to preserve cross-fidelity correlation, and by monitoring estimator uncertainty to prevent variance inflation. We also introduce a budget-aware MFPG-PPO to di-vide a fixed sampling budget among high- and low-fidelity data sources. Across simulated robot locomotion tasks of varying LF-to-HF transfer difficulty and HF data budgets, MFPG-PPO improves upon PPO trained on HF data alone in nearly all settings, and consistently matches the performance of PPO trained with 16 ×

more HF data on the hardest task at the smallest HF budgets. In contrast, most baselines that use LF data perform well only where direct LF-to-HF transfer suc-ceeds. MFPG-PPO enables stable learning on a physical Franka arm using only 4 real-robot episodes per update and no human demonstrations. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

On-policy reinforcement learning (RL) with policy gradient methods underpins advances in areas ranging from language-model post-training (Ouyang et al., 2022) to fine-tuning vision-language-action models (Lu et al., 2025) for robot control. Yet in many real-world settings, target-domain data are expensive to collect and therefore scarce, e.g., a physical robot may collect only a handful of rollouts per policy update. In such data-scarce regimes, standard on-policy methods can break down because their policy gradient estimates become too noisy to support stable learning. GPU-simulation RL pipelines (Rudin et al., 2022). The key is access to multi-fidelity data: we as-sume that the target environment (a physical system or high-accuracy simulator) provides limited, expensive high-fidelity (HF) samples, whereas a simplified simulator provides abundant, inexpen-sive, but biased low-fidelity (LF) samples. Exploiting LF data requires care, however, and can easily bias policy updates and degrade performance in the target HF environment. Existing approaches use LF data in different ways. Off-dynamics RL methods (Eysenbach et al., 2021; Lyu et al., 2024) learn to correct LF rewards for the dynamics mismatch, and sim-and-real co-training (Wei et al., 2025) mixes LF and HF data. However, reliability is a major challenge. Most of these methods still directly optimize biased objectives based on LF data, so their performance in the target HF environment is largely tied to how well policies learned from LF data transfer. The recent multi-fidelity policy gradient (MFPG) framework (Liu et al., 2026) offers a principled alternative. Here, LF samples are used to construct a control variate (Nelson, 1987)—a correlated auxiliary random variable with a known mean—for the HF policy gradient estimator. The MFPG es-timator remains unbiased with respect to the HF objective, and the policy gradient surrogate variance shrinks as the cross-fidelity data correlation grows. MFPG is therefore robust to LF data biases. However, the MFPG construction of Liu et al. (2026) is limited to the classic REINFORCE algo-rithm (Williams, 1992) and small-scale, single-environment simulation tasks. How MFPG interacts with modern actor-critic learning and GPU-parallel simulation (Mittal et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024), and how it translates to real-robot learning, remain unresolved. An earlier preprint (Liu et al., 2025) directly substituted proximal policy optimization (PPO) for REINFORCE. The resulting method did not consistently improve over the REINFORCE instantiation and sometimes even underperformed single-fidelity PPO. Hence, an effective extension requires more than algorithm substitution. We address this gap with two contributions: (i) MFPG-PPO, a multi-fidelity PPO algorithm. 

Through analysis and experiments, we show how naive PPO instantiations of MFPG can lose cross-fidelity correlation or inflate variance by orders of magnitude. We address these failures by redesign-ing the sampling, advantage estimation, and control variate construction to preserve cross-fidelity correlation, and by monitoring estimator uncertainty to prevent variance inflation. Preserving cor-relation comes at the price of potential bias, a tradeoff we characterize analytically. (ii) A budget-aware MFPG-PPO. When HF and LF samples incur different costs, we allocate a fixed per-iteration sampling budget across fidelities, guided by the variance-minimizing allocation framework of Pe-herstorfer et al. (2016). We test MFPG-PPO in GPU-parallel simulation (Mittal et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024) and on a physical robot arm. Across simulation tasks and HF data budgets, we compare with 5 baselines that use LF data in different ways and with PPO and soft actor-critic (SAC) (Haarnoja et al., 2018) trained on HF data alone. MFPG-PPO is the only method in our comparison that improves over PPO trained on HF data alone in nearly all settings, is never distinguishably worse, and scales monotonically with 2Preprint more HF data. Most baselines that use LF data perform well only where direct LF-to-HF transfer succeeds. On the hardest task at the smallest HF budgets, MFPG-PPO consistently matches the mean return of PPO trained with 16 × more HF data, cf. Fig. 1. Finally, MFPG-PPO enables stable RL on a Franka arm with only 4 episodes per policy update and no human demonstrations. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

LF simulation can support policy learning for an HF target domain through system identifica-tion (Ljung, 1998; Ramos et al., 2019), zero-shot transfer with domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2018), adapting simulation-trained priors with scarce HF data (Smith et al., 2022; Levy et al., 2026), and combining multi-fidelity data within policy updates. We focus on the last category. A complementary line of multi-fidelity RL research (Cutler et al., 2015; Marco et al., 2017; Ryou et al., 2026; Bhola et al., 2023; Sifaou & Simeone, 2025) studies which fidelity to query at each training stage rather than how to combine fidelities within a policy update. Three mechanisms for combining multi-fidelity data are particularly relevant. Pooling multi-fidelity data is a simple and effective recipe for sim-and-real co-training in imitation learning (Maddukuri et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2026) and in RL (Di-Castro et al., 2021). Off-dynamics RL 

corrects for estimated dynamics mismatch in LF data, e.g., through reward penalties from domain classifiers (Eysenbach et al., 2021) or representation mismatch (Lyu et al., 2024), filtering by value discrepancy (Xu et al., 2023), support extension (Le Pham Van et al., 2024), or imitation-based transfer (Guo et al., 2024), with extensions to offline and hybrid offline-online settings (Liu et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2022; 2025; Lyu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026). Pooling and correction optimize biased objectives based on LF data, whereas multi-fidelity control variates use LF data to reduce variance in HF estimates. Control variates are a classical tool in single-fidelity RL (Greensmith et al., 2004; Gu et al., 2017; Cheng et al., 2020). Recently, Khairy & Balaprakash (2024) develop multi-fidelity control variates for state-action value estimation in tabular Markov decision processes (MDPs), while Liu et al. (2026) propose a policy gradient framework based on REINFORCE with function approximation. Building on these advances, we develop multi-fidelity control variates for actor-critic learning in GPU-parallel simulation and on a real robot.

## 4 APPROACH 

We develop MFPG-PPO by addressing two challenges that arise when extending the control variate construction of Liu et al. (2026) to PPO, then introduce a budget-aware MFPG-PPO. 

Challenge 1: What to pair as (Xhigh , X low ), and how to keep them correlated. Liu et al. (2026) pair the complete-episode REINFORCE statistic Xτ , which is unbiased but has two limitations. First, PPO collects short rollouts from desynchronized environments, which need not contain any complete episode. Second, since the multi-fidelity environments have different dynamics, coupled trajectories can diverge over long episodes despite shared initial states and action noise, weakening or destabilizing their correlation ρ on which the variance reduction (1 − ρ2) relies. One trajectory may even terminate while the other continues. 

Challenge 2: How much to trust the estimates. The correlation ρ and the coefficient c∗ are un-known and estimated from scarce HF and coupled LF data. However, a poorly estimated coefficient can make the estimator noisier than using HF data alone. Moreover, PPO reuses each batch across policy updates, so coefficient estimates must account for changes in the surrogate statistics. The control variate should therefore be applied cautiously only when the evidence supports its use. 4.1 MFPG-PPO

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Evaluation criteria. A practitioner rarely knows in advance how informative the LF data will be. We therefore evaluate two criteria. (i) Monotone scaling , i.e., an approach’s performance should improve monotonically as more HF data become available, until it plateaus. (ii) Reliability , i.e., compared to training with limited HF data alone, an approach should perform better when the LF data are informative and no worse when they are misleading. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Simulation setup and HF data budgets. We evaluate locomotion on rough and flat terrain with the Unitree H1 humanoid and the ANYmal-D quadruped in Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025). We sweep a sample-scarcity axis by varying the number of parallel HF environments N high from 8 to 512, up to 512 × fewer than the Isaac Lab default of 4096, while fixing N low = 4096 uncoupled LF environments. The LF simulation simplifies the robot collision model, coarsens the physics time step, reduces the number of physics solver iterations, and disables self-collisions. Since exploration from a random initialization is difficult with scarce HF data, all on-policy methods that use HF data begin from a shared policy briefly pre-trained with PPO in the LF environment. SAC starts from its own LF-pretrained policy with matched LF training return. Unless otherwise stated, we evaluate each final policy deterministically over 2048 HF episodes and report mean returns with 

95% bootstrap confidence intervals across 6 seeds. Appendix A.6 reports details. 

Real-robot setup. We evaluate cube picking with a physical Franka arm and ManiSkill simula-tion (Tao et al., 2024) as the HF and LF environments, respectively. Figure 5(b) shows that all variants without monitoring amplify variance in most iterations (variance ratio above 1), with ratios sometimes exceeding 1000 . For the full MFPG-PPO, the ratio exceeds 1 in only a small fraction of iterations. Minibatches failing the variance reduction check fall back to HF-only PPO updates. ## 6 CONCLUSIONS AND DISCUSSION 

We introduced MFPG-PPO and its budget-aware variant to stabilize data-scarce on-policy RL. Through designs that preserve cross-fidelity correlation and monitor estimator uncertainty, MFPG-9Preprint PPO enables stable learning with per-update target-domain data batches as small as 4 real-robot episodes or up to 512 × smaller than in common GPU-simulation RL pipelines (Rudin et al., 2022). Unlike methods that directly optimize biased objectives based on LF data, MFPG-PPO uses LF data to construct a variance-reducing control variate. In our comparison across tasks and HF budgets, MFPG-PPO is the only method that improves upon PPO trained on HF data alone in nearly all settings, is never distinguishably worse, and scales monotonically with more HF data. Three directions call for future research. First, re-synchronizing coupled LF rollouts to recorded HF states while uncoupled LF rollouts evolve freely can introduce bias in Eq. (1) if the two LF statistics differ in mean. Re-synchronizing both LF batches would remove this bias but leave the variance associated with the re-synchronization states irreducible , cf. Lemma 2 in Appendix A.2. Our con-struction keeps the full variance reducible, but doing so without bias remains an open challenge. Second, raising cross-fidelity correlation, e.g., through representation alignment (Lei et al., 2026), could strengthen variance reduction. Third, real-to-sim tools that turn scene scans into interactive simulators (Jain et al., 2025; Team, 2026) could ease the construction of coupled LF simulators. AI USE STATEMENT

A.2 WHY ARE THE UNCOUPLED LF R OLLOUTS NOT RE-SYNCHRONIZED ?The coupled LF rollouts are re-synchronized to recorded HF states to preserve correlation (Sec-tion 4.1), whereas the uncoupled LF rollouts (which supply the LF baseline ˆμlow in Eq. Re-synchronizing the uncoupled LF rollouts to the same states would remove the bias noted in Section 4.1, since the coupled and uncoupled LF rollouts would then follow the same distribu-tion. Two-process implementation. To support different simulator fidelities or a physical robot as the HF environment, we implement MFPG-PPO with two processes. An HF worker collects HF rollouts, and a learner collects coupled and uncoupled LF rollouts and optimizes the policy and critics. The learner sends the policy parameters and action noise to the worker, then uses the returned HF states to initialize coupled LF rollouts before collecting LF data. For locomotion, both processes use Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025), and for Franka robot learning, the worker controls the robot and the learner runs ManiSkill (Tao et al., 2024). In locomotion, coupled LF environments match HF velocity-tracking commands, and coupled and uncoupled LF environments inherit HF terrain assignments. In manipulation, coupled LF environments match the measured HF object pose.

Franka task. Figure 7 shows the physical Franka robot HF environment and its ManiSkill LF simulation counterpart. In the HF environment, an Intel RealSense D435 camera estimates the cube’s position and orientation from AprilTags attached to its six faces. The task is to grasp a cube with edge length 3.8 cm and lift its center to the fixed goal (x, y, z ) = (0 .45 , 0, 0.2715) min the robot base frame. The tabletop is at z = 0 .0545 m, placing the goal 0.198 m above the cube center when the cube is on the table. Initial cube positions lie within x ∈ [0 .35 , 0.50] mand y ∈ [−0.075 , 0.075] m. Simulation samples these coordinates uniformly, with yaw sampled uniformly from [−π/ 4, π/ 4] . The physical cube may rest on any face and at any yaw. At reset, we relabel its axes using cube symmetry to express its initial orientation in the same convention. Between physical episodes, a scripted routine repositions the cube within this region if it remains grasped. Otherwise, a human operator randomly repositions it. Each episode comprises H = 60 

actions at 5 Hz and continues to the horizon after success. The task is harder in the HF environment because the side-mounted camera can lose sight of the cube during contact. Across both methods, approximately 69% of training control steps lack a valid cube pose estimate. When perception fails, we retain the last valid pose, updating its position from the end effector if a grasp is detected. Grasp detection relies on gripper width and may be delayed. By contrast, the LF simulator provides ground-truth object and grasp states. Friction and controller stiffness also differ between fidelities.
