# Soft-Update MSA for Nonconvex Stochastic Optimal Control: Theory and Algorithms 

Shaolin Ji ∗ Xinmeng Liu †

Abstract 

For the method of successive approximations (MSA), a fundamental challenge is that the stochastic maximum principle (SMP) provides an optimality condition rather than a descent rule. To overcome this difficulty for nonconvex stochastic control problems, we develop a soft-update MSA (SU-MSA) with a second-order-adjoint correction of the Hamiltonian and a neural realization called DeepSMP. Both an explicit stepsize rule guaranteeing cost descent and the convergence of the algorithm are established. Moreover, the global SMP condition is asymptotically satisfied when the corrected Hamiltonian is convex, while in a special case the cost is further proved to converge to the global optimum. To extend SU-MSA to high dimensions, we develop DeepSMP using a differentiable smooth approximation of the corrected Hamiltonian and analyze the convergence of the resulting objective and its gradient. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. 

Key words: method of successive approximations; stochastic maximum principle; noncon-vex stochastic optimal control; soft update; high dimension; deep learning. 

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 49M05, 93E20; Secondary 49K45, 49M20, 68T07. 

# 1 Introduction 

The rapid development of deep learning has opened new possibilities for solving high-dimensional stochastic control problems; see, e.g., [15, 18, 3, 2]. A common approach is to parameterize the control by a neural network and optimize the resulting cost by gradient-based methods. In parallel, the method of successive approximations (MSA) provides an iterative framework built directly upon the Pontryagin maximum principle. At each iteration, MSA solves the state and adjoint equations and updates the control through Hamiltonian minimiza-tion. Its central difficulty is that the maximum principle gives an optimality condition rather than a descent rule: a Hamiltonian-improving control also perturbs the state and adjoint pro-cesses, and the resulting error may offset the nominal improvement. Consequently, the basic MSA need not converge. Existing modifications recover stability by restricting or regularizing the control perturba-tion. In deterministic control, representative approaches include convex-combination updates, short-interval replacement, and augmented-Hamiltonian schemes; see, e.g., [14, 20, 19, 17]. For controlled diffusions, the perturbation analysis becomes more delicate when the control enters the diffusion coefficient. Kerimkulov, ˇSiˇ ska, and Szpruch [11] introduced quadratic penalties on variations in the drift, diffusion, and state derivative of the Hamiltonian, and established

Ji and Xu [9] extended this framework to stochas-tic recursive control with possibly nonconvex control domains, using the second-order adjoint process together with BMO martingale estimates to relax structural assumptions required in the earlier analysis. More recently, they incorporated the second-order adjoint correction di-rectly into a spike-update scheme, yielding an O(ε) corrected-Hamiltonian contribution with an

Under the differentiability setting of the local stochastic maximum principle, the control derivative of the Hamiltonian represents the first variation of the cost functional. In particular, the gradient flow studied by Sethi and ˇSiˇ ska [24] is the L2-gradient flow of J in the control space, whereas direct neural methods such as [5] perform gradient descent after parameterizing the control. Although the optimization variables are different, both are driven by first-order information of the cost functional. The second-order correction determines the candidate, while the soft update controls the realized displacement. Under uniform semiconvexity of the corrected Hamiltonian, this balance yields an explicit stepsize rule that guarantees a fixed proportion of the corrected-Hamiltonian improvement is converted into actual cost decrease. This distinction is also important in numerical implementation. Exact pointwise Hamilto-nian minimization can be difficult in high-dimensional problems; Li et al. We therefore develop DeepSMP, which samples perturbed controls around the current control, evaluates their corrected-Hamiltonian values, and optimizes a smooth soft-min of these values in the network parameter space. The perturbations expose the candidate geometry created by the second-order correction, while gradient descent remains the computa-tional engine. Hence, the essential difference from direct neural control is not whether gradient optimization is used, but what information is supplied to it. Our main contributions are as follows. First, we propose a soft-update MSA (SU-MSA) for stochastic control problems with control-dependent diffusion. The second-order correction is endogenous, signed, and direction-dependent, while the soft update independently controls the realized displacement. In particular, the correction can alter the local geometry of the candidate objective and produce a convexity–concavity reversal at an ordinary-Hamiltonian local minimizer, revealing favorable finite perturbations where ordinary-Hamiltonian gradient updates stagnate and positive proximal regularization further strengthens the local minimum. Second, we establish the cost upper bound (1.4), which separates the corrected-Hamiltonian improvement from the quadratic soft-update remainder. Combined with uniform semicon-vexity of the corrected Hamiltonian, this estimate yields an explicit stepsize rule guarantee-ing proportional cost descent. The resulting cost values converge, while both the accepted corrected-Hamiltonian improvement and the control increments vanish asymptotically. When the corrected Hamiltonian is convex, the corrected-SMP residual further converges to zero in 

L1([0 , T ] × Ω). For a class of nonconvex stochastic control problems admitting an exact Itˆ oreduction, this residual coincides with the global optimality gap, so its convergence yields con-vergence of the cost to the global optimum. Third, we develop DeepSMP as a neural realization of SU-MSA. DeepSMP combines perturbation-based exploration, corrected-Hamiltonian evaluation, a differentiable smooth-min, and parameter-space soft updates, allowing a gradient-based neural solver to exploit second-order diffusion information without solving the pointwise corrected-Hamiltonian minimization exactly. We further prove that both the sampled smooth-min objective and its actor-parameter gradient converge in probability to their corrected-Hamiltonian counterparts as the action perturbations localize. Illustrative examples demonstrate ordinary-Hamiltonian stagnation, convexity–concavity reversal, and the time- and direction-dependent adjustment induced by the second-order adjoint, while numerical experiments compare DeepSMP with the direct deep-learning method of Han and E [5] and modified-MSA baselines. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formulates the stochastic control problem and introduces the adjoint equations and the corrected Hamiltonian. Section 3 3presents SU-MSA and establishes its descent and convergence properties. Section 4 develops DeepSMP and its theoretical analysis. Section 5 reports the numerical experiments.

3.2 Analytical comparisons with proximal MSA 

The following examples demonstrate three advantages of SU-MSA over the scalar proximal update in [24]. First, every control that pointwise minimizes its ordinary Hamiltonian is a fixed point of the proximal iteration, including nonoptimal controls. The first example shows that the second-order-adjoint correction can reverse the local geometry of such a stagnation point: a local minimum of the ordinary Hamiltonian becomes a nonoptimal point for the corrected Hamiltonian, thereby generating an improving direction. Second, the proximal parameter ρ as-signs the same weight at every time, state, and sample, whereas the second-order adjoint adapts the evaluation of diffusion perturbations to the current trajectory and their future cost conse-quences. The second example illustrates this adaptation through an explicit time-dependent weight. Third, the correction provides risk-sensitive weighting across diffusion directions, as demonstrated by the multidimensional example. In each case, the corrected-Hamiltonian candi-date equals the optimal control, and the stepsize rule accepts the full update under the common parameter choice in Remark 3.2.

# 4 DeepSMP: A Neural Realization of the Soft-Update MSA 

In this section, we develop DeepSMP, a neural implementation of the soft-update MSA introduced in Section 3. The continuous-time scheme is generally not directly computable: the state and adjoint equations rarely admit closed-form solutions, and the pointwise corrected-Hamiltonian minimization must itself be solved numerically and may be nonconvex. Moreover, minimizing actions obtained at finitely many simulated time–state pairs do not by themselves define a feedback law that can be evaluated at unseen states. 42 DeepSMP addresses these issues through time discretization, Monte Carlo sampling, and neural approximation. At each outer iteration, the reference control network is frozen, the state dynamics are simulated, and the first- and second-order adjoint processes are approximated using the Deep BSDE methodology. A differentiable actor objective is then constructed from a smooth surrogate of the sampled corrected-Hamiltonian minimum. Optimizing this objective yields candidate control-network parameters, which are incorporated into the reference network through a parameter-space soft update. The resulting scheme avoids spatial state grids and produces a reusable feedback law. The distinction from a direct method lies in the optimization objective. A direct method minimizes a Monte Carlo approximation of the original cost functional J(πθ) over the control-network parameters, whereas DeepSMP constructs its actor objective from the state and adjoint variables and the corrected Hamiltonian. Monte Carlo samples are therefore used to estimate this maximum-principle-based objective and its gradient, rather than merely to provide a finite collection of pathwise minimizing actions. Although the resulting actor problem may remain nonconvex, its updates are guided by the action-level structure encoded in the corrected Hamil-tonian rather than by the end-to-end cost alone.

4.3 DeepSMP and parameter-space soft update analysis 

DeepSMP jointly trains the first- and second-order adjoint networks and the control net-works within a single iterative procedure. This differs from classical MSA and many of its modified numerical implementations, which typically employ a nested inner–outer structure: for each outer control update, the state and adjoint systems are first solved, or the corre-sponding inner problem is optimized, to a prescribed accuracy. Instead, DeepSMP updates the adjoint-related and control parameters simultaneously, while separating their coupled evolution through a two-time-scale learning rule, with faster updates for the adjoint-related parameters and slower updates for the control parameters. The reference control parameters are further stabilized through parameter-space soft interpolation. This simultaneous scheme avoids repeat-edly solving the inner problem to high accuracy before each control update, thereby reducing the computational burden of nested iterations, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Since a convex interpolation of neural-network parameters does not in general coincide with a pointwise convex interpolation of the corresponding feedback controls, the process-level descent estimate cannot be applied verbatim. We therefore establish a parameter-space counterpart of the corrected-Hamiltonian upper bound. The analysis is carried out in an exact-adjoint oracle setting: for a given reference control, the first- and second-order adjoint processes are assumed to be the exact solutions of their corresponding adjoint equations. Under this idealized setting, the result below shows that the cost variation along a parameter interpolation path is controlled by the variation of the corrected Hamiltonian, up to a second-order remainder measured in a weighted discrete L2-norm of the parameter displacement. In the practical DeepSMP algorithm, these adjoints are replaced by learned neural-network approximations, whose effect can be viewed as an additional inexactness term. # 5 Numerical experiments 

5.1 General experimental setup 

In this section, we evaluate the proposed second-order corrected DeepSMP method on a sequence of nonconvex stochastic control problems. The numerical experiments are organized in two layers. The first layer compares DeepSMP with the direct method, where the control network is trained by directly minimizing a Monte Carlo approximation of the cost functional. The second layer compares DeepSMP with a modified-MSA-type baseline, in order to isolate the effect of the second-order adjoint correction. All continuous-time problems are discretized by the Euler–Maruyama scheme on the uniform grid 0 = t0 < t 1 < · · · < t N = T , with ∆ t = T /N . Unless otherwise stated, we set T = 1, N =40, and U = [ −2, 2] du , and enforce the control constraint through the componentwise tanh-box parameterization uθ,k (x) = 2 tanh( rθ,k (x)). All algorithms are implemented in Python 3.10.20 with TensorFlow and run in double precision on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The default training batch size is 256, and the reported costs are evaluated on a fixed validation sample of 50 ,000 Brownian trajectories. Unless otherwise specified, results are reported as the mean and standard deviation over five independent runs; within each comparison, all methods use the same random seeds and validation paths. 57 In DeepSMP, the control functions and the martingale-integrand processes q and Q are rep-resented by separate time-indexed fully connected networks, one for each time level, while p0 and 

P0 are trainable parameters. Each network has two hidden layers with Swish activations, with the layer widths specified for each test problem. The q- and Q-networks have linear outputs, whereas the control-network outputs are mapped through the tanh-box transformation above. No batch normalization is used. The adjoint-related and control-network parameters are up-dated by separate Adam optimizers with learning rates 10 −3 and 2 × 10 −4, respectively, thereby implementing a practical two-time-scale training scheme in which the adjoint-related parameters evolve on the faster scale. The control network is trained through a candidate-based smooth approximation to the pointwise minimization of the second-order corrected Hamiltonian. The Swish–tanh architecture satisfies the activation and output-map conditions of Assumption 4.1; under its bounded-parameter-path condition, Proposition 4.1 applies. Following Han and E [5], the direct method represents the feedback control at each time level by a separate neural network, propagates the state through the Euler–Maruyama dynamics, and jointly trains all control-network parameters by directly minimizing the Monte Carlo approxi-mation of the discretized cost functional. It therefore introduces neither adjoint equations nor a Hamiltonian-based control update. The direct baseline uses the same control-network archi-tecture and tanh-box output parameterization as DeepSMP. In the main comparison, batch normalization is additionally inserted after each hidden affine transformation and before the Swish activation. For the comparison with modified MSA, we construct a modified-MSA-type neural base-line in the same computational framework as DeepSMP. The second-order adjoint system and the corresponding matrix-weighted Hamiltonian correction are replaced by the scalar-weighted isotropic ρ-augmentation of modified MSA, while the remaining neural implementation and training protocol are unchanged. This comparison is designed to isolate the effect of the second-order adjoint correction. 

Remark 5.1 (Two-time-scale training) . The use of different update speeds is motivated by the classical two-time-scale stochastic-approximation principle [1, 13, 12]. The adjoint-related pa-rameters are assigned a larger learning rate than the control-network parameters. On the faster adjoint time scale, the reference control varies slowly and may therefore be regarded as ap-proximately frozen over a short training window, allowing the first- and second-order adjoint networks to track the adjoint processes associated with the current reference control. Con-versely, on the slower control time scale, the adjoint variables adapt more rapidly, so that the corrected-Hamiltonian update is based on adjoint estimates that are better adapted to the current reference control. This separation mitigates the feedback instability caused by simul-taneously changing the control and the adjoint approximations. Since fixed learning rates are used in the present implementation, the term “two-time-scale” refers to a practical separation of update speeds rather than to the classical asymptotic stochastic-approximation condition requiring the ratio of the slow and fast step sizes to vanish. 

5.2 Comparison with the direct method 

We first compare DeepSMP with the direct method. The direct method optimizes the mapping 

θ 7 −→ J(uθ)directly in the neural-network parameter space, without introducing adjoint equations or a Hamiltonian-based control update. Although this approach is straightforward and does not require a spatial grid, its end-to-end optimization may be sensitive to nonconvex parameter landscapes containing locally flat regions or multiple competing low-cost regions. By contrast, DeepSMP exploits the state–adjoint–Hamiltonian structure induced by the stochastic maximum principle and performs the control update through a candidate-based 58 smooth minimization of the second-order corrected Hamiltonian in the action space. Rather than relying solely on an infinitesimal descent direction of the Monte Carlo cost in parameter space, the method compares finitely perturbed candidate actions according to their corrected-Hamiltonian values. This action-space comparison may provide a more discriminative update signal when the end-to-end parameter gradient is weak or locally flat, and offers a mechanism for moving between separated low-value action regions.

The two equal-Frobenius-norm diffusion perturbations are therefore assigned different values by the Pt-weighted quadratic form. The example thus compares two distinct update geometries. The modified-MSA augmen-tation applies a scalar isotropic penalty in the space of diffusion perturbations, whereas the DeepSMP correction applies a direction-dependent matrix weighting determined by the second-order adjoint. When the uncorrected Hamiltonian only weakly separates the two active action regions, the scalar augmentation provides no additional discrimination between their equal-norm diffusion perturbations.
