# Motion Retargeting with Spatiotemporal Preferences for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation 

Xiongbo Shi 1,2,3, Shihang Du 1, Zongyou Yu 1, Ao Zhou 1, Xudong Zhang 1,Ziyu Lin 1,2, Haodong Zhang 1, Junqiao Zhao 1, and Guang Chen 1,2,3,∗

Abstract — Human motion retargeting bridges the embodi-ment gap between humans and humanoid robots by converting human demonstrations into reference trajectories for whole-body control and loco-manipulation. For object-interaction tasks, successful execution depends on accurately preserving body–object relations at critical stages. However, uniform per-frame iteration budgets do not account for these localized accuracy requirements, limiting the computational efficiency of retargeting. To address this limitation, we present Spa-tiotemporal Retargeting (STR), which uses keyframes and key body parts to allocate optimization effort. Specifically, a vision-language model selects relevant motion signals and body parts, and keyframes are identified from changes in the selected signals. Based on these selections, STR assigns additional solver iterations to keyframes and higher interaction weights to the selected parts. To further improve contact fidelity, we intro-duce Mechanics-Aware Contact Anchoring (MCA) to refine object-surface contact positions using an approximate object-wrench model while retaining the selected surface directions. Building on the resulting references, we develop Spatiotemporal Reinforcement Learning (STRL), which combines adaptive key-stage sampling with a contact reward based on target proximity and object contact forces for downstream control. Simulation experiments on a Unitree G1 and real-world experiments on a Unitree R1 demonstrate that STR improves geometric contact coverage and continuity at lower computational cost, while the resulting references improve downstream task completion across diverse object-interaction motions. 

I. INTRODUCTION Human demonstrations provide a rich source of whole-body motions for learning humanoid locomotion and loco-manipulation skills [1], [2], [3]. Parametric body models and motion collections provide reusable representations of these demonstrations [4], [5], while interaction datasets addi-tionally capture object motion and body–object contact [6], [7], [8]. However, directly transferring human motions to humanoid robots is challenging because of substantial dif-ferences in body proportions, joint configurations, and kine-matic limits [9], [10]. Motion retargeting addresses this embodiment gap by converting human demonstrations into robot-compatible reference motions. Learning tracking poli-cies from reference trajectories has enabled both simulated character control [11], [12] and humanoid whole-body con-trol [13]. The quality of these references directly affects how accurately and robustly the learned policy can reproduce the demonstrated behavior [14]. For loco-manipulation, retarget-

Recent retargeting methods have substantially improved the quality of such references. Video-derived references can also encode scene context for humanoid control [18]. General Motion Retargeting (GMR) reduces artifacts caused by the human–robot embodiment gap through non-uniform local scaling and inverse kinematics, and shows that retar-geting artifacts can significantly affect motion-tracking ro-bustness [14]. OmniRetarget further preserves body–object– environment interactions using an interaction mesh together with kinematic and non-penetration constraints [16]. These methods improve the fidelity of the complete reference mo-tion, but do not explicitly determine, from the demonstration, when additional retargeting accuracy is most important and which body parts should receive greater emphasis at those moments. Errors at such keyframes or key body parts can disrupt the interaction even when the remaining refer-ence closely follows the human demonstration. Therefore, improving the overall fidelity of a reference motion alone may not be sufficient. The retargeting process should also account for where, both in time and across the body, higher accuracy is most valuable. A. Motion Retargeting 

Motion retargeting transfers motion between bodies with different kinematics. Classical methods adapt reference mo-tions through spacetime constraints [21], end-effector track-ing with inverse rate control [22], or sequential filtering with kinematic and dynamic constraints [23]. Learning-based methods use forward kinematics and cycle consistency [24], or skeleton-aware network operations [25], to learn motion mappings across skeletons. Vision-language supervision can further preserve motion meaning by aligning embeddings of rendered source and retargeted motions [26]. Beyond joint poses, interaction meshes preserve local spatial relations between a character and its surroundings [15]. Contact-aware retargeting preserves self-contact and ground contact while reducing self-penetration [27]. Cheynel et al. use sparse mesh keypoints with relevance weights that vary over space and time to describe contacts for character retargeting [28]. For humanoid robots, early work adapts captured ges-tures to joint-angle and velocity limits [9]. Geometric re-targeting matches body-link orientations through inverse kinematics [10], and interaction-mesh-based adaptation ac-counts for robot kinematics and dynamic balance [29]. GMR combines non-uniform local scaling and two-stage inverse kinematics, and evaluates the effect of retargeting artifacts on motion tracking [14]. VideoMimic jointly reconstructs human motion and scene geometry from video to generate references for contextual humanoid control [18]. OmniRetar-get preserves body–object–environment interactions through an interaction-mesh objective with joint, velocity, non-penetration, and stance-foot constraints [16]. Our method builds on constrained interaction-preserving retargeting and uses demonstration-derived keyframes and body-part anno-tations to allocate solver iterations and objective weights. 

B. Humanoid Whole-Body Control 

Humanoid whole-body control requires coordinated mo-tion while maintaining balance and respecting physical constraints. Motion imitation uses reference trajectories to train policies for these skills. DeepMimic combines motion-imitation and task rewards in reinforcement learning for sim-ulated characters [11], while AMP learns an adversarial mo-tion prior that supplies a style reward for task execution [30]. ASE learns reusable skill embeddings from unstructured motion data [31], and PHC scales imitation to large motion collections while learning recovery from falls [12]. For humanoid robots, H2O and OmniH2O learn whole-body imitation policies for human-to-humanoid teleoperation [1], [2], and HOVER unifies multiple control interfaces through policy distillation [32]. HumanPlus uses human shadowing to collect demonstrations for learning autonomous whole-body tasks [3]. ASAP learns an action correction model from real-world data and uses it to fine-tune policies in simulation [33]. BeyondMimic combines whole-body motion tracking with guided diffusion for downstream task control [13]. Loco-manipulation additionally requires maintaining the intended interaction with an object. HumanoidBench illus-trates the difficulty of learning coordinated locomotion and manipulation in a common benchmark [34]. InterMimic learns physics-based human–object interactions from motion capture in simulation [35], while SkillMimic learns reusable basketball interaction skills by jointly imitating human and ball motion [36]. Opt2Skill trains humanoid policies to imi-tate dynamically feasible trajectories produced by trajectory optimization [37]. HDMI learns interactive humanoid skills from reconstructed human and object motions in video [38], while ResMimic adds object-aware residual control to a pretrained motion tracker [39]. 

C. Preference-Aware Motion Control 

Spatial and temporal priorities can be expressed through the body targets, contact relations, and reference frames used for motion generation and control. Spatially, ExBody emphasizes upper-body imitation while relaxing leg imitation in favor of velocity tracking [40]. ExBody2 separates local keypoint tracking from velocity control [41], and HOVER uses command masks to support different combinations of body and joint targets [32]. In human–object motion syn-thesis, OMOMO uses hand positions as intermediate contact targets [8], CHOIS combines contact guidance with sparse object waypoints [17], and InterDiff corrects predicted inter-actions in contact-centered coordinate frames [42]. For robot object interaction, OmniRetarget preserves body–object– environment relations through an interaction mesh [16], while ResMimic uses object-tracking and contact-tracking rewards to train an object-aware residual policy [39]. HOI-Retarget directly minimizes errors to labeled contact targets in the object frame through windowed trajectory optimiza-tion, preserving contact locations and their active intervals under kinematic limits [43]. Temporally, selected poses and motion segments can guide both the control objective and the training distribution. Guided time warping edits motion timing under sparse temporal constraints [44]. MaskedMimic controls simu-lated characters from partial motion specifications, including sparse keyframes and body targets [45]. RobotKeyframing combines sparse keyframe objectives with dense rewards for legged locomotion [19]. AdaMimic combines sparse global keyframe tracking with dense local tracking and learns timing and action adjustments [20]. KINO uses a vision-language model to select library keyframes, aligns them to the scene, and uses motion saliency to guide keyframe sampling during policy training [46]. BeyondMimic uses failure-based adaptive sampling in its motion-tracking stage to emphasize difficult reference segments [13]. Language and vision-language models offer another way to specify task-relevant relationships. Code as Policies gen-erates executable control programs from language instruc-tions [47], and VoxPoser constructs spatial value maps for trajectory planning [48]. ReKep expresses manipulation as sequences of relational keypoint constraints [49], while VLMPC uses vision-language reasoning to guide candidate actions and evaluate predicted outcomes [50]. These ap-proaches connect high-level task descriptions to geometric or control objectives. Our method applies spatial and temporal priorities to retargeting: keyframes receive additional solver iterations, and the associated key body parts receive higher objective weights. The resulting dense references are used for policy learning with key-stage sampling. III. M ETHOD 

In this section, we introduce STR and STRL for humanoid loco-manipulation (Fig. 2). We first formulate the retargeting problem, then select motion signals and participating body parts from the demonstration video. Evaluating the selected signals on the aligned trajectories yields key stages and their trigger keyframes. STR combines these spatial and temporal priorities with contact targets designed from object geometry and motion to optimize a dense robot reference. Finally, STRL uses this reference for whole-body policy learning with key-stage sampling and contact reward shaping.

C. Spatiotemporal Retargeting (STR) 

Successful object interaction requires key parts to estab-lish the required body–object relationships at the relevant keyframes and maintain them throughout key stages. How-ever, assigning the same iteration budget to every frame and the same weight to every body part does not reflect these spatial and temporal requirements and reduces retar-geting efficiency. We therefore allocate more iterations to keyframes and assign higher weights to the body–object relationships of key parts throughout key stages, as shown in Fig. 2(b). We implement these preferences within the constrained interaction-mesh formulation [16].

D. Spatiotemporal Reinforcement Learning (STRL) 

A kinematically feasible reference alone does not ensure that the intended contacts can be maintained during execution under robot–object dynamics. Different stages also vary in learning difficulty, while whole-body tracking rewards may provide insufficient emphasis on the body parts responsible for contact. We therefore introduce STRL to prioritize key and difficult stages during training and explicitly reward the selected parts for tracking their object-surface contact targets. Inspired by [13], [16], we use proximal policy optimiza-tion (PPO) to train a whole-body tracking policy. Specif-ically, the actor receives robot proprioception, reference-motion commands, and the previous action. Its actions are converted to joint-position targets for a PD controller. The critic additionally receives privileged body and object states during training. Dense body- and object-tracking rewards and the penalties for action changes, joint-limit violations, and undesired contacts are retained. The contact term augments these rewards. Training settings are provided in Appendix B. 

Allocating training starts across stages. After mapping the detected stage boundaries to the RL reference clock, we partition the sampleable timeline into disjoint key and ordinary stages {I j}. Stage j includes its start frame and excludes its end frame. The end serves as a target for measuring stage completion. The stage-length factor makes w j( f j + β ) a relative priority per frame, so short stages do not receive excessive mass solely because the timeline has been partitioned. The global branch maintains coverage throughout the motion. At the sampled frame, the simulation is initialized from the reference state and the policy begins tracking the subsequent trajectory. All f j start at zero. For an adaptively assigned stage, reaching its end records one success, while early termination before that end records one failure. We update only stages with new outcomes using f j ← (1 − μ) f j + μ bf j, where bf j

is the fraction of failures among the newly resolved assign-ments and μ ∈ (0, 1] is the averaging rate. Each assignment contributes at most one outcome. Domain Randomization 

The current object-interaction preset randomizes ob-ject mass over 0.1–2.0 kg, object center-of-mass offsets within ±0.08 m per axis, and inertia over 50–150% of the nominal value. Torso center-of-mass offsets are bounded by ±(0.025 , 0.05 , 0.075 ) m, and joint-position bias by ±0.01 rad. Velocity perturbations are applied every 1– 3 s, with per-axis limits of 0.3 m/s and 0.78 rad/s. The actor observation-noise bounds are 0.05 for the reference rotation representation, 0.2 rad/s for base angular velocity, 0.01 rad for joint position, and 0.5 rad/s for joint velocity. Object-shape randomization is not implemented in this preset.
