Abstract
Benchmarking in reinforcement learning is usually relative: algorithms are ranked by re-
turns on environments with unknown optima. Distance to optimality remains unobservable,
while conclusions may depend on reward design, stochasticity, hyperparameters, environ-
ment selection, and simulator details. We develop a ground-truth-first framework based
on converse optimality, constructing each task with a certified optimal value function and
policy withheld from learners and used only for evaluation.
The framework unifies two
modes: sensitivity benchmarking samples certified environments from continuous-time gen-
erators to assess robustness to task variation, while oracle-referenced comparison uses native
discrete-time generators to evaluate policies via optimality gap, regret, and policy error.
Continuous-time models support physically interpretable sensitivity studies but introduce
dependence on the simulator step (∆t) and numerical scheme. Native discrete-time models
align the environment, oracle, and learner on the same transition law, eliminating external
integration choices as a confounder. We reproduce quantitative results covering optimizer
sensitivity and oracle-referenced comparisons on serial planar arms, dynamically extended
nonholonomic vehicles, and quadrotor-inspired tasks.
The methodology extends beyond
smooth affine Gaussian systems: virtual-input pullback handles non-affine raw-action inter-
faces and finite-covariance non-Gaussian disturbances. A non-smooth converse optimality
formulation enables certified benchmarks with kinked value functions and switching surfaces.
For partially observed evaluation, an information-state formulation reduces benchmarking
to converse optimality on a sufficient statistic and provides an information-penalty reference.
Code, fixtures, and diagnostics are available on the public project page.

Introduction
Performance evaluation has become the default empirical language of reinforcement learning
(RL). A new method is introduced, evaluated on benchmark environments, and compared against
a changing set of baselines. This protocol is valuable, but it leaves a basic control-theoretic quan-
tity hidden: distance to optimality. When the optimal policy is unknown, a benchmark can tell
us that one algorithm achieved a larger return than another; it cannot tell us whether that
algorithm is near optimal, whether all compared methods are poor, or whether the ranking is
an artifact of reward scaling, random seeds, task weighting, environment selection, or simula-
tor conventions.
This limitation is not superficial; it is fundamentally structural.
Deep RL
conclusions are well-known to depend strongly on implementation choices, evaluation protocols,
random seeds, hyperparameters, and statistical reporting practices [1, 2, 3]. For example, aggre-
gate rankings may fluctuate significantly when tasks are weighted or normalized differently [4].
Recent critiques therefore argue that benchmark scores alone are insufficient evidence for broad
algorithmic claims; controlled scientific tests are needed to explain how and when algorithms
work [5]. For RL control, two particular questions arise: does a learning pipeline remain reliable
when the environment varies? and how far is a learned policy from the true optimum on a fixed
task? Conventional benchmarks struggle with both when the optimum is unavailable.
Converse optimality offers a direct alternative. It reverses the usual direction of optimal
control: rather than solving a Bellman or Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, one starts
from a desired value function and policy and constructs environments for which they are optimal.
The environment is therefore generated together with its certificate, turning evaluation from
relative scoring to absolute calibrated measurement: optimality gaps, regret, Bellman residuals,
and sensitivity across distributions of certified tasks. The central claim of this paper is that
converse optimality transforms RL benchmarks into known-answer experimental instruments: it
tells not only which method returns the most, but why a method fails, how far it is from optimal,
whether conclusions survive controlled environment variation, and whether the strongest baseline
is genuinely near the certified optimum.
We organize this view into two complementary modes.
In sensitivity benchmarking, a
continuous-time generator defines a distribution over certified systems, turning environment
variation into an experimental variable that tests pipeline consistency; the learner experiences
a discretized version whose behavior depends on the integration step, which is precisely what
sensitivity studies are meant to probe. In oracle-referenced comparison, native discrete-time
fixtures define the transition law, oracle (V ⋆, π⋆), and evaluation protocol in the same formal-
ism, removing the discretization confound and enabling absolute evaluation by optimality gap,
regret, Bellman residual, and policy error.
Time formalism is part of this design. Continuous-time generators produce physically inter-
pretable non-linear systems, but the MDP experienced by a digital learner depends on the inte-
gration step ∆t and numerical scheme. This dependence is not a defect; it makes continuous-time
generators well-suited for sensitivity studies. Native discrete-time generators define the transi-
tion law, oracle, and evaluation protocol in the same formalism, removing the discretization con-
found. Thus, continuous-time generators are natural for sensitivity, whereas native discrete-time
fixtures are natural for absolute algorithm comparison.

The framework is not limited to smooth, affine, Gaussian, fully observed examples. The
present work extends it along three independent axes. First, non-affine raw-action interfaces and
finite-covariance non-Gaussian disturbances are handled by virual-input pullback, and demon-
strated by the example of the ConverseQuad benchmark. Second, non-smooth value functions
are managed via viscosity solutions and Clarke subdifferentials, enabling benchmarks with kinked
values and switching surfaces. Third, partially observed evaluation is reduced to converse opti-
mality on a sufficient information state, with a linear-quadratic Gaussian (LQG) information-
penalty reference. These extensions share the same certificate logic; they differ in the interface
the learner sees and the regularity of the certified value.
Our contribution is methodological. We review the foundations of converse optimality and
contribute: (i) a ground-truth-first fixture formalism exporting certified values and policies;
(ii) a separation between continuous-time sensitivity families and native discrete-time oracle-
referenced fixtures; (iii) an extension to non-affine and non-Gaussian tasks, including Converse-
Quad; (iv) a non-smooth converse theory via viscosity solutions and Clarke subdifferentials;
(v) an information-state formulation for partially observed benchmarks; and (vi) a validation
and evaluation protocol using Bellman consistency, closed-loop checks, and Common Random
Numbers (CRN) schedules, demonstrated on optimizer sensitivity and on robotic control fami-
lies. Section 2 reviews related work, Sections 3––5 develop the framework, Sections 6––8 present
the extensions, and Sections 9––10 discuss limitations and open problems. The accompanying
project page provides code, fixtures, results, and diagnostics.

Related Work
RL benchmark suites.
Shared environment suites—ALE, MuJoCo, Gym, Procgen, Mini-
Grid, bsuite, D4RL [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]—enable large-scale empirical comparison but generally
lack known optimal policies. Their primary signal is relative return, and a method may outper-
4


form baselines while remaining far from optimal.
Empirical reliability and statistical practice.
RL conclusions depend heavily on imple-
mentation details, random seeds, and statistical reporting [1, 2, 3]. Benchmark scores alone are
insufficient evidence for broad claims [5]. We adopt this diagnostic perspective but change the
experimental substrate: generating tasks with certified optima makes absolute error observable.
Classical optimal control and inverse optimal control.
Classical optimal control fixes
dynamics and cost and solves for a value function and policy, typically via the HJB equation
or dynamic programming [13, 14]. This is intractable for general nonlinear systems due to the
curse of dimensionality.
Inverse optimal control and inverse reinforcement learning take the
opposite direction: they infer a cost or reward from observed trajectories or demonstrations
[15, 16]. Converse optimality differs from both. It neither solves a forward problem nor infers
a cost from data; it prescribes a value function and constructs dynamics and state costs for
which the prescribed pair is provably optimal. This makes it uniquely suited to benchmark
generation, because the optimal reference is an input to the construction rather than an output
of an intractable computation.
Converse optimality.
Converse optimality was formalized for continuous-time deterministic
systems by Nevistic and Primbs [17], using HJB certificates, and extended to discrete-time sys-
tems by G¨ohrt et al. [18], using Bellman-equation conditions. Yaremenko et al. [19] turned the
discrete-time construction into a coordinate-free parametric generator for RL benchmarks, and
Ibrahim et al. [20] extended it to stochastic control-affine systems with direct oracle metrics.
We build on this line. We reuse its certificate logic, its quadratic specializations, and its CRN
evaluation protocol, and we extend it in three directions: non-smooth values, non-affine raw ac-
tions with non-Gaussian disturbances, and information-state formulations for partially observed
evaluation.
Non-smooth optimal control and viscosity solutions.
Non-smooth value functions arise
naturally in optimal control whenever the control enters linearly without a quadratic penalty
(bang-bang control), when the horizon is minimized rather than discounted, when state or
control constraints are active, or when the dynamics switch between modes. In these regimes
the value function is typically only locally Lipschitz, with kinks along switching manifolds where
the classical gradient does not exist. The standard machinery replaces pointwise derivatives with
viscosity inequalities [21, 22] and classical gradients with Clarke subdifferentials [23, 24]; when
the resulting optimal feedback is discontinuous, the closed loop is interpreted as a differential
inclusion in the Filippov sense [25]. Existing converse constructions, however, assume candidate
values that are at least C1 or C2 [17, 18, 19], which excludes precisely the switching-surface and
bang-bang structures that make learning hard. We therefore develop a non-smooth converse
theory in which the smooth HJB certificate is replaced by a viscosity–Clarke condition, yielding
certified benchmarks whose optimal values have known kinks and whose optimal policies may
be discontinuous.
Partial observability and information-state reduction.
Partially observed control is clas-
sically reduced to a fully observed problem on an information state: a statistic of the observa-
tion–action history that is sufficient for dynamic programming [26, 27]. In finite-state POMDPs
this statistic is a belief vector over the hidden state; in linear–Gaussian problems it is the Kalman
filter’s conditional mean and covariance; in general nonlinear continuous-state models it is an
infinite-dimensional belief density. Recurrent policies approximate this reduction in practice
[28, 29], and memory-oriented benchmarks probe it empirically [30], but they do not provide a
5


certified optimal reference. A further complication is that a full-state oracle may exploit infor-
mation the learner cannot access from its history, so comparing a history-based policy against a
latent-state optimum conflates learning error with an information penalty. No converse construc-
tion has been formulated on an information state with a certified output-feedback oracle. We
address this by reducing partially observed benchmarking to converse optimality on a sufficient
statistic, certifying the oracle in the learner’s own information set, and isolating the information
penalty analytically in an LQG reference.

What the extensions change.
The extensions in Sections 6–8 modify one element of this
picture each, while leaving the fixture concept unchanged. Non-smooth values replace the point-
wise Bellman residual with a viscosity–Clarke condition, so that the certificate remains valid at
kinks and switching surfaces. Non-affine raw actions and finite-covariance non-Gaussian distur-
bances change the action interface and the disturbance law, while preserving the same certificate
in virtual-input coordinates. Partial observability replaces the state s with a sufficient informa-
tion state b, so that the oracle is attainable from the learner’s history rather than from a hidden
state; this also makes the information penalty separable from the learning gap. In all three
cases, the task and its certificate are constructed together, and the reference pair is withheld
until evaluation. The remainder of the paper develops the two modes (Sections 4–5), the three
extensions (Sections 6–8), and validation and reproducibility protocols.

Mode I – Continuous-Time Sensitivity Benchmarking
The certificate of Section 3 guarantees optimality when certain conditions hold. To turn that
certificate into a usable benchmark, one needs converse generator templates—recipes that, given
a desired value function V and policy, produce the dynamics and costs that satisfy the certifi-
cate. This section develops Mode I for continuous-time sensitivity studies. Mode II (Section 5)
develops native discrete-time fixtures for absolute comparison, and Sections 6–8 extend both
to non-affine interfaces, non-smooth values, and partial observability. Modes I and II together
cover the smooth, fully observed, affine-action case; the extensions relax each of these in turn,
while leaving the certificate logic unchanged.

a sampled environment θ and a learning procedure, a trained policy πθ is obtained. The relative

sample environments, train, compute RS with the known V ⋆

4.3
Sensitivity experiment
The sensitivity protocol is illustrated with the continuous-time case study of a generator pro-
ducing a sample of 160 unstable three-dimensional control problems, each with a known optimal
value [19]. Two PPO pipelines, identical in all respects except the optimizer (Adam vs. RAdam),
were trained on independent splits of 80 environments.
Relative suboptimality is computed for each trained policy πθ. An F-test comparing the two
RS distributions gave a variance ratio F ≈2.59 (p ≈1.7 × 10−5), rejecting the null of equal
variance at any conventional significance level.

Mode II uses native discrete-time generators. Unlike Mode I, the transition law itself is the

deterministic to stochastic dynamics, then to example families, then to the evaluation proto-

interface and the regularity class change.

method performs best, but also how far each is from optimality and how error is distributed

In the serial-random regime, A2C is concentrated far below zero, while DDPG has a wide

initial states favour algorithms that can exploit broad transition coverage, while still revealing
instability in DDPG.
In the serial-fixed regime, PPO’s distribution is shifted closest to zero, whereas SAC and

Extension II: Non-Smooth Converse Optimality
The fixture viewpoint extends to value functions that are merely locally Lipschitz, such as
piecewise-quadratic templates with kinks along switching surfaces. This section develops non-
smooth converse optimality.
We replace the smooth HJB certificate of Sections 4–6 with a
viscosity–Clarke condition: classical gradients are replaced by Clarke subdifferentials, and the
pointwise HJB equation is replaced by its viscosity formulation. The construction is determin-
istic and continuous-time. A homotopy parameter selects a generalized gradient branch on the
switching surface, generating families of systems that share the same value function but differ
in the sharpness of the non-smooth behaviour. We work the construction end-to-end for a two-
17


dimensional piecewise-quadratic value function with a straight switching line, and we validate
the resulting fixture analytically.
7.1
Motivation: why non-smooth values matter
Bang-bang control, minimum-time problems, saturated policies, state and control constraints,
and hybrid or switching systems all produce optimal value functions that are locally Lipschitz but
not differentiable everywhere. When the control enters linearly and the cost does not penalize
control quadratically, the optimal policy is typically bang-bang, and the value function has kinks
along switching manifolds where the classical gradient does not exist [24]. In these regimes, the
classical HJB equation is ill-defined, and the smooth converse constructions of Sections 4–6 do
not apply.
Without a certified ground truth, a learner’s poor performance on such a task cannot be
attributed to the non-smoothness of the optimum rather than to approximation or optimization
error. Standard RL benchmarks can exhibit non-smooth value functions, but absent a refer-
ence they cannot distinguish genuine non-smoothness from overfitting. Non-smooth converse
optimality closes this gap: it produces tasks whose optimal value has a known kink and whose
optimal policy may be discontinuous, so that a learned policy can be measured against a certified
reference in exactly the regime that stresses smoothness assumptions.

7.5
General Constructive Procedure
We now turn Theorem 7.4.4 into a systematic procedure that, given a candidate locally Lipschitz
value function V , produces a triple (f, g, c) for which V is optimal in the viscosity sense. The
procedure makes no assumption about the specific form of the control cost ρ beyond strict
convexity and C2 regularity; the quadratic case is treated in Section 7.6 and instantiated in
Section 7.9.
The procedure uses Assumptions 7.3.1–7.3.2. The input-coupling matrix g : Rn →Rn×m
is prescribed and satisfies Assumption 7.3.1(b). The control set A is compact and nonempty,
or A = Rm in the unconstrained case. The candidate V : Rn →R is locally Lipschitz and its
Clarke subdifferential ∂CV (s) is explicitly characterized. Among the six steps below, Steps 1–3
are design choices: they depend on V , on the geometry of the switching surfaces, and on a gain
matrix K that shapes the uncontrolled dynamics. Steps 4–6 are then determined: the state cost,
the supersolution check, and the closed-loop well-posedness follow from the design choices.
Step 1. Subdifferential analysis. Compute the Clarke subdifferential ∂CV (s) for every s ∈
Rn. Identify:
• smooth regions—open sets on which ∂CV (s) = {∇V (s)} is a singleton;
• switching surfaces—lower-dimensional manifolds on which V is non-differentiable
and ∂CV (s) has more than one element.
On each switching surface, express ∂CV (s) as the convex hull of finitely many limiting
gradients from adjacent smooth regions. Introduce local parameters α ∈[0, 1]k (a single
scalar α for a codimension-one surface) that parametrize this convex hull. These pa-
rameters are homotopy knobs: different values of α produce different admissible systems
that share the same value function V .
Step 2. Selection of a generalized gradient. Choose a measurable selection p : Rn →Rn
with p(s) ∈∂CV (s) for every s. On smooth regions the selection is unique, p(s) =
∇V (s). On switching surfaces the selection may depend on the parameters α introduced
in Step 1. Different selections yield different admissible systems but leave V unchanged.

and regrets, followed by TD3, while the on-policy methods (PPO and A2C) are significantly
worse. Two effects drive this ordering. First, off-policy algorithms reuse past transitions many

the synchronous updates of A2C are less effective at representing the sharp jump in the optimal
control than the entropy-regularized exploration of SAC or the deterministic policy gradient of
DDPG.

This piecewise-quadratic family example demonstrates that the non-smooth theoretical frame-

Extension III: Partially Observed MDPs
The fixtures of Sections 4–7 expose the state to the learner. In many safety-critical applications—
robotic manipulation under occlusion, vehicle control with intermittent GPS, aerial navigation
36


with noisy sensors—the learner receives only observations, and the underlying state is hidden.
This section extends the ground-truth-first framework to partially observed evaluation. The
central idea is to replace the physical state s in the converse construction with an information
state b, the control-theoretic analogue of a belief state: a statistic of the observation–action
history that is sufficient for dynamic programming. The certificate is then constructed on b, and
the optimal output-feedback policy is certified in the learner’s own information set rather than
in a hidden latent state.
The extension has two layers. The first layer is a fully observed Markov decision process
(MDP) in b, to which the converse constructions of Sections 5–6 apply without modification.
The second layer is an output process that generates observations and updates b recursively.
The learner sees only observations and rewards; the private evaluator maintains b and applies
the certified feedback a∗(b). This separation is what allows partially observed benchmarks to
inherit the certified-optimum property of their fully observed counterparts.
A full-state oracle that observes s is generally not an admissible reference for an output-
feedback learner, because it may exploit information the learner cannot access. The same initial
belief and cost objective decompose as

9
Discussion
The framework trades broad ecological coverage for calibration: converse-generated fixtures
provide known-answer control problems with measurable optimality gaps, not replacements for
open-ended simulators or real-world platforms.
The families studied here exhibit nonlinear
drift, state-dependent actuation, delayed input channels, and stiff closed-loop behaviour, with
difficulty tunable via dimension, dynamic extension, coupling strength, control authority, and
disturbance structure. They remain constructed tasks; strong performance indicates diagnostic
capability under certified dynamics, not deployment readiness.
41


What each extension covers, and what it does not.
Extension I (Section 6) shows that
the certificate survives a nonlinear raw-action coordinate and a non-Gaussian finite-covariance
disturbance law.
What it does not cover is state-dependent, heteroscedastic, or temporally
correlated noise, all of which would break the moment-only argument behind (22). The reported
ConverseQuad results are a single configuration in η and ϵ; a systematic sweep over actuator
nonlinearity and mixture weight is left to future work.
Extension II (Section 7) gives a viscosity–Clarke certificate for value functions that are locally
Lipschitz but not differentiable. The theory is continuous-time; the numerical experiments of
Section 7.12 use an explicit Euler discretization, which inherits the discretization confound of
Mode I. A native discrete-time non-smooth construction would eliminate this confound, but
the discrete-time analogue of the viscosity inequality is not standard and is left open.
The
supersolution condition couples the gain matrix K to the geometry of the Clarke subdifferential,
and the scalar-gain version requires the denominator (38) to be positive.
Extension III (Section 8) constructs a finite-dimensional information-state benchmark whose
optimal output-feedback policy is certified in the learner’s own information set. The construction
does not solve arbitrary nonlinear continuous-state POMDPs. For a general hidden-state model,
the belief density is infinite-dimensional, and the value function is a functional on densities
rather than a matrix on Rd. The innovation realization of Proposition 8.4.1 is exact but not
physically natural: it is a deliberately designed benchmark whose sufficient statistic is known
by construction, not a solution to the general partial-observability problem.
10
Conclusion
We presented a ground-truth-first framework for reinforcement learning benchmarking based on
converse optimality. The central idea is to make the optimum part of the benchmark specification
rather than an unknown target outside it. This supports two complementary modes: continuous-
time generators for sensitivity and robustness studies, and native discrete-time fixtures for oracle-
referenced comparison by optimality gap, regret, Bellman residual, and policy error. Continuous-
time and discrete-time generators play distinct roles: the former provide physically interpretable
probes but inherit simulator-step dependence, while the latter remove timestep ambiguity for
absolute comparison.
The paper combines a review of the converse-optimality lineage with three extensions of
the certificate logic. Extension I extends the construction to non-affine raw actions and finite-
covariance non-Gaussian disturbances, with the ConverseQuad aerial family as the concrete
instantiation. Extension II replaces the smooth HJB certificate with a viscosity–Clarke con-
dition, enabling certified benchmarks with kinked value functions and switching surfaces, and
reports a discrete-time instantiation on a piecewise-quadratic family. Extension III reduces par-
tially observed evaluation to converse optimality on a sufficient information state, certifies the
optimal output-feedback policy in the learner’s information class, and supplies an analytic LQG
information penalty that separates learning error from information loss.

Impact Statement
This work aims to improve the reliability and interpretability of RL experimentation. Its main
positive impact is methodological: it can help researchers distinguish relative performance from
distance to optimality and design more diagnostic experiments. Potential negative impact is
limited but not absent. If known-answer fixtures are overused as narrow leaderboards, they could
encourage overfitting to generated families or create unwarranted confidence about deployment
readiness. We therefore frame the method as a complement to broader benchmarks and real-
world validation, not a substitute for them.
