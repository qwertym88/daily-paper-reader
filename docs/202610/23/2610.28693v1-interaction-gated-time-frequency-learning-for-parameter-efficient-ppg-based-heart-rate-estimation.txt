Abstract. Photoplethysmography (PPG) enables low-cost and non-invasive heart rate (HR) monitoring but remains sensitive to waveform distortion, physiologi-cal variability, and measurement interference. Temporal waveform characteris-tics and frequency-domain periodicity provide complementary information for HR estimation; however, effectively exploring and integrating these representa-tions within a compact model remains challenging. This paper proposes TF-IG ,a parameter-efficient interaction-gated time–frequency learning framework for PPG-based HR estimation. TF-IG employs a dual-branch architecture in which a temporal branch captures pulse morphology via dilated depthwise-separable con-volutions, while a frequency branch extracts cardiac periodicity information from bounded log-spectrogram representations. To combine the two domains, we intro-duce an interaction-gated fusion module that explicitly models feature agreement and discrepancy, while also learning feature-wise weights to balance temporal and spectral information. A parameter-shared regression head further maps the temporal, spectral, and fused representations into the final HR prediction space. Experiments on the benchmark BIDMC PPG and Respiration Dataset using a subject-exclusive training, validation, and test protocol show that TF-IG achieves an MAE of 1 .227 BPM, a RMSE of 2 .060 BPM, Pearson correlation of 0 .9934, and 92 .28% of estimates within 3 BPM, while remaining parameter-efficient with only 84.6K parameters. Ablation results further demonstrate the comple-mentary contributions of the temporal and frequency branches, interaction-gated fusion, and shared regression head. These results indicate that explicitly modeling cross-domain interactions provides an effective accuracy–complexity trade-off for lightweight PPG-based HR estimation. 

Keywords — PPG, HR Estimation, BIDMC PPG, Time-Frequency, Feature inter-action. 

## 1 Introduction 

Continuous heart-rate (HR) monitoring plays an important role in clinical observa-tion, fitness assessment, ambulatory healthcare and long-term physiological monitor-ing [13], [1]. Although electrocardiography (ECG) remains a standard reference for

cardiac monitoring, photoplethysmography (PPG) provides an attractive alternative for continuous and wearable sensing because it measures peripheral blood-volume varia-tions using inexpensive and non-invasive optical hardware [2], [9]. Its compact sensing configuration has consequently enabled widespread integration into wearable devices, pulse oximeters, smartphones, and other resource-constrained monitoring platforms. PPG therefore represents a practical sensing modality for scalable and continuous car-diovascular monitoring [2], [1]. Despite these advantages, reliable HR estimation from PPG remains challenging. The measured waveform is influenced not only by cardiac pulsation but also by physiological and acquisition-related factors such as respiration, body site, tissue characteristics, ambient illumination, applied pressure, and artifacts [4]. A broad range of signal-processing and learning-based approaches has therefore been developed for PPG-based HR estimation. Classical methods typically rely on filtering, peak detection, spectral analysis, or handcrafted signal descriptors [12], [6]. More recently, deep learning has enabled HR-relevant features to be learned directly from PPG observations rather than relying entirely on manually designed processing rules. For example, PPGNet [17] proposed an end-to-end network operating directly on short PPG segments for device-independent HR estimation; Deep PPG [15] leverages convolutional neural networks over spectrogram of PPG and accelerometer signals; Ismail et al. [7] further explored a set of features characterizing the signal, modeling by a convolutional-recurrent framework, etc. These studies demonstrates that temporal and spectral representations of PPG can provide useful information for HR estimation. The complementarity of temporal and spectral domain follows naturally from the characteristics of PPG. The temporal domain preserves local pulse morphology and short-term waveform dynamics, whereas the frequency domain makes periodic cardiac activity more explicit. Time-frequency representations further characterize how this spectral structure evolves over the observation interval, emphasizing different aspects of the same underlying physiological process [7]. Although prior work has demonstrated the utility of signal, spectral, and time-frequency representations, effectively structuring the interaction between complementary representations remains an important design problem. The availability of multiple representations does not by itself determine how they should be combined [5]. Under signal variability or corruption, temporal and spectral features may provide partly consistent yet partly different evidence about the underlying HR. Therefore, an effective fusion mechanism should explicitly characterize where they agree, where they differ, and how strongly each should jointly contribute to the final representation. Motivated by these observations, we propose TF-IG , a parameter-efficient Interaction-Gated Time–Frequency learning framework for PPG-based HR estimation. First, TF-IG 

learns complementary HR representations through two lightweight branches: a temporal branch based on dilated depthwise-separable convolutions and temporal attention pool-ing, and a frequency branch that processes a bounded log-spectrogram using spectro-temporal attention pooling. Second, these representations are integrated through a struc-tured interaction-gated mechanism. Element-wise multiplication and absolute difference explicitly encode cross-domain co-activation and discrepancy, while a feature-wise gate adaptively balances the temporal and spectral embeddings. The resulting fused repre-sentation, together with the individual branch embeddings, is processed by a shared Interaction-Gated Time–Frequency Learning for PPG HR Estimation 3

regression head that provides a common mapping to HR, with the three predictions subsequently combined through learned normalized weights. In this way, TF-IG jointly addresses complementary representation learning, explicit cross-domain interaction, and parameter-efficient prediction within a unified lightweight framework. Accordingly, the main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 

– Compact dual-domain representation learning. We propose a parameter-efficient dual-branch architecture that jointly captures temporal pulse morphology and spectro-temporal cardiac periodicity using dilated depthwise-separable convolutions and attention-based pooling. 

– Structured cross-domain fusion and shared prediction. We introduce an interaction-gated fusion mechanism that explicitly models temporal–spectral co-activation and discrepancy through element-wise multiplication and absolute difference, while a feature-wise gate adaptively balances the contribution of the two domains. A parameter-shared regression head is further applied to the temporal, frequency, and fused embeddings, providing a common space for HR prediction mapping while avoiding separate prediction networks. 

– Empirical validation of the proposed design. On a subject-independent BIDMC split, TF-IG achieves an MAE of 1 .227 BPM, an RMSE of 2 .060 BPM, a Pearson correlation of 0 .9934, and a CP 3 of 92 .28% using only 84.6K parameters. Ablation experiments further demonstrate the complementary contributions of dual-domain representation learning, interaction-gated fusion, and shared regression to the final performance.

2.2 Overall Architecture 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, TF-IG adopts two complementary processing branches. The time-domain branch operates directly on the PPG waveform and captures local pulse morphology and temporal dependencies. In parallel, the frequency-domain branch trans-forms the waveform into a bounded log-magnitude spectrogram and models cardiac periodicity over time-frequency transformed representation. To reduce the computational operations, both streams use depthwise-separable con-volutions [3] as their primary building block. Both branches share a structured pro-cessing flow comprising a convolutional stem, domain-specific feature refinement, and targeted attention pooling. Their outputs are converted into fixed-dimensional embed-dings through domain-specific attention pooling. An interaction-gated fusion module then combines these embeddings by jointly modeling their agreement, discrepancy, and relative contribution. Finally, a shared regression head generates predictions from the time-domain, frequency-domain, and fused representations, which are combined through a learnable ensemble.

## 3 Experimental Settings 

3.1 Dataset and Evaluation Metrics 

The experiments were conducted on the BIDMC PPG and Respiration Dataset (BIDMC), which contains physiological waveforms, including PPG, together with corresponding vital-sign numerics derived from recordings in the MIMIC-II database [14]. To prevent subject-level information leakage and evaluate generalization, we adopted a subject-exclusive split with a fixed random seed of 36. Specifically, the 53 subjects in the dataset were partitioned into a training set of 31 subjects, a validation set of 11 subjects for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping, and a held-out test set of 11 subjects for final evaluation. Model performance was evaluated using six complementary metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Pearson correlation coefficient ( 𝑟 ), and the coverage percentages CP 3 and CP 5. The first three metrics quantify the magnitude of HR estimation errors, while 𝑟 measures the linear agreement between estimated and reference HR values. In addition, CP 𝑘 denotes the percentage of predictions whose absolute error is within 𝑘 BPM of the reference value. 

3.2 Implementation Details Training Setup and Hyperparameters: The proposed model was implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 GPU. Continuous PPG signals were windowed into 30-second segments with a 2-second shift between adjacent frames. All models were trained for 150 epochs using the Adam optimizer [10] with a batch size of 128, an initial learning rate of 𝜂 = 0.007, and a weight decay coefficient of 10 −4. The hidden channel 𝐶 is set to 64. To facilitate stable convergence, ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler was employed to scale the learning rate by a factor of 0 .5 whenever the validation loss stagnated for 8 consecutive epochs. To maintain reproducible evaluation protocol across all experiment runs, the global random seed was fixed to 36. The detail code implementation will be released upon acceptance. ## 4 Results and Discussion 

4.1 Main Performance Comparison 

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed TF-IG architecture, we conducted com-parative experiments against both a conventional signal-processing baseline, which estimate heart rate from the dominant spectral component of the PPG signal, refered to as Welch PSD [11], and three learning-based PPG baselines: FCN, FCN_Residual, and FCN_DCT [16]. All methods were evaluated using the same preprocessing pipeline and subject-level split of the BIDMC dataset to ensure a fair comparison. Table 1 summarizes the performance of all methods across six evaluation metrics. As shown in Table 1, TF-IG achieved strong performance across nearly all metrics, with an MAE of 1 .2270,BPM, an RMSE of 2 .0601,BPM, and a Pearson correlation coef-ficient of 𝑟 = 0.9934. Compared with the Welch PSD baseline, TF-IG produced a slightly higher MAE but substantially lower RMSE and MSE, indicating fewer large estimation errors and a more concentrated overall error distribution. This is further reflected in the strict accuracy metric, where TF-IG achieved 𝐶𝑃 3 = 92 .28%, compared with 90 .79% for Welch PSD. Moreover, TF-IG substantially outperformed all learning-based base-lines in terms of MAE, RMSE, correlation, and 𝐶𝑃 3. In particular, its 𝐶𝑃 3 exceeded the strongest learning-based baseline, FCN_Residual by 4 .83 percentage points.

4.3 Ablation Study 

To evaluate the contribution of each individual component in the proposed TF-IG 

architecture, we conduct an ablation study across systematically isolated variants. The quantitative results are summarized in Table 2. The single-branch variants demonstrate that both temporal and spectral representa-tions can independently support HR estimation, although neither is sufficient to achieve the performance of the complete model. The time-only variant obtains lower overall errors than the frequency-only variant, with an MAE of 2 .1677 BPM and an RMSE of 3.3984 BPM, compared with 2 .3470 BPM and 4 .5638 BPM, respectively. In contrast, the frequency-only variant achieves higher 𝐶𝑃 3 and 𝐶𝑃 5 values, suggesting that the two branches capture different but complementary characteristics of the PPG signal. Combining the time and frequency branches without the interaction-gated fusion module substantially improves performance over both single-branch configurations, re-ducing the MAE to 1 .5878 BPM and increasing the Pearson correlation to 0 .9925. This result confirms that jointly exploiting waveform morphology and spectral periodicity provides a more informative representation than relying on either domain alone. Incorpo-rating the proposed fusion module further reduces the MAE and RMSE to 1 .2270 BPM and 2 .0601 BPM, respectively, while increasing 𝐶𝑃 3 from 87 .17% to 92 .28%. These im-provements indicate that explicitly modeling cross-domain interactions and adaptively balancing the two representations significantly improve model performance. The shared regression head also contributes to the final performance. Replacing it with three distinct prediction heads increases the MAE from 1 .2270 to 1 .5020 BPM and decreases 𝐶𝑃 3 from 92 .28% to 84 .75%. Overall, the full TF-IG configuration achieves the best result across all reported metrics, validating the complementary contributions of the dual-branch design, interaction-gated fusion, and parameter-shared regression head. 4.4 Window Size Sensitivity Analysis 

To investigate the effect of the observation duration on HR estimation, we evaluated 

TF-IG using raw PPG windows of 16 and 30 seconds. Here, the window size refers to the duration of each raw signal segment before resampling; after segmentation, both configurations were processed using the same resampling pipeline and experimental protocol described above. The results are reported in Table 3. Increasing the raw window duration from 16 to 30 seconds consistently improves performance across all evaluation metrics. In particular, the MAE decreases from 1 .9331 to 1 .2270 BPM, while the RMSE decreases from 3 .2854 to 2 .0601 BPM. The longer window also increases 𝐶𝑃 3 and 𝐶𝑃 5 by 9 .72 and 6 .40 percentage points, respectively, and raises the Pearson correlation from 0 .9856 to 0 .9934. These results suggest that the 30-second raw window provides the model with a broader temporal context and a larger number of cardiac cycles, supporting more stable extraction of waveform morphology and spectral periodicity after resampling. Conse-quently, the 30-second configuration is adopted for the main experiments. Nevertheless, the 16-second setting remains a potential alternative when a shorter observation interval is preferred, albeit with reduced estimation accuracy.

Points were colored according to five reference-HR ranges: < 60, 60–80, 80–100, 100–120, and ≥ 120 BPM with their corresponding number of windows belong to that range. Overall, the visualization supports the com-plementary roles of the temporal and frequency branches and the effectiveness of their fusion. 

## 5 Conclusion 

This work introduced TF-IG , a parameter-efficient framework for PPG-based heart-rate estimation that jointly models temporal morphology and spectral periodicity. Its interaction-gated fusion captures cross-domain agreement and discrepancy while adap-tively balancing their contributions, and a shared regression head provides a com-pact common mapping to the final HR prediction. Evaluation on the subject-exclusive BIDMC split demonstrates that TF-IG consistently outperforms the evaluated baselines across most of error-based, correlation, and strict accuracy metrics, while maintaining a compact parameter footprint. Ablation studies further validate the complementary roles of dual-domain representation learning, interaction-gated fusion, and shared regression in improving estimation performance. Overall, these results demonstrate that explic-itly modeling temporal–spectral interactions provides an effective accuracy–complexity trade-off for efficient PPG-based HR estimation.
