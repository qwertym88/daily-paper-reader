## ABSTRACT 

Recent advances in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs) have expanded the manipulation and whole body capabilities of individual robots. Yet a single robot remains limited by its embodiment, and many real world tasks require capabilities that can only emerge through cooperation. Ex-isting multi robot frameworks largely emphasize task decomposition, allocation, and replanning, while providing limited support for adapting executable coordi-nation when physical execution deviates from the intended behavior. We present 

CoEvolve , a framework that represents team coordination as executable code over fixed robot motor skills. CoEvolve introduces Verifier Guided Coordina-tion Evolution, where a multimodal foundation model instantiates coordination strategies through motion primitives, semantic objectives, temporary robot roles, and bounded execution parameters. Physical verification evaluates each executed transition, rejected behaviors trigger structured coordination revision from an ac-cepted checkpoint, and verified experience is retained to guide subsequent deci-sions. We evaluate CoEvolve with two simulated Unitree G1 humanoids trans-porting a freely supported rigid payload through constrained corridors. Across 15 base task runs with three multimodal backbone families, CoEvolve completes 8 tasks, with every backbone achieving end to end success. Additional evalua-tions yield verified placement on two additional corridor layouts and after both same-backbone and cross-backbone experience initialization, while exposing per-sistent limitations in motion precision and contact stability. These results support executable coordination as a practical interface for adapting long horizon multi robot collaboration under shared physical constraints. Our code will be available at https://github.com/Innovationlzc/CoEvolve .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in robot foundation models have equipped individual robots with increasingly gen-eral visuomotor capabilities, enabling them to follow language instructions and perform diverse ma-nipulation tasks (Brohan et al., 2022; 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024). Yet some physical tasks require capabilities that cannot be provided by one robot alone. Transporting an extended ob-ject through a constrained environment, for example, requires coordinated support, relative motion, corner negotiation, and placement (De Vincenti & Coros, 2023; Pandit et al., 2025). Collaboration is therefore not simply the aggregation of individual skills. Robots that can independently walk, turn, and move laterally must still determine how to combine these capabilities while carrying a shared object. We view this formation of team behavior as a form of co-becoming : effective cooperation is realized through the changing relationship between individual capabilities and the shared physical state. This motivates our central question: given fixed individual motor skills, how can an agent instantiate and revise executable team coordination as a shared physical task unfolds? CoEvolve sepa-rates fixed individual motor capabilities from the coordination strategy that composes them for the current task phase. Revised policies are evaluated after restoration of the latest accepted simulator checkpoint. Verified outcomes also contribute complementary forms of experience: abstract coordination records guide subsequent de-cisions, while accepted executable examples inform code generation. Foundation model and motor policy weights remain fixed. The adaptation concerns the task plan, instantiated coordination strate-gies, and stored experience rather than the acquisition of new motor skills. The base evaluation contains eight complete tasks across fif-teen runs and three foundation model families. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Self-Evolving Agents. Self-evolving agents adapt multiple components of an agent system. Build-ing on feedback accumulation and skill reuse in Reflexion and Voyager (Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), SkillOpt optimizes textual skills for frozen agents, while MemEvolve evolves the mem-ory architecture itself (Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). Continual Harness adapts prompts, sub-agents, skills, and memory within an ongoing episode, and Hyperagents additionally makes the improvement procedure editable (Karten et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c). Related systems auto-mate the design or modification of the agent itself, including Automated Design of Agentic Systems and the Darwin G¨ odel Machine (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b). Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) enables experience sharing among evolving coding agents, whereas CORAL supports au-tonomous multi-agent search through shared persistent memory (Weng et al., 2026; Qu et al., 2026). These approaches concern agent implementations, knowledge, or search processes. CoEvolve in-stead studies online revision of executable inter-robot coordination during a shared physical task, with motor skills and runtime contracts fixed. 

Robot Policy Refinement from Execution Feedback. REMAC revises multi-robot task plans through reflection, while HyCodePolicy and Act-Observe-Rewrite revise executable robot programs from execution feedback (Yuan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025b; Kumar, 2026). ASPIRE com-bines multimodal failure diagnosis, program repair, and reusable skill discovery, including biman-ual manipulation; ENPIRE automates policy improvement through real-robot experimentation and reset (Lu et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026). These works already connect program revision with phys-ical experience. ## 3 METHOD 

Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of CoEvolve , from semantic coordination decisions to executable policies, physical verification, and experience-driven revision. 3.1 OVERVIEW AND COORDINATION REPRESENTATION 

Task formulation. CoEvolve separates fixed individual motor skills from mutable team co-ordination. For N robots manipulating a shared payload, a persistent semantic plan Pt =[( qk, γ k, σ k,t )] Kk=1 records task phases, completion criteria, and status. The host provides route-segment geometry and progress constraints, while the model determines how the robots coordinate within those constraints. State, observation, and route interfaces are detailed in Appendix A.1. 

Executable coordination. We use executable code as the coordination interface, following the Code as Policies paradigm (Liang et al., 2023), but generate paired robot policies under a shared coordination decision. Above the fixed motor interface, CoEvolve exposes coordinated linear transport, pivot, and alignment. The Task Orchestrator commits zt = ( κt, d t, m t, ρ 1: Nt ), where

These variables denote the coordination fields of the committed subtask; its structured representation also carries formation intent, completion evidence, and execution constraints. CoEvolve maintains two dynamic stores, Mt = ( MDt , MAt ). Strategy memory MDt stores abstract coordination knowledge, while action exemplar memory MAt stores accepted executable implementations. Strategy records capture applicability conditions, observed failure patterns, and repair directions, whereas action exemplars retain executable code and effective parameters. Before orchestration, strategy records are retrieved using the active phase qt and recent failure con-text ξt as rDt = RetrieveStrategy( MDt ; qt, ξ t). After the Orchestrator commits (κt, d t, m t), com-patible action exemplars are retrieved as rAt = RetrieveExemplar( MAt ; κt, d t, m t). ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTING 

Task and observations. We evaluate CoEvolve on cooperative payload transport in Mu-JoCo (Todorov et al., 2012). Two Unitree G1 humanoids jointly carry a freely supported 0.5 kg 

rigid payload through constrained corridors. The task requires coordinated transport, corner negoti-ation, target placement, and release. The base task additionally requires both robots to retreat while remaining upright. The model receives simulator measurements, global and payload-centered observations, the active task phase, and retrieved experience when enabled. The host provides route-segment geometry and 5Preprint progress constraints. The model selects the coordination primitive, temporary roles, coordination mode, and bounded execution parameters within these constraints. 

Execution and models. Each robot uses an independent SONIC deployment with frozen policy weights (Luo et al., 2026), together with task-specific skill wrappers and support posture control. The host validates paired robot policies as one team transaction and dispatches their planner com-mands. The controllers retain independent observation histories and execution threads, so joint dispatch does not imply identical physical responses. We evaluate GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini-3.5-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6. One backbone performs all semantic roles within a run. Except for experience reuse experiments, dynamic memory starts empty and updates remain local to that run. 4.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOL AND METRICS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We propose CoEvolve , a framework for adapting executable multi-robot coordination over fixed motor skills through physical verification, structured revision, and verified experience. Across three foundation model families, CoEvolve achieves end-to-end cooperative transport, while additional evaluations show successful execution under new layouts and same-model and cross-model experi-ence reuse. The experiments also separate local execution validity from sustained task advancement: accepted actions can fail to resolve the current coordination bottleneck, and verified progress can precede later loss of physical support. These findings identify structured coordination revision as a useful interface for long-horizon cooperation while leaving motion precision, contact stability, and physical recovery as open limitations. 9Preprint 

## 6 LIMITATIONS 

CoEvolve has limitations. First, end-to-end execution incurs substantial wall-clock cost be-cause model inference, physical execution, verification, and recovery recur throughout a run (Ap-pendix C.4). Second, rejected executions are recovered by restoring an accepted simulator state rather than a physically executable recovery policy, which limits direct transfer to real robots. Third, the frozen motor interface provides bounded motion intentions rather than precise endpoints: un-equal robot responses, residual motion after stopping, and accumulated formation drift can reduce clearance and destabilize the shared payload. Payload stability also depends on finite contact re-gions, friction, and simulator contact dynamics, which we do not systematically randomize in the study. Finally, all experiments use simulation, privileged physical state information, and supplied route references. Real-world deployment will require perception from onboard sensing, more accu-rate motion and contact models, safe physical recovery, and evaluation under broader environments and hardware conditions. The relatively small and heterogeneous evaluation cohorts also limit strong causal claims about individual components, scene generalization, and experience transfer.

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work studies multi-robot coordination entirely in simulation and does not involve human par-ticipants, personal data, or deployment on physical robots. The proposed framework is intended for research on cooperative robot reasoning and execution. Although physical verification and recov-ery are used within the simulator, they should not be interpreted as safety guarantees for real-world deployment. Applying the system to physical robots would require additional safeguards, state and contact estimation, calibrated motion control, and hardware-specific safety validation.

A.1 STATE , P LAN , AND ROLE INTERFACES 

The state st = ( x1: Nt , x ot , c t, G) contains robot states, payload state, contact and support measure-ments, and scene geometry. Observations combine serialized measurements, global and payload-centered views, and geometric references for the active route segment. These references may include world directions, local progress targets, corridor boundaries, and completion gates. They are distinct from the semantic plan Pt = [( qk, γ k, σ k,t )] Kk=1 , which records task phases, completion evidence, and status rather than an executable waypoint sequence. The tuple zt = ( κt, d t, m t, ρ 1: Nt ) denotes the coordination fields of the committed subtask. Its structured representation additionally contains formation intent, completion evidence, and execution constraints. Acceptance depends on measured payload support and stability, robot state, environmental contacts, phase validity, and progress relative to the committed objective. Visual interpretation assists diagno-sis but cannot override contradictory physical measurements. An accepted setup or corrective action can establish a checkpoint without completing a task phase; phase advancement requires separate completion evidence. Revision acts on the failed strategy χt = ( κt, d t, m t, ρ 1: Nt , θ 1: Nt ). Strategy revision concerns the maneuver and objective, coordination revision concerns mode and temporary roles, and parameter revision concerns bounded numerical arguments. The host checks the child against its failed parent and the selected mutable fields. Once approved, that revision constrains the next orchestration and code-generation step rather than being discarded by an unrelated decision. After rejection, the host restores the latest accepted simulator state, clears transient execution state, resynchronizes the controllers, and verifies the restoration. A fingerprint of the checkpoint, normal-ized policy, and effective arguments blocks identical rejected transactions at that checkpoint. Recov-ery neither physically reverses the motion nor automatically searches earlier checkpoints. Separately selected diagnostic restart points are evaluation conditions, not autonomous recovery actions. Algorithm 1 summarizes this loop. Its model procedures include bounded validation and repair; an inability to produce a valid next transaction can terminate execution before the physical budget is ex-hausted. Only dispatched physical transactions consume that budget. Execution attempt identifiers can therefore differ from cumulative transaction counts. A.4 EXPERIENCE CONTENTS AND RETRIEVAL 

The two dynamic stores are written as Mt = ( MDt , MAt ). The static skill catalog belongs to the capability interface and does not evolve. Strategy records store applicability conditions, coordina-tion principles, observed failure patterns, and repair directions. The active phase filters applicable records, while recent failure type and level contribute to ranking. The next primitive, objective, and mode are not query conditions because they have not yet been selected. After the coordination decision is committed, action exemplars are matched by primitive, semantic objective, and coordination mode. Phase only ranks otherwise compatible exemplars. These entries retain accepted code and effective parameters but remain subject to the current subtask and execu-tion constraints. Strategy retrieval therefore informs the coordination decision; exemplar retrieval informs its implementation. Promotion requires agreement between a record’s claim, the executed behavior, and the verifier outcome. Eligible accepted executions can yield positive strategy records and executable exem-plars; rejected executions can yield counterexamples. Unexecuted repairs are not successful experi-ence. Strategy records omit scene coordinates, checkpoint poses, and numerical action magnitudes, whereas exemplars retain concrete implementations. Standard fresh runs start with empty dynamic memory; transfer runs preload a fixed source bank and update only their own subsequent experience. A.5 REPRESENTATIVE COORDINATION REVISION
