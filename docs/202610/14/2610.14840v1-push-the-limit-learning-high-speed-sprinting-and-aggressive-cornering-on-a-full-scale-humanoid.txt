Abstract: We present a learning-based framework that enables a full-scale bipedal humanoid to sprint at over 7 m /s with motion-prior-guided gait and perform ag-gressive cornering at high speed with a pronounced trunk lean of up to 25 ◦.Achieving this combination is challenging: above approximately 4 m/s, periodic foot impacts induce severe, high-frequency yaw-rate oscillations intrinsic to high-speed gaits. This high-frequency temporal structure corrupts the history of pro-prioceptive feedback that flat-MLP policies rely on, while rendering conventional yaw-rate tracking rewards counterproductive, as they fight against the robot’s natu-ral oscillatory dynamics. Moreover, a single style prior shared across skills cannot tell which skill is commanded and inherently compromises the low-speed skill fidelity. To overcome these limitations, we adopt a causal multi-head attention (MHA) module to explicitly model the high-frequency temporal feedback. In place of the oscillatory yaw rate, we design a heading-direction reward that su-pervises only the low-frequency velocity direction, allowing agile turning at high speed without suppressing natural gait oscillations. ## 1 Introduction 

Bipedal robots can now sprint in a straight line at speeds exceeding those of human athletes, yet the resulting gaits bear little resemblance to human running—arms flail, torsos remain rigidly up-right, and high-speed turning is absent from published demonstrations. Conversely, learning-based controllers that leverage adversarial motion priors produce convincingly human-like movement, but only at moderate speeds and with limited agility [1, 2, 3]. The combination that matters for real-world deployment—sustained sprinting with natural stride dynamics and agile cornering—remains undemonstrated on physical hardware. Achieving this combination is a system-level stress test that simultaneously exposes weaknesses in state estimation, motion-style supervision, and reward de-sign, weaknesses that remain hidden at walking pace. Three specific failure modes emerge when a learned bipedal controller is pushed into the high-speed regime. (i) Above approximately 4 m /s, the proprioceptive stream is dominated by high-frequency oscillations from alternating leg impacts; a standard MLP consuming a flat observation history can-not extract the temporal structure—relative phase, contact timing, acceleration profiles—needed for stable state estimation at these frequencies. (ii) The conventional yaw-rate tracking reward breaks down at sprinting speeds: alternating leg strides force the torso to oscillate in yaw, making instan-taneous yaw rate a noisy mixture of the true turning signal and gait-induced oscillation. The policy either slows down to produce a cleaner signal or abandons the turning objective altogether, failing to achieve effective yaw-rate command tracking at sprinting speeds. (iii) When multiple locomo-tion skills (standing, turning, walking, sprinting) share a single adversarial discriminator trained on the mixed reference dataset, the discriminator rates whether a motion resembles any reference skill, not whether it matches the commanded one. A skill that the task reward barely distinguishes from standing then receives almost no learning signal; in our setting the policy loses the ability to turn in place—a mode collapse across the skill space. Here we present a learning-based locomotion control framework that addresses each failure mode through a targeted design choice: (i) a causal multi-head attention (MHA) history encoder that replaces the flat-history MLP, extracting a compact dynamics representation from the propriocep-tive window without increasing network capacity; (ii) a heading-direction reward that supervises the low-frequency velocity direction rather than the high-frequency instantaneous yaw rate, restor-ing a usable turning signal at full sprint; and (iii) a skill-conditioned Multi-AMP framework whose per-skill discriminators condition the style reward on the commanded skill, preventing mode col-lapse across the skill set. The resulting policy is deployed zero-shot on a physical 17 -DOF bipedal humanoid; quantitative evaluation in Gazebo simulation confirms 6.5 m /s velocity tracking and ag-gressive cornering at high speed (Figure 1,4), while a timed 100 m hardware sprint confirms an average speed of 7.14 m /s (Sec. 4.6). Ablation experiments isolate each component: structured temporal encoding is necessary for high-speed tracking, and the MHA encoder further enables sta-ble aggressive cornering where GRU, LSTM, and learned-estimator alternatives fail (E1); replac-ing heading-direction with yaw-rate tracking eliminates agile high-speed turning (E2); and a single shared discriminator causes in-place turning to collapse (E3). We validate all claims through stan-dardized step-speed and turning test protocols in both simulation and real-robot deployment. 22 Related Work 

Adversarial motion priors. AMP [1] introduced a GAIL-style discriminator that provides a unified style reward from unstructured motion clips, removing the need for explicit clip selection required by tracking-based methods such as DeepMimic [4]. WASABI [5] adopted a Wasserstein formulation to learn agile skills from rough, partial demonstrations, and HumanMimic [6] applied Wasserstein adversarial imitation to natural humanoid locomotion and gait transitions. Multi-AMP [7] extends this to one discriminator per motion style for quadrupeds, so that each skill receives its own style reward, but has not been applied to regimes spanning standing through high-speed sprinting with policy-side skill conditioning. 

Attention-based history encoders. Most locomotion controllers consume flat proprioceptive his-tories via an MLP [8, 9] or decouple history processing into an auxiliary estimator [10]. More broadly, Transformer architectures have shown promise for sequential decision-making in RL [11] and embodiment-aware policy learning [12], motivating their adoption in locomotion control. Ma et al. [13] demonstrated that causal self-attention over proprioceptive history substantially improves humanoid motion tracking, using cross-attention to fuse high-dimensional reference trajectories. 

High-speed bipedal locomotion. Sim-to-real RL for bipeds has progressed from periodic gait composition [14] to versatile dynamic control encompassing walking, running, and jumping on Cassie [15]. On the quadruped side, Margolis et al. [16] demonstrated 3.9 m /s sprinting via model-free RL. For humanoids, Radosavovic et al. [3] achieved robust outdoor walking with a causal Transformer policy, and denoising world-model learning extended blind locomotion to challeng-ing terrains [17]. Vision-based parkour policies traverse obstacles such as platforms, hurdles and gaps [18], and unified whole-body controllers command versatile gaits with fine-grained parameters such as step frequency and foot height [19]. In parallel, Cheng et al. [20] demonstrated expressive whole-body motion tracking, Fu et al. [21] enabled humanoid teleoperation through human motion shadowing, ASAP [22] reduced the sim-to-real dynamics gap for agile whole-body skills, and Liao et al. [23] introduced a lightweight humanoid research platform for learning-based control. These works target either moderate speed with high robustness, or full-body motion tracking at low to moderate speeds; none addresses the coupled failure modes—temporal state estimation, turning re-ward corruption, and multi-skill style collapse—specific to sustained high-speed bipedal sprinting that we identify in Sec. 1.

3.2 Multi-Head Attention Actor 

To address the flat-history limitation described in Sec. 1, we adopt the causal self-attention history encoder of Ma et al. [13], which extracts a compact dynamics representation from proprioceptive history via multi-head self-attention. We simplify the command interface: the cross-attention en-coder in Ma et al. [13] targets high-dimensional reference trajectories ( R38 per step); our velocity-plus-skill command ( R7) is low-dimensional, so we replace cross-attention with MLP fusion. The encoder processes T proprioceptive frames through a single-layer, two-head causal Transformer, average-pools across time to produce a dynamics summary, and fuses it with the embedded com-mand; the result is passed to the actor MLP to produce joint-position targets (Figure 2; architectural details in Appendix D). The MHA actor totals 3.6% fewer parameters than the MLP baseline consuming the same history window, so any performance gain reflects the structural inductive bias of temporal attention rather than added capacity. In practice, the MHA design yields substantially stronger speed generalization and high-speed turning stability than the MLP baseline (Sec. 4.3). 43.3 Heading-Direction Reward

3.4 Skill-Conditioned Multi-AMP Discriminator 

To counter the mode collapse described in Sec. 1, we follow Vollenweider et al. [7] and instantiate 

K=4 skill-specific discriminators {Di}Ki=1 , each with its own reference dataset Ri and policy replay buffer Biπ . For a transition emitted under active skill i, only Di updates and only Di provides the style reward. This routing makes the style reward conditional on the commanded skill, so that producing the motion of another skill is penalized (Sec. 4.5). The multi-discriminator structure follows Vollenweider et al. [7]; we employ a Wasserstein GAN di-vergence for the discriminator loss, which replaces AMP’s original least-squares loss with a Wasser-stein distance that maintains informative gradients even when policy and reference distributions are far apart. We introduce three stabilizing modifications for the high-speed bipedal regime: (i) a tanh 

output bound tanh( α x ) that prevents gradient explosion from unbounded discriminator scores; (ii) a non-negative reward mapping ρ(x) = exp(tanh( α x )) − e−1 that eliminates negative style rewards—without this, the policy learns to terminate episodes early to avoid accumulating negative style penalty; and (iii) gradient penalties on both expert and policy samples ( R1 + R2 regulariza-tion), which smooth the discriminator’s decision boundary across the full motion manifold rather than only near the expert distribution. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate the full pipeline and each of its three components in simulation and validate the full pipeline through real-robot deployment on a 17 -DOF bipedal humanoid. All policies share the same domain-randomization and training hyperparameters described in Sec. 3.5; only the ablated component differs.

4.2 Main Results 

In simulation, the full pipeline tracks the 6.5 m /s step command with a steady-state error of approx-imately 0.1 m /s and sustains stable cornering at full speed. The trunk lean angle during cornering depends on the centripetal acceleration ac = v · | ωz |: at v=5 .5 m /s with a larger commanded yaw rate, the policy achieves a maximum lean exceeding 25 ◦ (confirmed in both simulation and real-robot deployment; Figure 1); at v=6 .5 m /s with ωz =0 .35 rad /s (the standardized turning test, Figure 4), the lean reaches 13 ◦ with a turning radius of 24 .2 m . Both regimes exhibit the emergent trunk-lean behavior characteristic of the heading-direction reward. On hardware, a timed 100 m 

sprint yields 7.14 m /s average speed (Sec. 4.6). All ablation comparisons below are simulation results under identical conditions per pair of policies. 

4.3 Effect of History Encoder Architecture (E1) 

All encoder variants are trained with the same velocity curriculum ( 5.5 m /s), reward, and Multi-AMP configuration; only the history encoder differs. On the 6.5 m /s step test (Figure 3a), MHA, GRU, LSTM, and HIM all track the target stably—generalizing 18% beyond the curriculum limit— while the flat-history MLP saturates near 6.0 m /s. This indicates that high-speed straight-line tracking is enabled by structured temporal encoding in general , rather than by any single ar-chitecture; the framework’s reward design and speed curriculum are the primary contributors. A history-length sweep over 5–20 frames showed negligible performance variation. However, the architectures diverge sharply in cornering (Figure 3b). Under a (6 .5, 0, 0.35) com-mand, MHA achieves a turning radius of 24 .2 m with pronounced trunk lean; LSTM and GRU require 1.8× and 2.2× larger radii, respectively; the HIM baseline falls outright. MHA’s advan-tage concentrates in dynamic stability during aggressive maneuvers , where the self-attention mechanism can selectively weight the most informative time steps within a gait cycle to maintain balance under high centripetal loads. All actors have comparable parameter counts (the MHA actor is 3.6% smaller than the MLP), confirming that performance differences stem from the inductive bias of temporal modeling rather than capacity.

4.5 Effect of Per-Skill Style Supervision (E3) 

E3 replaces the four per-skill discriminators with a single discriminator trained on the pooled refer-ence data; both runs sample the four skills with equal probability, which rules out data imbalance. We evaluate both policies in Isaac Lab under fixed commands for standing (0 , 0, 0) , in-place turning 

(0 , 0, 0.6) , walking (1 , 0, 0) and running (4 , 0, 0) .Both policies execute standing, walking and running, but in-place turning collapses under the shared discriminator . The Shared-D policy rotates at 0.08 rad /s instead of the commanded 0.6

Its turning DTW ( 5.66 ) is twice that of Multi-AMP ( 2.62 ) and matches that of standing still ( 5.76 ; Table 1): the turning skill has 8collapsed onto a quasi-static stance. Multi-AMP also attains lower DTW on walking ( −28% ) and running ( −18% ). The cause is that the shared discriminator is not skill-conditioned. A single discriminator rates whether a transition resembles any reference motion, not whether it matches the commanded skill. It therefore scores standing and turning alike, both on the reference clips ( ρ=1 .83 vs. 1.56 ) and on correctly executed rollouts ( 1.44 –1.52 vs. 1.51 ), whereas Dturn separates the two clips by 2.8×

(Table 1). Both discriminators score the collapsed motion lowest, so correct turning remains the optimum; what the shared discriminator lacks is the incentive to reach it. Under a turning com-mand, its style term does not favor rotating over standing, and correct turning outscores not rotating only through the yaw-rate tracking reward, by about 0.03 per step in units of rt. With Dturn , the style term raises this margin to 0.10 –0.12 . The weak margin is insufficient for the policy to learn the stepping pattern that in-place turning requires; translating skills do not collapse because their velocity-tracking terms alone penalize standing still by about 0.08 per step. Routing each transition to the discriminator of its commanded skill makes the style reward skill-conditioned, D(ϕ | g), and restores the margin. 

4.6 Hardware Validation and Robustness 

To verify the achieved speed, we timed 100 m sprints on a standard athletics track (Figure A.1). Under a 7.0 m /s command, a weight-reduced iteration of the full-scale platform (same actuators and kinematics) finished in 14 s , a measured average of 7.14 m /s; the deployment in Figure 1 runs stably at a 6.5 m /s command, but its ground speed was not measured. With the identical framework, a second humanoid ( 1.36 m , 40 kg , 23 -DOF) finished in 22 s (4.55 m /s); its lower top speed reflects smaller actuators, as both robots reach their torque limits at top speed. Operating this close to the limits leaves little reserve for rejecting disturbances. In simulation, under velocity impulses at the maximum training magnitude ( ∆vx=−1.0 m /s backward, ∆vy =1 .0 m /s lateral; 50 trials each), the policy recovers from every push at 1.0 and 5.5 m /s, but only from 56% of backward and 72% of lateral pushes at 6.5 m /s, consistent with the torque bottleneck in Sec. 5. 

## 5 Limitations 

Training covers only mild terrain variation (rough, wavy and gently sloped ground); the purely proprioceptive policy would need exteroceptive sensing for stairs or unstructured ground. Transfer beyond our two kinematically similar humanoids remains untested. The sustained top speed is bounded by joint torque rather than by the learning framework: at the highest hardware-tested speed, hip-pitch and knee-pitch joints operate near ∼99% of their torque limits and ankle-pitch near ∼90% ,and relaxing these limits in simulation raises the achievable speed. Beyond torque, an energy penalty is needed to keep joint power within motor capacity, and at still higher speeds the gait departs from the reference motion toward hardware-exploiting postures (lowered center of mass, exaggerated arm swing) that no motion prior guides, making suitable high-speed reference motions an open challenge. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We have shown that three targeted mechanisms—a causal multi-head attention history encoder, a heading-direction reward, and skill-conditioned multi-discriminator style supervision—can be in-tegrated to train a single policy that sustains over 7 m /s sprinting and aggressive cornering with reference-consistent gait on a physical humanoid, deployed zero-shot from simulation. Each com-ponent addresses a distinct failure mode of high-speed locomotion: temporal attention stabilizes aggressive maneuvers, the heading-direction reward unlocks agile cornering with emergent trunk lean, and skill-conditioned discriminators keep every commanded skill from collapsing. ## B Implementation Details 

Robot platform. The primary platform is 1.8 m tall with 17 actuated joints (Sec. 4.1). It weighs

## D Architecture Details 

D.1 MHA Actor 

The MHA actor processes a T =10 -frame proprioceptive history. Each per-step observation has dimension D=63 : command (7) + base angular velocity (3) + base Euler angles (2) + joint positions (17) + joint velocities (17) + previous actions (17). The full architecture is listed in Table D.1. The proprioceptive slice pt−k ∈ R56 is embedded via a shared two-layer MLP into a token 

zt−k ∈ R128 , with sinusoidal positional encoding added. Tokens pass through a single pre-norm Transformer encoder layer with h=2 attention heads, feed-forward dimension 256 , GELU activa-tion, and a causal mask so that zt−k attends only to positions ≤ t−k. The output is layer-normalized and average-pooled across time to produce a dynamics summary ¯zt ∈ R128 .The current command ct ∈ R7 is embedded by a linear projection. The dynamics summary and command embedding are concatenated, passed through a two-layer MLP with GELU, and layer-normalized to produce ut ∈ R128 . The actor head concatenates the current observation ot with ut

and maps the result through a three-layer ELU MLP ( [512 , 256 , 128] ) to the action mean. Action variance is a learned per-dimension scalar. E.2 Actuator Model 

Each joint uses a DelayedImplicitActuator that models the first-order response of the real mo-tors with a random actuation delay of [0 , 4] simulation steps ( 0–20 ms at 200 Hz ). Per-joint stiffness and damping are derived from natural-frequency specifications: leg and waist joints use 2–5 Hz nat-ural frequencies with damping ratios of 0.5–0.707 ; arm joints use fixed stiffness ( 50 Nm /rad ) and 15 damping ( 3 Nm · s/rad ). Joint-level static and dynamic friction are modeled per joint group. Action scaling is computed as 0.25 ×τmax /k p per joint, where τmax is the effort limit and kp is the stiffness.
