Abstract 

Asynchronous reinforcement learning (RL) for large language model (LLM) agents trains one policy on trajectories generated by another: rollouts come from stale checkpoints, and the inference engine’s probabilities differ from the trainer’s even at identical parameters. Standard remedies either clip importance ratios, which biases the update, or, as in GRPO, sample a group of responses per prompt, which is costly when episodes are long. We propose KL-Regularized Policy Optimization (KLPO), a framework that anchors the KL regularizer at the sampler. The regularized improvement step then has a closed-form Gibbs solution, and KLPO fits its log-ratio optimality condition by least squares on the sampler’s own trajectories, so the sampler probability enters through a log-ratio and no importance weights are needed. Profiling out the regression intercept replaces the intractable log-partition function with the signal’s sampler mean plus a sampler-to-trainer KL divergence. For token-level policy mirror descent targets, we show that the resulting gradient can be computed from terminal returns without a critic, via sampler-centered scores or a single trajectory residual, even under stochastic tool outputs. We further prove that independent Monte Carlo estimates of the KL term keep these gradients unbiased, derive the exact KL gap of cheaper top-K and binary approximations, and show that SPPO, GPO, REBEL, and BPO arise as special cases of KLPO. The result is a critic-free update that uses one rollout per prompt and requires neither a learned normalizer nor a group of responses.

# 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) for language agents is asynchronous by necessity. Episodes last minutes, interleave tool calls, and finish at unpredictable times, so workers keep generating with the last checkpoint they received while the learner trains on trajectories as they arrive. Every update therefore involves two policies: the trainer πtrainer  

> θt

that the learner improves at step t, and the sampler πsampler 

> θt−k

that generated the data, a checkpoint k steps old. They differ for two reasons. Staleness makes the lag k positive and different for every trajectory, with no fixed bound when tool latency varies. Execution makes their probabilities differ even at k = 0 , because the inference engine applies its own sampling transforms, quantization, and numerical kernels. Agentic training is off-policy by construction, and the trainer–sampler gap is a permanent feature of the system. 

> ∗Revised: October 5, 2026

1arXiv:2610.08963v1 [cs.LG] 6 Oct 2026 Most policy-optimization objectives are written for a single policy and treat the second as a correction. Token-level importance ratios grow more extreme with lag and accumulate along long trajectories, so they are clipped, as in PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), which biases the update. A learned critic adds a second model that must track a moving policy. Group-relative estimators such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) avoid the critic by sampling several responses per prompt, which is costly when a single episode is long. We build the objective around both policies by anchoring the KL regularizer at the sampler, the policy that collected the data. The regularized improvement step then has the closed-form Gibbs solution that underlies REPS, MPO, policy mirror descent, and RPG (Peters et al., 2010; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018; Lan, 2023; Zhang et al., 2026). Its logarithm is an equation between the trainer-to-sampler log-ratio and an improvement signal, which we fit by least squares on the sampler’s own trajectories. The log-ratio that an importance-weighted method must exponentiate and clip becomes the regression variable, so the gradient carries no importance weight, and the regression target is exactly the policy selected by the regularized objective. Because only the probabilities the sampler actually used enter, execution mismatch is covered along with staleness, and replay across checkpoints becomes a mixture of well-posed regressions, one per sampler version. We call the resulting family of objectives KL-Regularized Policy Optimization (KLPO); Figure 1 traces its token-level instance from objective to update. Our contributions are as follows. • Regression and profiling (Section 3). For any signal and granularity, regressing the log-ratio optimality condition identifies the sampler-anchored Gibbs target without importance weights; its gradient is the regularized-return gradient with the likelihood ratio removed. Profiling the intercept replaces the intractable log-partition function with the signal’s sampler mean plus a sampler-to-trainer KL divergence. • A common view of prior objectives (Section 3.4). With exact normalizers, SPPO and GPO are response-level KLPO, REBEL is its pairwise profiled form, and BPO’s exact trajectory loss equals the profiled token-level objective. Each practical loss differs from its KLPO instance by an identified approximation. • Critic-free token-level gradients (Section 4). The gradient of the profiled token-level policy mirror descent objective follows from terminal returns, either by centering the trainer’s score under the sampler or from one baseline-free trajectory residual, and both routes hold with stochastic tool outputs. • Estimators for asynchronous training (Sections 5 and 6). Independent Monte Carlo estimates of the local KL keep both gradients unbiased, and top-K and binary approximations fall short of the KL by exactly the divergence inside the merged tail. Replay across versions is a fixed mixture of per-version regressions, so token-level KLPO trains from one rollout per prompt with no critic, learned normalizer, or response group.

Assumption 2.1 (Finite Positive Model) . The vocabulary is finite and responses have bounded length. The reward R(x, y ) is a bounded deterministic function of the prompt and the complete response, such as a verifier score. The trainer and the sampler are normalized and positive on a common feasible support. 

Assumption 2.2 (Matched Collection) . For a fixed collection version, prompts are drawn from D and complete responses from πsampler  

> θt−k

, without outcome-dependent filtering. Every sampler-dependent quantity refers to that same sampler: the denominator of each log-ratio, the distribution used for centering or KL estimation, and the continuation that produces each return. 

Assumption 2.3 (Deterministic Transitions and Terminal Reward) . The next history is determined by the current history and action, and the reward is received only at termination. Assumptions 2.1 and 2.2 hold throughout and ensure that the sampler visits every feasible history and action. Assumption 2.3 is used only for samplewise identities that telescope token-level quantities along a trajectory; plain text generation satisfies it, and with tools it requires tool outputs to be deterministic functions of the history. A target policy is realizable if the model class {πθ} contains it. Statements about exact fits assume realizability; gradient identities do not. 

# 3 KL-Regularized Policy Optimization 

KLPO is built from one primitive, a KL-regularized improvement step taken from the policy that collected the data. We state it generically: a context h is followed by an outcome z drawn from a 4positive sampler conditional μ(z | h) on a finite support, such as a prompt and a complete response or a history and the next token. An improvement signal f (h, z ), such as a reward, an advantage, or a win rate, scores outcomes, and β > 0 is fixed. Proofs for this section are in Appendix E.1.

6.2 Consequences for Asynchronous Training 

No ratio to clip. The regression gradient is the regularized-return gradient without the trainer-to-sampler ratio (Section 3.2 and Proposition I.1), so KLPO has nothing to clip as checkpoints age or trajectories grow. The sampler probability still enters through the logarithm, so errors in logged probabilities or stored conditionals shift the log-ratio or the KL correction. Appendix I.2 compares KLPO with the importance-weighted update of FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al., 2026). 

One rollout per prompt. With full KL or MC-KL, one complete rollout gives an unbiased stochastic gradient of Ltok (Theorem 4.2 and Proposition 5.1), with no value function, prompt normalizer, or second response. SKLPO instead needs a normalizer for every prompt and sampler version, and with one response both natural estimators degenerate (Proposition B.4): self-centering zeroes the residual, and a log-mean-exp estimate of c∗(x) returns the observed reward. Its normalizer must come from independent data or a learned predictor (Appendix B), and an error ε(x) biases the gradient by 

ε(x)Eν [gθ | x] (Equation (I.9)). Table 5 lists what each instance needs. 

Interactive trajectories. Neither gradient route needs Assumption 2.3. Token regression uses only the sampler’s own continuation values, which a complete return from that sampler estimates without bias, and stochastic tool outputs add only a θ-independent term to sequence regression (Theorem 4.2 and Proposition 4.4). KL-regularized policy improvement. Lemma 3.1 is the classical solution of a relative-entropy-regularized improvement step. REPS, MPO, and AWR (Peters et al., 2010; Abdolmaleki et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019) fit a parametric policy to it by weighted maximum likelihood, a forward-KL projection; policy mirror descent (Lan, 2023) analyzes repeated steps; and RPG (Zhang et al., 2026) maximizes the regularized return directly, a reverse-KL projection weighted by the trainer-to-sampler ratio. KLPO fits the same target through its log-ratio condition. The three projections share the unrestricted optimum and differ within a restricted model class (Appendices G and K). 

Log-ratio regression and preference optimization. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) reads β times a policy log-ratio as an implicit reward. SPPO (Wu et al., 2024) and GPO (Zhang et al., 2025) regress log-ratios onto win rates and preference scores, and REBEL (Gao et al., 2024) regresses log-ratio differences onto reward differences. Section 3.4 places all three within KLPO through the signal f

rather than a scalar reward, since general preferences can be cyclic. 

Token-level PMD and score centering. BPO (Song et al., 2026) starts from token-level PMD with sampler advantages. Its Equation (25) derives the full-KL gradient of the exact trajectory loss before linearizing the residual, and its practical loss adds group normalization, a binary KL, smoothing, and clipping; our sequence regression keeps the residual, drops the prompt value, and allows random tool outputs. Score centering (Marek and Ryabinin, 2026) subtracts the sampler mean of the trainer’s score and multiplies by the reward. KLPO token regression applies the same correction (Marek and Ryabinin, 2026, Equation (4)) with coefficient R − βℓ θ,u , and its top-K variant (Marek and Ryabinin, 2026, Section 4.1) is the derivative of TopK-KL. 

Off-policy and critic-free RL for language models. Clipped importance ratios (Schulman et al., 2017) and group-relative advantages (Shao et al., 2024) are the standard ways to handle stale data and to avoid a critic. FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al., 2026) studies critic-free, single-rollout asynchronous training with token importance sampling and batch reward centering. KLPO changes the objective so that the sampler probability enters a log-ratio rather than a multiplicative weight. 

Soft values and implicit Q-functions. Regularized MDPs (Geist et al., 2019) and soft Q-learning (Haarnoja et al., 2017) characterize KL-regularized optimal policies through Bellman equations, and IQL (Kostrikov et al., 2022), IQ-Learn (Garg et al., 2021), and Rafailov et al. (2024) extract policies from value functions or read policies as value functions. At an exact fit, the response-level KLPO log-ratio is the optimal sampler-regularized soft advantage (Appendix C); token-level KLPO fits ordinary sampler advantages and learns no value function. 

# 8 Conclusion 

We introduced KLPO, which anchors the KL regularizer at the sampler and fits the resulting Gibbs target by regressing its log-ratio optimality condition on the sampler’s own trajectories, without importance weights. Profiling the intercept turns the unknown normalizer into a sampler mean plus a sampler-to-trainer KL divergence, and at the token level only the local KL remains. The profiled token-level gradient then follows from terminal returns, through sampler-centered scores or a single trajectory residual; both routes hold under random tool outputs, and independent Monte Carlo estimates of the KL keep them unbiased. The same principle recovers SPPO, GPO, REBEL, and BPO 14 as special cases. Token-level KLPO trains from a single rollout per prompt, with no critic, learned normalizer, or group of responses, which makes it a natural fit for asynchronous agentic training. Next steps include large-scale empirical evaluation, finite-sample and convergence analysis, and value learning for stochastic environments (Appendix I.7).

D.6 Reusing Auxiliary Draws 

Proposition 5.1 conditions on a trainer chosen independently of the auxiliary draws. Once the trainer has been updated with a set of draws, the draws are no longer independent of it. A conditionally unbiased gradient at every update therefore requires fresh draws from the historical sampler, which in turn requires its stored conditionals or the ability to rerun it. Reusing a fixed set of draws still defines a sensible training objective, a fixed sample-average surrogate, but the expectation identities of Section 5.1 no longer apply to it.

N.1 Records, Reductions, and Reuse 

Each response stores its prompt, policy tokens, reward, collection version, and the sampler log-probability of every generated token. The KL correction adds the following: M auxiliary token IDs per position with their sampler log-probabilities for MC-KL, stored as arrays of shape [B, T, M ]; the head IDs and their sampler probabilities for TopK-KL; the sampler’s next-token distributions for full KL; and nothing for binary KL. The rollout action’s log-probability is stored separately in every case, including when the action lies outside a top-K head. Correctly logged sampler probabilities make a separate reference-model scoring pass unnecessary. They should include the sampling transforms used at collection, such as temperature and truncation, because inference and training can produce different probabilities at identical parameters. At every update, including reuse of the same batch, everything that depends on the trainer is recomputed: the log-ratios, the coefficient hi,u or residual Di,θ , and the KL correction. Sampler records, rewards, and collection versions stay fixed. The loss sums over policy tokens and averages over responses; prompt, padding, and tool-output tokens are excluded and termination is counted consistently. An outer weight 1/T i breaks the exact identities. For MC-KL, repeated token IDs are valid. Token regression subtracts the mean auxiliary log-probability. Sequence regression uses the leave-one-out coefficients of Equation (5.3) and reports the U-statistic of Equation (5.4), which can be negative and is not clipped. A conditionally unbiased gradient at each update requires fresh draws from the historical sampler (Section 5.1); the labs-molt integration consumes each set of draws once before updating the trainer. For sequence regression with TopK-KL, kK and DKθ are recomputed at every reuse, and Equa-tion (D.5) is differentiated with only the residual coefficient detached, keeping gradients through both the head and the tail terms.
