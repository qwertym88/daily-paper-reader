Abstract 

In this paper we study Koopman operators associated with finite periodic realizations of elementary cellular automata. The spectra of these operators encompass various properties of the underlying dynamical systems, independently of any initial condition. A characterization of these spectra is proposed, leading to a spectral classification of all elementary cellular automata. This work is thought as a preliminary step for studying the spectra of Koopman operators for infinite realizations of elementary cellular automata. 

Keywords: Elementary cellular automata, Koopman operator, finite periodic realizations, spec-tral classification 

## 1 Introduction 

Since the seminal works of Wolfram in the 80’s [19, 20, 21], Elementary Cellular Automata (ECA) have been extensively studied in different settings and through multiple approaches. These investigations led to numerous results and to various classifications which have recently been summarized for example in [1, 10, 14, 15, 18], see also the references therein. Clearly, adding something new to this extended corpus of knowledge looks like an impossible task, nevertheless it is the humble aim of this manuscript. Koopman operator has been introduced a long time ago [6], but it is only over the last couple of decades that its usefulness and importance have been revealed again. An account on these recent developments can be found for example in the book [12] or the review paper [2]. Koopman approach provides a linear framework for studying nonlinear dynamical systems, with the price of often ending in an infinite dimensional space of observables. Our investigations on ECA will precisely be based on Koopman theory, as explained in the rest of this introduction. Note that our interest in this setting has been triggered by the two papers [16, 17] in which ECA are also analyzed through a Koopman operator’s approach.

1arXiv:2610.10967v1 [math.SP] 7 Oct 2026 What we provide in this paper is relatively simple: we study the spectral theory of Koopman operators for finite periodic realizations of all ECA (or more precisely of the 88 independent clusters of ECA, as introduced in [8]). For a periodic realization on n cells, Koopman operator corresponds simply to a 2 n × 2n matrix which can be described in terms of a directed graph made of 2 n vertices and 2 n edges. As explained in Section 2 this matrix contains all information on the dynamical system defined by the elementary cellular automata, and in particular the evolution of arbitrary initial conditions. Consequently, the spectrum of this matrix, namely the set of its eigenvalues, is very much related to the evolution of the dynamical system. This spectrum clearly does not contain as much information as the original matrix, since eigenvectors or generalized eigenvectors are disregarded. On the other hand, it can be easily evaluated in terms of the structure of the already mentioned directed graph. In particular, there is no need to solve a 2 n-dimensional characteristic polynomial or to fully analyze a huge matrix for getting this set of eigenvalues. Once the spectra of Koopman operators of the 88 clusters of ECA have been determined for n in a sufficiently large set, the taxonomy work can start. Indeed, instead of classifying all ECA based on the evolution of specific initial conditions, as performed in the seminal work of Wolfram, they can be classified according to specific properties of the spectra of Koopman operators. This approach is what we call a spectral classification of all ECA. By definition, it does not depend on any initial condition, and does not rely on any fixed n. In the classification proposed in Section 3 we also tried to remove all subjective factors, but are aware that the initial choice of the periodic boundary condition plays a role. This classification has also been established by keeping its future extension to infinite realizations in mind. We do not address this extension in the present manuscript. Given a fixed ECA, the study of the spectrum of its Koopman operator for various n also reveals some unexpected dependence on this parameter. From different perspectives, most of these properties have already been observed and are documented in the literature. However we also mention in Section 4 a few additional observations that we could not track down in the literature. For now, these are only observations and additional work would be necessary to turn them into proper mathematical statements. We leave this for future work. In summary, Koopman’s approach provides a unifying framework for studying dynamical systems in a linear setting. This allows us to investigate finite periodic realizations of elemen-tary cellular automata though the eigenvalues of the corresponding Koopman operators. By systematically analyzing these spectra, a classification and additional structural information are presented and discussed. These results pave the way for further investigations on specific ECA or for studying their infinite realization on Z.

## 2 Setting 

In this section, we introduce the setting and the notations. Namely, we firstly provide a clear definition of elementary cellular automata and of their finite periodic realizations. We then associate a directed graph to each elementary cellular automaton, and define the state transition network. Finally, we introduce Koopman operator, and explain how its spectrum can be deduced from the state transition network. 22.1 Elementary cellular automata and finite realizations 

We consider the alphabet A := {0, 1} and the three consecutive integers {− 1, 0, 1} which define a discrete neighborhood N0 of 0 ∈ Z. The notation AN0 represents the set of the eight triples with entries in A, and a local rule is a map R : AN0 → A assigning to each of these triples an element of A. There exist exactly 256 local rules, which are called Elementary Cellular Automata 

(ECA). A typical illustration of one of these rules is provided in Figure 1. 2.2 State transition network 

Given a global rule Rn, the state space AN is naturally endowed with the structure of a directed graph (with possibly several disjoint components). The set of vertices of the graphs is given by the set of states, and edges are defined by the global rule, namely the map u 7 → Rnu defines a directed edge from u to Rnu for any u ∈ A N . As a result, each vertex has an out-degree exactly equal to 1. Thus, the graph does not contain multiple (directed) edges, but cycles and loops certainly exist. The resulting directed graph is usually called state transition network . An example for the ECA 74 and for n = 4 is provided in Figure 3. Given this structure of directed graph, let us further decompose the set of vertices. Vertices with in-degree 0 are called Garden of Eden : they are vertices which are not the image by Rn

of any other vertex. We call cyclic vertex any vertex which belongs to a cycle (containing at least two vertices), and a fixed vertex a vertex which is the source (and the target) of a loop. Finally, any vertex which is neither a Garden of Eden nor a cyclic or a fixed vertex is called a 

transient vertex . By extension, we use the same names for the corresponding states: Garden of Eden, cyclic state, fixed state, and transient state. Before introducing a concrete example, let us mention an easy observation: each component of a state transition network contains one and only one cycle or loop. For later use, we denote by Cm any cycle containing m vertices. By extension, a fixed vertex is denoted by C1. As a consequence, to each component of the state transition network is associated a unique m ∈ N

## 3 Classification 

Based on the spectra of Koopman operators, we establish a classification of all ECA. Let us im-mediately stress that this classification is based only on the characteristic of the spectra, namely one the set σ(KRn ) of eigenvalues of KRn including their multiplicities. This information can be deduced from the cycle length spectrum, as already emphasized. Note that the eigenvalue 0 does not play a major role for the classification, but its minor role will be mentioned subsequently. Note also that the full structure of the directed graph introduced in Section 2.2 is not taken into account, since this structure becomes too complicated for large n. Clearly, this classification is partially heuristic, but nevertheless it provides useful information. 

Remark 3.1. Since our classification is based on the spectra of Koopman operators, it is im-portant to represent several of them. As shown in Proposition 2.2, the spectrum consists only of eigenvalues on the unit circle of the complex plane, and of the eigenvalue 0. In the subsequent figures, we represent the non-zero eigenvalues together with their multiplicity. For that purpose, observe that any λ ∈ C with |λ| = 1 can be written uniquely as λ = e iθ for some θ ∈ (0 , 2π].We call θ the argument of λ, and have chosen the cut at θ = 0 in order to have the eigenvalue 

λ = 1 represented at θ = 2 π. Obviously, the eigenvalue −1 is represented at θ = π, while no eigenvalue is represented at θ = 0 since since value is excluded. On the same pictures we also give an information about the 0-eigenvalue by providing the 

zero eigenvalue ratio , namely the ratio of the multiplicity of the eigenvalue 0 divided by the total number of eigenvalues 2n, see (4) and the explanations provided there. 

The 88 clusters of ECA have been divided into five main categories, with some natural subdivisions inside some of them. For concreteness, our analysis is motivated by the realizations for n ∈ { 13 , 14 , . . . , 24 }, with the spectrum computed from Γ Rn as explained in Proposition 2.2. The information on Γ Rn , namely the list of all cycles Cm with their multiplicity is provided in [22]. 4.2 Unitarity 

As mentioned in Proposition 2.2 any transient state or any Garden of Eden produces an eigen-value 0 in the spectrum of KRn . However, there exist situations for which none of them exists, which means that 0 does not belong to the spectrum of KRn . In these cases the entire spectrum of Koopman operator is included in the unit circle of the complex plane, and this operator is then unitary. Let us mention when this happens. For Rules 204 (identity), 51 (inversion), 170 (left shift), and 15 (right shift with inversion), this is trivially the case. More interestingly, for Rules 105 and 150, this happens whenever n is not a multiple of 3, see [4] and earlier references mentioned there for Rule 150, and [3, 7] for relations between Rules 105 and 150. 4.3 Families in Category IV 

Category IV contains various ECA which do not fit in other categories and for which we could not find any specific pattern in the spectra of KRn for different n. Inside this category, some families of ECA can now be defined. The most interconnected family is made of Rules 18, 122, 126 and 146. Very surprisingly, the spectra of the corresponding Koopman operators for these four ECA are very similar, for all n ∈ { 13 , . . . , 27 }. In fact, despite having many eigenvalues (growing with n), their spectra differ at most by a change of multiplicity of the eigenvalues ±1. At the level of the cycle length spectrum Γ Rn , the only difference is the appearance of one additional fixed state C1 or of one additional periodic orbit C2. From a different perspective these similarities had already been observed visually in [11] and in [5]. Another family is made of Rules 60, 90, 105, 150, and 154. Firstly, they share the common feature of being balanced (meaning their code in binary numbers contains four number 1 and four numbers 0). Most importantly, the ratio providing the information of the loss of information introduced in (4) has regular patterns that are not shared by other ECA. For Rules 105, 150 and 154 these patterns have already been mention in Section 4.2. On the other hand, Rules 60 and 90 do not lead to a unitary operator KRn (meaning the absence of eigenvalue 0), but their zero eigenvalue ratio are 0 .5 for odd n ∈ { 13 , . . . , 24 }, 0 .75 for n ∈ { 14 , 18 , 22 }, and above 0 .93 for n ∈ { 16 , 20 , 24 }. We do not have an explanation for this surprising pattern. For the remaining ECA in Category IV, any additional subdivision is less clear. The only additional characteristic we have found which could provide two families is the size of the largest cycle of the cycle length spectrum Γ Rn . However, precisely because the values inside the cycle length spectrum Γ Rn are quite difficult to predict for different n for the ECA in this category, we do not propose any further subdivisions. 14 5 Epilogue 

The various results presented above have all been obtained by looking at finite periodic real-izations of ECA obtained for n in a sufficiently large set. The next step is to investigate the infinitely extended realizations, when ECA are considered on Z. For that purpose, tracking the spectral distributions as the system sizes increase will be essential. In particular, it will be necessary to investigate which spectral features remain stable and which are caused mainly by the finite size of the systems. For properties which highly depend on n, as for example some mentioned in Section 4.2, it is not clear what is going to happen. We intend to investigate these questions in the future.
