# Higher-Order Morphology Priors for Quadruped Reinforcement Learning Under Actuator Degradation 

Derek You ∗1, Zafir Shamsi ∗2, Keqin Wang ∗† 3 and Christine Allen-Blanchette †3

Abstract — Actuator degradation turns quadruped locomo-tion into a coordination problem requiring joints to compensate for lost actuation. Prior work suggests that morphology-aware graph policies improve learning and generalization under body perturbations. We ask whether these benefits can be strength-ened by explicitly modeling higher-order mechanical structure. We represent the Unitree Go1 as a cell complex with limb- and body-level rank-2 cells and apply Hodge-based message passing. Under degradation training, the node-edge-face Hodge actor achieves the highest return on unseen actuator degradations, with higher survival and lower velocity-tracking error. These results support higher-order morphology as a useful inductive bias for whole-body compensation under actuator degradation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Learning-based controllers have made quadruped loco-motion increasingly robust to terrain variation, uncertain dynamics, and changes in external loading [1, 2]. Actuator degradation instead directly reduces the control authority available at specific joints, requiring the remaining body to compensate. Existing fault-tolerant approaches address this challenge through explicit mechanisms for adapting to the fault [3–7]. This motivates the question: rather than adding dedicated fault-handling mechanisms, can robustness emerge simply from how the quadruped is represented in the policy? Morphology-aware policies show that representations of robot morphology shape locomotion and transfer [8–11]. Most such work encodes morphology as a classical graph, with joints as nodes and links as edges. At a higher level, joints form mechanical units such as limbs and body-level structures. Such a graph can represent the edges bounding these structures, but not the structures themselves as higher-order objects. We therefore ask whether enriching the graph with mechanically meaningful higher-order structure pro-vides a useful inductive bias for coordinated compensation under actuator degradation. To investigate this question, we represent the Unitree Go1 as a cell complex whose rank-2 cells encode four limb-level structures and one body-level structure, and apply Hodge-based message passing over nodes, edges, and faces. The resulting faces make these multi-joint units explicit rather than leaving them implicit in their bounding edges. The main contributions of this work are twofold. (1) We introduce a higher-order morphology representation for the Unitree Go1 with mechanically motivated rank-2 limb and body cells, and a node-edge-face Hodge actor over it. (2)

Learned quadruped locomotion. Reinforcement learning now yields quadruped controllers that traverse demanding terrain [12, 13], transfer to hardware [1, 14], adapt online [2], and train in minutes [15, 16]. These are typically centralized networks over a flat observation vector, leaving the body’s mechanical organization implicit. 

Fault-tolerant locomotion. Robot damage adaptation has been studied through both online search and learning-based control. Cully et al. [3] rapidly adapt to physical damage by searching a repertoire of precomputed behaviors. Recent quadruped methods instead incorporate faults into policy learning, including hierarchical fault-tolerant control [4], fault-conditioned locomotion [5], teacher-student distillation across compensatory gaits [6], and gait adaptation under actuator power loss [7]. Rather than introducing a new adaptation mechanism, we ask whether the policy’s morphol-ogy representation itself facilitates coordinated compensation under a fixed learning protocol. 

Morphology-aware locomotion. Morphology-aware poli-cies organize computation according to an agent’s articu-lated body, typically using graph-based architectures [17– arXiv:2610.10934v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026 19]. NerveNet [8] and Shared Modular Policies [9] showed that morphology-structured communication improves trans-fer across embodiments and disabilities, and DMAP [10] found these benefits become more pronounced under larger body perturbations. Body Transformer [11], however, showed that purely local morphological communication can bottle-neck information propagation on harder tasks. These works establish morphology as a useful prior, but model it through pairwise relations. We instead enrich it with mechanically meaningful higher-order relations. 

Higher-order graph learning. Higher-order neural archi-tectures extend graph learning beyond pairwise edges by placing signals on edges, faces, and higher-dimensional cells and coupling adjacent ranks through incidence and Hodge operators [20–23]. Simplicial neural networks and message-passing simplicial networks generalize graph propagation to simplicial complexes, while cellular and Hodge-based constructions further support communication among cells of different ranks [24, 25]; the underlying signal processing rests on Hodge Laplacians [26–28]. We use this machinery in a different role: rank-2 cells represent persistent mechanical structures of a quadruped, and higher-order message passing serves as a policy-level morphology prior whose effect is evaluated under actuator degradation. III. P RELIMINARIES 

A. Unitree Go1 

The Unitree Go1 is a 12-DoF quadruped with four three-joint legs attached to a rigid trunk (see Fig. 1). Each leg comprises a hip abduction/adduction joint and hip and knee flexion/extension joints. Two properties motivate the higher-order representation in Sec. III-C. First, each leg acts as a mechanical unit: foot placement depends jointly on all three joints, so lost actuation at one must be compensated for by the others. Second, the four hips are rigidly mounted in a fixed coplanar arrangement, forming a configuration-independent hip ring with a shared reference frame.

A. Experimental Setup and Baselines 

All experiments use the Unitree Go1 problem setting described in Sec. III-B. All actor variants share the same critic, reward, domain randomization, optimization setup, and training budget. Each policy is trained for 400M environment steps with seeds {11 , 12 , 13 }.We compare five parameter-matched actor architectures:  

> •

MLP-C : centralized MLP mapping the global 48-D ob-servation directly to all joint outputs.  

> •

MLP-D : factorized per-joint MLP with shared weights and no inter-joint message passing.  

> •

GCN : two-layer graph convolutional network [32].  

> •

Hodge-L : node-edge Hodge actor introducing multi-rank propagation over ranks 0-1.  

> •

Hodge-F : node-edge-face Hodge actor extending Hodge-L with rank-2 cells. MLP-D, GCN, Hodge-L, and Hodge-F use the same 20-D per-joint inputs described in Sec. IV-B.

This is also reflected in Fig. 3, where Hodge-F clearly outperforms Hodge-L only in the degraded regime.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

We investigated whether higher-order morphology can serve as an inductive bias for quadruped locomotion under compound actuator degradation. In our experiments, the cen-tralized MLP remains strongest after healthy regime training, whereas degraded regime training shifts the advantage to Hodge-F, indicating that rank-2 limb and body structure matters most when the policy must learn compensatory coordination rather than nominal locomotion.
