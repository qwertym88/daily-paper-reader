# Deep neural networks, in their different shapes and flavors, are having a remarkable impact in almost every area of science and engineering. While they are also influencing the field of control, the purpose of this article is to discuss recent efforts in using ideas from control to better under-stand and design neural networks. The connections between neural networks and control are both old and new. It was already observed in the ’80s that backpropagation, the main technique to train neural networks, is closely related to optimal control. A different connection emerged more recently, motivated by a class of neural networks termed residual neural networks .It was noticed that their update equations are similar to those resulting from the forward Euler discretization of differential equations. This insight motivated the use of differential equation models, and control systems in par-ticular, to describe deep neural networks. Consequently, several research efforts were initiated to tackle a range of problems in deep learning by leveraging the interplay between control and deep neural networks. It is from this control vantage point that this article offers a gentle introduction to deep learning with the objective of making its key ideas more accessible to a control audience while emphasizing how control theory has played, and continues to play, a fundamental role in deep learning. The choice of contents is based on the last author’s plenary talk at the 2024 IEEE CDC and it reflects the authors’ idiosyncratic view of the area. Any omission of relevant research efforts is simply a consequence of the authors’ incomplete view of the rapidly moving interface between control theory and deep learning. Other articles adopting a control theory perspective include [1], [2] and complement this article. ) Notwithstanding the topic of this article, the authors emphatically state that no AI tools were used to generate its contents. 

Summary 

# This article offers an overview of recent developments in deep learning from a control vantage point. It reviews several classes of models including feedforward and residual neural networks, transformers, large-language models, and selective state space models while explaining how control theory plays an important role in understanding their train-ing, properties, or architecture design. The article explains how residual neural networks can be interpreted as the discretization of a continuous-time control system thereby enabling the use of control theoretic techniques for its analy-sis. To illustrate the power of this bridge between control and deep learning, two technical results are discussed: 1) the ability of a residual neural network to approximate arbitrary continuous functions is a non-standard controllability prob-lem that can be solved using geometric nonlinear control and 2) transformers, the key architectural component of most large language-models, can be seen the discretization of a consensus algorithm and thus large language-models exhibit consensus. A CONCISE HISTORICAL ACCOUNT OF ARTIFICIAL NEURAL NETWORKS 

Despite the recent rise into prominence of neural networks (NNs), their origins date back to the ’40s. This section provides a brief overview of the historical context of NNs. Readers not familiar with NNs are encouraged to first read the sidebar “What are feedforward neural networks?”. NNs were originally introduced as simple models of biological neurons and synapses. They were composed of two main components: neurons and the connections between them. An influential early model was introduced by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 [3], who described neurons as Boolean threshold elements. Building on this line of work, Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron in 1958 [4], usually regarded as one of the first trainable artificial NN architectures. This network could, in theory, solve any classification task whose classes are linearly separable, see “Classifica-tion vs regression tasks” sidebar. However, as noted in the book Perceptrons (1969) [5], it could not solve simple nonlinear problems such as classifying the inputs of the XOR function into the class tp 0, 0 q, p1, 1 qu , mapped by the XOR function to 0, and the class tp 0, 1 q, p1, 0 qu , mapped to 1. This limitation arises from the architectural limitations of single layer perceptron networks, and the lack, at the time, of an effective method for training networks with multiple layers. The book [5] is often cited as a contributing factor to the so-called “AI Winter”, a period where artificial intelligence (AI) research stagnated dramatically. For an overview of how AI relates to NNs and deep learning see the sidebar “AI, machine learning, or deep learning?”. The main breakthrough that pushed the field forward was the introduction of backpropagation. This approach introduced differentiable activation functions, such as sig-moids, along with a practical method for training mul-tilayer NNs [6], [7]. Other derivations of backpropaga-tion appeared earlier in different contexts such as [8]. Although these developments still shared some similarities with biological models, such as the sigmoid function used in the Wilson–Cowan model [9], the connection between artificial NNs and neuroscience gradually weakened. By the late ’80s, the two fields had largely diverged, with NNs increasingly driven by empirical results rather than biological realism. Nevertheless, same later models such as spiking NNs [10] retained closer ties to neuroscience, drawing inspiration from classical models including the Hodgkin–Huxley equations [11]. As the field progressed, many other NN architectures were introduced. Among the most prominent were recur-rent neural networks (RNNs) [12], which were constructed as sequence-to-sequence models, that is, as systems trans-forming input signals into output signals. RNNs possess, in theory, the ability to retain information over arbitrarily long time horizons, allowing current outputs to be related to past inputs. In practice, this capability is limited by sev-eral issues, most notably the vanishing gradient problem (the use of gradients in the training of NNs is discussed in Optimal control and backpropagation ) [13]. Despite these limitations, RNNs were widely used. Among the many different architectures that appeared in the literature, the following three have deep connections to control: residual neural networks, transformers, and selective state-space models. They are described in more detail in the sidebars “What are feedforward neural net-works?”, “What are residual neural networks?”, “What are transformers?”, and “What are recurrent neural networks and selective state-space models?”

Output and text generation: The transformer output is task-dependent. For classification, the output map h is typically the composition of two maps, similarly to what is done in feed-forward NNs, see sidebar “What are residual neural networks?”. The first is an affine map acting on a designated token (often the final token or a special classification token) to obtain logits. The second map is the entry-wise softmax to produce a distribution over classes. For text generation, the output map is similarly defined but the class set is the vocabulary of the language being used. The output map sends a token matrix to logits over all the vocabulary words, and the softmax converts these logits into a probability distribution for the next token. The next token is then selected by a decoding rule, passed through the embedding map f and appended to the end of the token matrix Z p1q. The process is then restarted by processing the enlarged token matrix starting with the map g1. This text generation process ends when a special end of sentence or end of text token is generated. In practice, decoding often modifies the softmax distribution before sampling. Two common rules are: (i) top-k sampling, which restricts sampling to the k most probable tokens; and (ii) 

top-p sampling, which restricts sampling to the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability is at least p, see [22]. Recurrent Neural Networks 

An early architecture for sequence-to-sequence modeling is the recurrent neural network (RNN) [12]. All the NN architectures so far described were formulated as composition of maps that update the state from one layer to the next. These maps were 

memoryless whereas RNNs employ memoryfull maps defined by a nonlinear control system. The memory of such maps is denoted by cpk , i q P Rd with k denoting the dependence on the layer and i denoting the dependence on the position (in the input sequence) of the token being processed. Novel architectures based on different discretization schemes 

The link between ResNets and the differential equa-tion (0.19) led several researchers to investigate if dis-cretization schemes beyond the forward Euler method could be used to produce new NN architectures with better properties. Some of the new architectures produced by this process include PolyNets [42], based on a polynomial ap-proximation of the backward Euler method, a Hamiltonian inspired architecture [31], based on the semi-implicit Euler method that is known to be symplectic, the momentum ResNet [43], based on the Verlet integration scheme, and several others [32], [44], [45], [46] based on generalizations of the differential model (0.19) that include second order derivatives. Rather than starting with a differential equation and discretizing it to arrive at a NN architecture, the differential equation can be directly taken as the architecture. This is the key idea behind Neural ODEs [33], in which the NN directly parameterizes the right hand side of a differential equation. The evaluation of a Neural ODE on a given input consists in numerically solving the corresponding differential equation using the input as initial condition. The objective of training is to find the differential equation whose solutions best match the training data. Similarly to evaluation, the training of Neural ODEs requires the use of a numerical solver for differential equations. Neural ODEs are especially useful when working with data that is known to be produced by a differential equation but the use of numerical solvers makes them less scalable than existing alternatives.

The modern connection 

The connection between optimal control and backprop-agation presented in The classical connection consists in formulating the training of NNs as an optimal control problem in discrete-time, deriving necessary conditions for optimality, and observing that those conditions essentially define backpropagation. As discussed in Differential equa-tions, control systems, and neural networks , for certain NN architectures, such as the ResNet, the state update equation can be regarded as the discretization of a differential equation. Similarly, the optimal control problem addressed in The classical connection can be seen as the discretization of a continuous-time optimal control problem. This obser-vation exposes an alternative path towards the training of NNs: formulate necessary conditions for optimality using an optimal control formulation in continuous-time and then discretize them to obtain a training algorithm. It is shown in this section that both approaches lead to the same solution, provided that suitable discretization techniques are employed. New training techniques 

The previous sections shed light into the interplay between optimal control and the training of NNs. However, the use of gradient descent is but one possible path to find the weights enforcing equality (0.34). When the weights are real valued, [45] discusses why gradient descent may be preferable to directly solving (0.34). The work in [50] uses the extra generality afforded by the maximum principle to derive training algorithms for NNs whose weights belong to finite sets, in which case gradient descent is not an option. Extending the optimal control perspective from one to two players brings us to the setting of games which proved useful to develop more efficient adversarial train-ing algorithms in [51], see also [52] for a rigorous conver-gence analysis. The modern continuous-time perspective was exploited in [48] to obtain training algorithms where the discretization time step is regarded as one additional control input used for more efficient training. Rather than linkages to optimal control, other advances in training techniques leveraged the view of NNs as dis-cretizations of differential equations. The important prop-erty of reversibility, addressed in [46], is intimately tied to training, since backpropagation requires both forward and backward passes. In particular, it shows how reversibility can be exploited for more efficient training and proposes new reversible architectures. Another important property is stability [31], [46], understood as Lipschitz continuity of the map sending the state of the first layer to the state of the last layer. Stability guarantees robustness of the network against noisy inputs or perturbations of the data. As discussed in [53], the Lipschitz constant depends on the activation function σ and the weights for the continuous-time model (0.19). For the discrete-time model (0.18), it also depends on the number of layers. The work in [53] discusses several architectures inspired by ideas from dynamical systems, such as Hamiltonian dynamics, that ensure stability by construction rather than relying on training. 

UNIVERSAL APPROXIMATION 

One of the reasons underlying the wide applicability of NNs is their perceived ability to approximate arbitrary functions. This is known as the power of universal approx-imation. To make this notion precise, recall that a NN is a family of functions ΦW : Rn1 Ñ Ro parameterized by the weights W, see “What are feedforward neural networks?” sidebar. It can then be asked if, given a function ψ : K Ñ Ro

defined on a domain of interest K Ď Rn1 , there is a choice of weights W so that ΦW approximates ψ arbitrarily well with respect to a chosen topology (uniform, Lp, Sobolev, etc.). 

A concise historical account of universal approximation in neural networks 

Classical universal approximation results established in late ’80s and early ’90s focused on proving that feed-forward NNs with small depth, that is, a small number of layers, but unrestricted width, that is, an arbitrary   

> 10 IEEE CONTROL SYSTEMS

» OCTOBER 2026 number of neurons per layer, can approximate continu-ous functions on compact sets, under mild assumptions on the activation functions. In particular, universality of sigmoidal activation functions was proved in [54], [55]. In a series of follow-up work, including [56], [57], it was shown that for broad classes of NNs, non-polynomial activations are essentially necessary and sufficient for approximating continuous functions on compact sets. The mathematical machinery behind these results is functional analysis’ den-sity arguments. A different argument was employed in [58] to establish that the approximation error decays at the rate 1{n, where n is the number of sigmoids in the hidden layer, independently of the dimension of the approximated function’s domain. The modern deep learning era revived these questions due to the evolution in NN architectures. Deep NNs have large depth, that is, a large number of layers, but bounded width, that is, a bounded number of neurons per layer. This mismatch between architectures prevents a direct application of the classical universal approximation re-sults. Establishing universal approximation for deep neu-ral networks relies on understanding composition of maps, which, due to nonlinearities of the activation functions, is challenging. The literature on this subject is quite recent. One of the early results, [59], focused on the class of ReLU activation functions to establish that one neuron per layer suffices to approximate an arbitrary Lebesgue integrable function from R to R with respect to the L1 norm. The results that followed addressed more general functions from Rn1 to Ro.For instance, when using the ReLU activation function and 

o “ 1, it was shown in [59] that universal approximation with respect to the L1 topology can be achieved with a minimum width between n1 ` 1 and n1 ` 4. The theory of universal approximation for deep NNs substantially advanced in early 2020, with notable discoveries in [60], and particularly in [61], where universal approximation was established with respect to the infinity norm for non-residual deep NNs, allowing for general classes of acti-vation functions. More precisely, it is shown in [61] that under very mild conditions on the activation functions, any continuous function ψ : K Ñ Ro, where K Ă Rn1 is compact, can be approximated in the infinity norm using a deep NN of width n1 ` o ` 2.

This setting is most closely related to [69], where controllability results for ensembles of infinitely many copies of the same control system are considered. Consensus dynamics 

Many empirical works starting with [92] observed that tokens tend to converge to consensus as the depth of the transformer increases, that is, the model collapses as all tokens converge to a single point. The first theoreti-cal explanation for this observed behavior was provided in [93], [94]. The continuous attention dynamics (0.43) was regarded as a mean-field interacting particle system and it was shown that, for almost every initial condition, all tokens converge to a fixed position as time goes to infinity. The analysis was restricted to a simplified model with only one head, a “ 1, and weight matrices assumed to be constant and proportional to the identity, WU ptq “ Id

and WPptq “ β Im, where β ą 0 is known as the coldness 

or inverse temperature .Under different assumptions on d, m and β, asymptotic convergence to consensus was proven using techniques from partial differential equations and ergodic theory. In subsequent papers [95], [96], [97], it was also shown that metastability holds under certain conditions on the param-eters and the initial conditions, that is, tokens first form several clusters and remain there for some time before eventually collapsing to a single one. The aforementioned papers address the full self-attention mechanism, but anal-ogous results have been proven for the causal (auto-regressive) attention [97]. The causal attention mechanism is a modification of (0.42) where the attention for the i-th token only depends on the previous ones. Ideas from consensus dynamics and control theory have also demonstrated usefulness in the asymptotic analysis of the transformer dynamics [98], [99], [100], enabling the analysis of (0.43) for more general weight matrices. To better understand the relation between (0.43) and the consensus models used in control, note that (0.43) can also be written in the following form when there is a single head ( a “ 1):

Another question that remains open is determining which tasks are better suited for transformers or for SSMs. A recent study [105] conducted a large-scale empirical comparison between Mamba and Mamba-2 models, and transformers. The results show that while Mamba-2 can outperform transformers of comparable size, this advan-tage is not transversal across all tasks. Interestingly, the same work introduced a hybrid model combining attention and SSM layers. This hybrid model maintained faster in-ference times than standard transformers while achieving superior performance on all standard benchmarks tested, thus suggesting that hybrid architectures may be prefer-able. Ultimately, the absolute scalability limits of pure SSM architectures remain an open question. While some models today have successfully reached approximately 8 billion parameters [105], it is still unknown how far SSMs can be pushed before encountering diminishing returns. Mean-while, a promising frontier has emerged in the form of hybrid architectures. By fusing traditional attention mech-anisms with state-space components, some works have shown that these hybrid models can outperform both SSMs and transformers of similar size [106], [29]. Safety and Alignment 

In recent years, the alignment of LLMs and SSMs with human intent has become a central challenge in deep learning. From a control-theoretic perspective, alignment can be formulated as a safety enforcement problem or as a trajectory tracking problem on a NN regarded as the plant. Several efforts have adopted a feedback control per-spective, even if this is not explicitly stated. One of the most popular techniques for alignment [107] uses human feedback to modify the underlying weights of a trained NN via reinforcement learning. This approach introduces a separate neural network, known as a reward model, which is trained to emulate human preferences. During the alignment phase, the language model acts as the plant, and the reward model provides the feedback signal used by a reinforcement learning algorithm to update the plant’s internal parameters. This effectively closes the loop between model generation and human intent, steering the plant towards a preferred behavior. Performing alignment during training is expensive since it delays the training process which is already quite expensive. A convenient alternative is test time alignment ,that is, algorithms that act as external controllers to regu-late the NN behavior during inference without modifying the underlying weights. This is analogous to adding a controller to steer a given system to a reference. Control barrier functions (CBFs) have been used for test time alignment purposes. One framework [108] designs an add-on safety filter that intervenes in the token prediction process. The filter acts by filtering out undesirable words thus ensuring the generated text will not violate specified safety constraints, such as toxicity thresholds. A different approach is taken in [109] by adding a one token prefix to every prompt. Control is exerted by steering this prefix so as to prevent toxic outputs. Toxicity is measured by a classifier that maps the state z of an LLM into a numerical toxicity score for the text encoded by the tokens. The key result in [109] is that, under mild technical assumptions, a toxicity classifier defines a CBF for almost every choice of weights of an LLM with the added one token prefix as an input. In [110], an optimal control approach is adopted to prevent toxicity. Text generation is framed as a trajectory in the space of tokens. The alignment is formulated as the problem of minimally changing this trajectory, subject to the token dynamics (0.24), so as to steer the output of the activation functions away from regions that generate toxic outputs. Often, preventing toxicity is but one of many objec-tives. Multi-objective decoding (MOD) [111] addresses the challenge of satisfying multiple competing alignment goals simultaneously. By formulating the problem as maximiz-ing a linear combination of a measure of goal satisfaction during the decoding step (the map h in ( S3 ) or ( S9 )), this approach allows for the dynamic adjustment of these objectives at runtime, functioning like a variable-gain con-troller that can be tuned on the fly. 

Controllers for architectural and training design 

Feedback controllers have been used to improve the be-havior of certain architectures. A prime example is the PIDformer [112] that inserts PID controllers in transformer blocks to create a new architecture. The objective of the PID controller is to prevent consensus, as discussed in Large language models, transformers, and consensus , and improve robustness to input perturbations. In addition to altering architectures, feedback con-trollers have been designed to improve the training of NNs. Research into PID-based optimizers has shown that applying PID control to adaptive gradient methods can dampen the oscillations seen in standard stochastic gra-dient descent or Adam method, leading to faster conver-gence [113]. Other approaches [114] treat learning rate scheduling as an online control problem, adjusting the optimizer’s step size based on real-time feedback from the training loss via an incremental PID controller. This line of work suggests that training algorithms can be seen as plants controlled by a feedback controller designed to drive the NN towards a minimum with desirable proper-ties. Until now, only relatively simple control techniques have been used and more advanced control ideas are likely to have greater benefits. 

Contraction and implicit neural networks 

Contraction theory [115] has found multiple applications within control theory. Recently, its use has been expanded to also include the analysis of deep equilibrium net-works [116]. This is a class of NNs where state zpk ` 1q

of layer k ` 1 is computed from the state of layer k using an implicit equation such as

Since a solution can be interpreted as a fixed-point, the use of contraction ideas is natural and was employed in [117] to establish existence of solutions as well as other properties. Recurrent versions of deep equilibrium networks have also been developed and, once again, contraction theory played a key role in their analysis [118]. The authors believe that contraction theory, and its generalizations such as k-contraction [119], can also play an important role in the analysis and design of other classes of NNs as well as their training algorithms.
