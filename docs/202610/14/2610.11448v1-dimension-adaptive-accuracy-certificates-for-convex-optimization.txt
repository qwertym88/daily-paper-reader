# Dimension-Adaptive Accuracy Certificates for Convex Optimization 

Egor Gladin 

HSE University 

# Abstract 

Many convex optimization methods use affine lower models to certify that a candidate so-lution is near optimal. Keeping full gradi-ents can be costly when the informative di-rections occupy a small, initially unknown subspace. We develop accuracy certificates that store projected affine minorants together with bounds on the discarded components. The certificates remain valid as the subspace, queries, and certificate weights are chosen adaptively. For quadratic regularization, we derive an exact correction that accounts for when each discarded component was recorded, and a compact problem for selecting cer-tificate weights. We also characterize the strongest lower bound supported by the com-pressed records when an ambient direction remains unused. The certificates can be used as stopping criteria for methods that provide valid affine minorants. We also analyze an adaptive cutting-plane construction whose or-acle bound depends on the discovered dimen-sion. Experiments on kernel SVM and ν-SVR problems show reduced certificate communi-cation and stopping times under an emulated bandwidth constraint. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

The problem of developing a reliable stopping criterion arises when an optimization method cannot bound the remaining gap to the minimum. For convex problems, affine lower models resolve this difficulty. For a convex function h, an exact subgradient ai ∈ ∂h (xi) at a query point xi gives the affine minorant 

h(x) ≥ bi + ⟨ai, x ⟩, bi = h(xi) − ⟨ ai, x i⟩. (1) Taking a convex combination of these minorants and minimizing the resulting model over the feasible domain gives a lower bound on the minimum. Subtracting this bound from a feasible objective value produces an ob-servable upper bound on the optimality gap (Teo et al., 2010). Accuracy certificates formalize this principle for execution protocols (Nemirovski et al., 2010; Gladin et al., 2025). The cost of retaining that information can be substan-tial. An archive of N slopes in Rd uses N d numbers even when the observed slopes lie close to a much smaller space. Projecting the slopes reduces storage, but also changes the affine models; the resulting pro-jected gap then need not certify the original problem. Greedy reduced-basis methods build approximation spaces incrementally (DeVore et al., 2013). For certifi-cation, an additional challenge arises when the subspace used to approximate observed subgradients expands: a component discarded at an earlier iteration may lie along a direction added later. We study the following question: what information must accompany adaptively compressed affine mino-rants to preserve a trustworthy, and as sharp as pos-sible, ambient stopping gap? Our answer is to store the projected part of each slope together with a veri-fied set containing the omitted residual. Following the uncertainty-set viewpoint of robust optimization (Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 1998), the construction accounts for every residual in that set before minimizing the model. The certificate allows arbitrary convex combi-nations of the recorded affine minorants and remains valid even when query points depend on the evolv-ing compression space or the assumed low-dimensional structure is inaccurate. For an objective with a known quadratic regularizer, the answer is especially explicit. Suppose the re-tained spaces grow by appending orthonormal direc-tions. Each raw cut stores its projected coordinates, the norm of its omitted slope, and the dimension of the space when it was recorded. A short backward recursion computes the exact largest possible norm of a weighted aggregate slope. The resulting certificate weights are selected by a compact second-order-cone arXiv:2610.11448v1 [math.OC] 8 Oct 2026 Dimension-Adaptive Accuracy Certificates

Contributions. 

• We generalize affine-minorant certificates to slopes represented by a retained component and a verified uncertainty set for the residual. • For a quadratic regularizer and a growing orthonor-mal basis, we give an exact recursion for evaluat-ing the certificate and derive a second-order-cone weight-selection problem that yields an optimal certificate under stated assumptions. • We quantify storage and evaluation costs and de-scribe how to use the certificates as auxiliary stop-ping criteria. • On a Euclidean ball, we analyze a construction that doubles its working dimension as needed. Relation to prior work. The weighted-minorant principle is established. Nemirovski et al. (2010) de-velop accuracy certificates for convex minimization, saddle problems, and variational inequalities. Gladin et al. (2025) extend them to inexact oracles and con-struct certificates for polytope cutting-plane methods. Fenchel views of first-order methods appear in Bach (2015); Gutman and Peña (2023), while Burns and Liang (2026) distinguish primal convergence from con-vergence of a computable certificate. We use these foundations; the focus here is the geometric informa-tion that survives adaptive slope compression. Weighted affine models, cut aggregation, and inexact minorants are established tools in convex optimization (Teo et al., 2010; de Oliveira et al., 2014; de Oliveira and Solodov, 2016; Parshakova et al., 2024). Our focus is preserving a lower bound for the target objective after adaptive slope compression. Robust optimization sup-plies uncertainty-set calculus (Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 1998), and exact worst-case oracle analysis emphasizes the role of the information retained (Taylor et al., 2017). The historical sets used here admit both an exact aggre-gate calculation and a restricted full-record minimax equality. Low-rank online learning and greedy reduced bases adapt to unknown spaces under different performance criteria (Hazan et al., 2016; DeVore et al., 2013). Error feedback addresses compressed updates (Karimireddy et al., 2019); matrix sketching and spectral bundling reduce storage of matrix iterates or problem-specific models (Yurtsever et al., 2017; Angell and McCallum, 2024). Our compressed object is a vector affine mino-rant, and the guarantee is a lower bound for the ambi-ent objective. Our adaptive cutting-plane construction combines established fixed-dimensional volume reduc-tion (Anstreicher, 1997; Jiang et al., 2020) with online discovery of informative directions.

Computational cost and storage. For fixed weights and k ≥ 1, evaluating the baseline certificate (4) costs O(N d ) arithmetic operations. Evaluating the compressed certificate (8) costs O(N k ), including O(k)for the recursion; forming each compressed record from a new dense slope during the underlying optimization requires an additional O(dk ) projection cost. Stor-ing the cut archive requires O(N d ) numbers for the baseline and O(N k ) for the compressed representation, with an additional O(dk ) for the basis. Optimizing the weights gives a quadratic program for the baseline and an SOCP for the compressed certificate; their solution costs depend on the solver and implementation. 

Use with optimization methods. Compressed cer-tificates can accompany any method that supplies valid affine minorants of the convex loss, including gradient and quasi-Newton methods. For methods such as L-BFGS that do not otherwise retain the full slope history, compressed records provide an economical auxiliary archive for certification. At each query, compute the intercept before compression and store ( bi, v i, η i, m i)with a verified residual bound. Any simplex weights are valid; they can optionally be optimized via (9). Evaluate (8) and use U − LQ(α) as the stopping gap. Compression is most useful when many slopes must be retained in a large ambient dimension while their discovered span remains small.

Only the first m working coordinates correspond to discovered basis vectors; the remaining coordinates are reserved for new directions. The unassigned coor-dinates of y remain zero, a property ensured by the symmetry of the volumetric barrier and the standard constraint-deletion rule; see Appendix C.2. # 5 DISCUSSION 

The information retained with a compressed minorant determines what it can certify about the ambient prob-lem. For nested spaces and a known quadratic reg-ularizer, the omitted norm and recording dimension give a compact description of each raw residual. The backward recursion converts that description into a global lower bound; comparing it with an incumbent value gives a stopping gap. When an unused ambient direction remains, optimizing the weights exhausts the information in the minorant records alone. The certificates can be evaluated independently of the query rule. Our adaptive cutting-plane method gives an explicit way to couple certification with an oracle bound governed by discovered dimension, under the stated fixed-dimensional volume-reduction assumption. The communication experiments show that compressed records can reduce transmission costs and stopping time while retaining useful aggregate certificates along first-order optimization trajectories. The gain depends on bandwidth, target accuracy, and the costs of pro-jection and weight selection. A separate numerical experiment illustrates dimension adaptation: oracle counts depend primarily on the discovered dimension, with a modest overhead relative to a known-subspace method. That experiment uses exact-rank gradients. Tests on approximately low-dimensional trajectories, larger problems, and actual distributed deployments would further clarify the practical scope of the results. Egor Gladin

A.6 Further consequences of the stored geometry Deleting cuts does not erase an already proved bound. Each evaluated scalar LQ remains a lower bound for the same target objective after its source cuts are deleted. Keeping their maximum therefore gives monotone Lbest . Replacing a selected aggregate by one record also preserves its residual profile and all subsequent nonnegative uses of that record. It does not preserve the maximum envelope of the original cuts. For a concrete example, set λ = 1 and begin with the exact cuts x and −x − 4. Their regularized model minimum is −1/2, supported by x alone, so keeping only that cut preserves the current certificate. Now add 3 x + 7. Thus reaching capacity can weaken the bound, but does not change its interpretation.

E.2 Common cutting-plane procedure and certificate 

We use the central-cut version of the volumetric method analyzed by Anstreicher (1998), with the explicit update rule in Algorithm 1 of Blanchard et al. (2023). Starting from [ −R, R ]q , the procedure approximately minimizes 

V (y) = 12 log det (A⊤S(y)−2A) , S(y) = diag( b − Ay ),

for the localizer Ay < b . It inserts a central halfspace at the current center or deletes the constraint with smallest leverage score if that score is below 0 .04. Deletion is followed by recentering before another oracle call. Centers are computed by damped Newton steps with decrement at most 10 −5; a QR factorization of S(y)−1A avoids forming ill-conditioned normal equations. When barrier values are indistinguishable numerically, the line search also checks a decrease in the Newton decrement. This is a volumetric center, rather than the analytic center used in other practical cutting-plane methods. The adaptive variant starts with an empty basis and capacity q = 1. It appends a reorthogonalized gradient residual when its norm exceeds τ = 10 −10 . On overflow it doubles q, retains the discovered basis, and discards the preceding phase’s records, as in Algorithm 1. The other variants use fixed dimensions q = k and q = d, respectively. Records are retained only while their associated objective cuts remain in the localizer. Ball-separation cuts are handled by the same procedure. At each center, after synchronizing retained records, we minimize C(α) from (13) over simplex weights using its second-order-cone formulation and the historical norm recursion. The conic solver uses feasibility and gap tolerances of 10 −10 . The recovered weights are made feasible by clipping negative entries and normalizing, and the certificate is evaluated directly from these feasible weights. A numerical allowance of 10 −11 is added. We stop at the first check with C ≤ ε, returning the corresponding weighted average. With ζ = ε − Rτ , this is the stopping threshold δ + Rτ + ζ in Theorem 6. We count every objective-oracle call, including overflow queries and queries repeated after a restart. Centering, constraint deletion, weight selection, and ball separation require no objective-oracle call. Several accuracy targets are read from the first certificate crossings of one trajectory, since the target affects only stopping. The common limits of 12,000 objective calls and 180 seconds per run are never reached. The 31 runs give 43 successful target crossings; the additional four targets for the ambient variant at d = 64 , k = 4 also include ε = 10 −6, reached in 694 calls.

E.4 Numerical checks and interpretation 

We check subgradient support inequalities, barrier derivatives against finite differences, and the volumetric center against an independent optimization solve. An independent weight optimization checks the historical certificate on small examples with nonzero residual profiles. For all 31 optimization runs, we reconstruct the final output from the retained points and weights and independently reevaluate its certificate. All recorded checks satisfy 

F (¯ x) − F∗ ≤ C, and all stopping checks satisfy C ≤ ε. The numerical dual gap at each reported endpoint is below 

ε/ 4. The largest basis orthogonality error is 1 .2 × 10 −15 , and the largest omitted gradient norm is 6 .4 × 10 −15 .At most 4 q constraints are retained during these runs, below the 25 q + 1 bound for the central-cut primitive. The controlled construction isolates the discovered-dimension dependence and permits direct checks of the value-free stopping certificate. These are floating-point illustrations of the theoretical contribution, not interval-certified executions of the exact-arithmetic theorem. The experiment uses one embedding per ambient dimension and effectively exact-rank gradients; it does not test approximate-rank trajectories or the benefit of historical residual information at a nonnegligible compression scale.
