# Phase-Gated Constrained Reinforcement Learning for End-to-End Capture and Detumbling of Non-Cooperative Targets 

´Alvaro Belmonte-Baeza ∗1, Celia Redondo-Verd´ u2, Jos´ e L. Ram´ on 2, Miguel Cazorla 1,Leonard Felicetti 3, and Jorge Pomares 2

Abstract —Active debris removal requires a servicer spacecraft to approach a non-cooperative tumbling object, capture it, and arrest its rotation. This sequence is conventionally divided among phase-specific controllers, each depending on knowledge of the target’s inertia, a dedicated grappling fixture, or a hand-designed switch at the contact-rich seam where such pipelines are most fragile. We show that a single constrained reinforcement learning policy can execute the entire mission, driving a dual-arm free-flying servicer from free-space closing through friction-based fixture-free capture to full detumbling of a target whose mass and inertia are unknown and whose state is observed only through noisy estimates. The enabling mechanism is a smooth proximity gate applied at three loci of a constrained Markov decision process. In the reward it induces the phase structure rather than imposing it; in the action space it forecloses a degenerate grab-and-pull exploit as a hard guarantee; and in the constraint set it resolves a normalization failure of constraint-based learning, in which a constraint spanning phases that load it unequally is rendered nearly inert in the phase where precision matters most. Both the failure mode and the remedy apply to any multi-phase problem of this kind. Over 1000 randomized trials the policy succeeds in 99 .5% while holding its contact-force, actuator, base-reaction and collision limits throughout, transfers zero-shot to unseen geometries and centre-of-mass offsets, and extrapolates across a tenfold range of target mass. 

Index Terms —Space Robotics and Automation, Learning and Adaptive Systems, Dual Arm Manipulation, Safe Reinforcement Learning

Active Debris Removal (ADR) has consequently become a mission priority [2], [3], with in-orbit demonstrations such as RemoveDebris beginning to validate candidate capture technologies [4]. The highest-value targets (large, massive, rapidly tumbling objects) are precisely those that are non-cooperative, lacking communication, at-titude control, and the standardized grappling interfaces on which cooperative servicing relies [5], [6]. For this class of target, dual-arm space manipulator systems mounted on a free-flying servicer are widely regarded as the most capable solution, since they permit deliberate, distributed force application throughout contact [6], [7]. The removal operation is conventionally decomposed into three phases: a pre-capture approach that brings the end-effectors into position relative to the tumbling target, contact establishment and capture , and post-capture stabilization that dissipates the coupled system’s residual momentum [6]. Each phase is individually demanding. The approach requires tracking a tumbling target with a free-flying base whose attitude is itself perturbed by every manipulator motion [8]; contact injects severe, unmodeled impact transients as the dynamic model changes discontinuously at grasp [9], [10]; and stabilization must arrest a large, uncertain inertia without saturating actua-tors or destabilizing the servicer’s own attitude. Model-based control has addressed these phases with con-siderable success when they are treated individually : detum-bling through optimal, adaptive, and fuel-efficient formula-tions [11], [12], and capture through robust and impedance-based schemes for non-cooperative spinning targets [13], [14]. These methods, however, share structural dependencies that limit deployment against real debris. They require accurate identification of the target’s mass and inertia, a process that converges only after a dedicated estimation transient and that model-based offline trajectory optimization can ill afford [15]; many assume a dedicated grappling fixture on the target [13]; and they rely on hand-crafted logic to switch between phase-specific controllers. At the transition between phases, where the assumed dynamics change discontinuously at the instant of impact, no mechanism guarantees that safety limits on contact force, actuator effort, and base reaction are maintained uniformly. The transition is left as an open seam. Reinforcement learning (RL) [16] is an attractive alternative for the regime that undermines model-based control: the severe base–manipulator dynamic coupling and inertial uncertainty of free-floating manipulation. Yet learning-based results in space robotics have so far addressed the mission one phase at a time: pre-capture guidance and motion planning [17]–[19], arXiv:2610.15491v1 [cs.RO] 11 Oct 2026 2

the physical capture or soft-capture grasp [20], or post-capture detumbling from an already-secured grasp [15]. Each typically assumes a favorable initial configuration in which the hardest parts of the phase transitions are bypassed. Moreover, standard RL offers no mechanism to strictly enforce safety: reward shaping, however carefully tuned, cannot prevent a policy from selecting constraint-violating actions at deployment [21], where a single excessive contact force or a lost grasp can end the mission. Constrained RL (CRL) addresses this last point by cast-ing the problem as a Constrained Markov Decision Pro-cess (CMDP), expressing safety as bounds on cumulative cost rather than as soft penalties [22]. Among CRL solvers, the Constraints-as-Terminations (CaT) formulation reframes each constraint violation as a stochastic episode termina-tion, embedding safety enforcement directly into the dis-counted policy-gradient objective with negligible overhead [23]. Constraint-based learning of this kind has recently pro-duced high-performing, safe controllers for legged locomotion [23], [24] and, in a robot-manipulation setting, has been given a rigorous safe-action-space treatment [25]. Its application to orbital manipulation, where contact-force, actuator-saturation, base-reaction, and collision limits must hold simultaneously and continuously through contact-rich maneuvers and across phase boundaries, remains unexplored. This leaves a clear gap: no single learned controller executes the complete ADR mission as one policy, spanning approach, fixture-free capture and full detumbling with safety enforced continuously across the phase transitions. The difficulty is not merely additive. The terminal approach and the capture– stabilization phase pursue fundamentally conflicting objec-tives: the former demands collision-free closing and precise pose alignment with a tumbling target, holding the manipula-tors quiescent against a moving base, while the latter demands contact-rich, force-regulated energy dissipation with those same manipulators. Naively summing phase-specific rewards yields a policy that learns neither cleanly. The central technical challenge is therefore a reward and integration design under which a single policy discovers all three behavioral modes and sequences them safely, with no hand-designed switching. We address this challenge with a CRL framework for end-to-end ADR on a dual-arm servicer equipped with disk-shaped end-effectors and a 6-DoF propulsion system. A single policy drives the servicer from a contact-free initial configuration to complete stabilization of the coupled system through friction-based pressure capture of a featureless tumbling target of unknown mass and inertia. Because the target’s inertial pa-rameters are unobserved and its state is available only through noisy estimates [26], [27], the task is naturally a partially observable decision problem [28] in which the policy must adapt online rather than rely on identified parameters. Safety is enforced throughout via a newly proposed multi-stage exten-sion of the CaT formulation [23]. Training and evaluation are conducted in a high-fidelity, GPU-parallel rigid-body physics environment [29], [30] that resolves the contact dynamics, actuator saturation, and continuously shifting composite center of mass that idealized numerical models omit. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first learning-based controller to execute the complete approach–capture– stabilization sequence for a non-cooperative tumbling target as a single policy, without fixtures on the target, prior identi-fication of its inertial parameters, or hand-designed switching between phase-specific controllers. The mechanism that makes this possible is a single, smoothly varying phase signal applied consistently at three distinct loci of the CMDP: the reward, the action space, and the constraint set. The main contributions of this paper are:  

> •

End-to-end learned ADR as a single policy. We for-mulate the full approach–capture–stabilization mission against a tumbling target of unknown inertia as one CRL problem and solve it with a single policy, removing the fixtures, inertial-parameter identification, and phase-switching logic on which prior pipelines depend.  

> •

Gated reward composition for conflicting phase objec-tives. Approach and capture demand opposite behaviors of the same manipulators. A gate and its complement form a partition of unity interpolating between hold-ing the arms quiescent during closing and tracking a co-rotating target face during capture, while a second, contact-derived gate cascades onto the stabilization terms so that detumbling reward is reachable only through a well-formed grasp. Naive summation fails to learn either behavior cleanly; the gated policy self-organizes into approach, capture, and detumbling modes with smooth transitions across the contact boundary.  

> •

Action-space gating as a hard guarantee. The same gate multiplies the manipulator command, suppressing arm motion at range. This forecloses the degenerate solution in which the arms seize the target and draw it toward the base instead of the base translating toward the target, a behavior reward shaping can penalize but not rule out. Ablation confirms the grasp becomes unreliable only when this guarantee and the reward gating are both removed.  

> •

Constraint decomposition for multi-phase constrained RL. We identify a failure mode of CaT [23]: because each constraint is normalized by a single scale tracking its largest violation anywhere in the task, a constraint spanning several phases is rendered nearly inert in the phase where precision matters most. We resolve it by splitting each such constraint into an always-active loose term and a contact-gated tight term, each with its own scale. Ablation shows the natural single-constraint alter-native fails as predicted, and the remedy applies to any multi-phase CRL problem.  

> •

Comprehensive evaluation in high-fidelity simulation.

A. Model-Based Capture and Detumbling of Non-Cooperative Targets 

Robotic removal of non-cooperative targets has been studied extensively under a model-based lens, and comprehensive surveys trace the maturation of capture mechanisms and control strategies [2], [6], [7]. The post-capture stabilization problem, in which a chaser must dump the residual momentum of a tumbling target while preserving its own attitude, has been addressed through time-optimal and coordinated tra-jectory optimization [11], fuel-efficient detumbling for dual-arm closed-chain systems [12], and momentum-redistribution schemes that channel the combined angular momentum into reaction wheels [31]. The capture problem has been treated with robust sliding-mode and impedance controllers designed to reject the model uncertainty of a spinning target [13], [14], and friction-based detumbling that regulates the tangential contact force to spin down the target while holding the base attitude has been demonstrated for dual-arm servicers [32]. A recurring feature of these works is a dependence on the target’s inertial parameters. Because these are unknown for real debris, they must be identified online or compensated adaptively: adaptive and task-compatibility controllers estimate the inertia during detumbling [33], and optimal concurrent formulations fold actuator and target-motion bounds into the control law while adapting to the same uncertainty [34]. In every case the compensation converges only after a dedicated estimation transient, which renders offline model-based trajectory opti-mization operationally impractical for time-critical maneuvers [15], [35]. These are precisely the crutches that the present work removes. 

B. Learning-Based Control for Space Manipulation 

Reinforcement learning has been applied to free-floating and free-flying space robots to exploit its capacity for han-dling nonlinear, strongly coupled dynamics without an explicit model [36]. A first body of work targets the approach phase: RL has been used for spacecraft proximity-operations guid-ance [17], and for motion planning of dual-arm free-floating manipulators toward non-cooperative objects, including track-ing targets that spin at unknown rates [18], [19]. Recent work has combined RL with Lyapunov-based control for proximity operations [37]. A second body of work targets the capture phase, where RL with tactile feedback has been shown to improve soft-capture success against free-floating debris [20]. Further research targets post-capture detumbling, where RL controllers stabilize a captured target with uncertain inertia and adapt across initial conditions without retraining [15]. Experimental studies on air-bearing testbeds have validated learned guidance for the pre-grasp motion, but they still hand off to a separately optimized law for the manipulator deploy-ment [38]. In every case, the learned policy addresses a single phase and assumes that the preceding phases have delivered a favorable initial configuration. The present work departs from this pattern, learning the entire approach–capture–stabilization sequence within one policy and under a constrained formula-tion that enforces safety throughout. Broader surveys of RL for spacecraft control corroborate both the promise of the paradigm and the open challenges of high-fidelity training and controller verification that any such method must confront [39]. 

C. End-to-End Versus Modular Policies for Long-Horizon Contact-Rich Control 

Outside the space domain, the trade-off between end-to-end and modular learned control has been examined directly for long-horizon, contact-rich tasks. Modular architectures that decompose a task into semantic sub-skills connected by a learned or scripted selector perform well within each sub-skill but tend to accumulate errors at the boundaries between them, where the local assumptions of adjacent controllers meet [40]. Hierarchical hybrid schemes mitigate this by pairing a library of specialized primitives with a high-level selector [40]; such designs, however, reintroduce a discrete switching mechanism whose transitions must themselves be managed. A single policy that internalizes the phase structure avoids an explicit handoff and preserves global task context across the transition, at the cost of a substantially harder learning problem when the constituent phases pursue conflicting objectives. Our staged reward and integration design is aimed squarely at making that harder problem tractable, and Section VI quantifies, through ablation, the failure of naive reward composition that motivates it.The staged-reward formulation is not specific to ADR; it applies to any multi-phase task of this kind. 

D. Safe and Constrained Reinforcement Learning 

Enforcing hard limits on a learned controller is the subject of the CRL literature. The Constrained MDP provides the formal setting [22], and Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) intro-duced a trust-region method with near-constraint-satisfaction guarantees at each update [21]. Subsequent methods trade off strictness, tunability, and computational cost: Lagrangian relaxations of policy-gradient methods [41], interior-point formulations that impose logarithmic barriers on cumulative constraints [42], exact-penalty variants that avoid second-order computation [43], and recovery-based schemes that separate task and safety policies for contact-rich manipulation [44]. Recent work has placed safe RL on a constraint manifold with formal safe-action-space guarantees and demonstrated it on physical robots [25]. Within this landscape, CaT is attractive for orbital manipulation because it enforces constraints in a state-wise, per-step fashion by construction [23]: whereas episodic, cumulative-constraint methods bound expected cost 4

over a trajectory, they cannot guarantee satisfaction at the single instant of impact, which is exactly where an ADR controller is most vulnerable. Enforcing the same contact-force, actuator, base-reaction, and collision constraints con-tinuously across all three phases is a central requirement of our formulation. The standard CaT formulation, however, does not transfer cleanly to multi-phase tasks whose phases load a constraint at different magnitudes. Section V-F introduces phase-gated constraints that apply only where they are needed, and the remedy carries over to any constrained multi-phase setting. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

F. Assumptions 

The scope of the results that follow is delimited by the following assumptions. 

A1 Noisy relative state, no ground truth. The relative pose and twist of the target are available only through the noisy estimates (7). The task is therefore a partially observable decision problem [28]. 

A2 Unknown inertial parameters. The mass and inertia tensor of the target are unknown and are never identified, 6

online or offline. The controller must adapt to the instance it faces rather than adapting a model of it. 

A3 Terminal-approach scope. The mission considered here begins at a stand-off of approximately 1 m ; the approach phase spans roughly 0.5 m of closing motion. Long-range rendezvous and far-field relative orbit control are assumed to have been completed by an upstream guidance system and are outside the scope of this work. The target tumbles but does not translate relative to the servicer at the start of the episode. 

A4 Rigid-body contact. Both bodies are rigid. Contact is modelled with Coulomb friction and compliant normal response. Flexible appendages, fuel slosh, and structural deformation of the target are not modelled. 

A5 Microgravity, orbit-free. Gravity is neglected. Over the 

T = 30 s mission horizon, orbital effects (gravity-gradient torque, J2, differential drag) are small compared with the contact and actuation wrenches and are omitted. Assumption A3 most narrowly delimits the claims of this paper. The contribution concerns the integration of the termi-nal approach with contact-rich capture and detumbling within a single policy; long-range rendezvous guidance, for which mature solutions already exist, is outside it. 

G. Problem Statement 

The control problem is now stated. Let ok denote the obser-vation available at control step k, constructed from the noisy target estimates (7) and the servicer’s proprioception, and let 

ak denote the command applied to the manipulators and the propulsion system. Given a set of safety constraints {cj }Jj=1 ,the objective is to find a single policy π : ok 7 −→ ak that drives an arbitrary instance of the task, from the contact-free initial configuration of Section III-D to the success condition (6), satisfying every constraint cj at every step of every phase and across the transitions between them, without any externally supplied phase signal, without a grappling fixture, and without knowledge of the target’s inertial parameters. Two properties distinguish this problem from the sub-problems solved in prior work. First, the phases pursue objec-tives that are not merely different but opposed : the approach requires the base to translate while the arms remain quiescent, whereas capture requires the base to hold station while the arms track and press a rotating face, so a reward that sums the two rewards a policy for doing each badly. Second, the constraint set is not phase invariant: the base velocity that constitutes safe, efficient closing during approach is a gross violation of the reactionless-base requirement during pressing, so a single fixed limit is either too loose to protect the capture or too tight to permit the approach. Section V shows that both properties are addressed by the same mechanism. IV. P RELIMINARIES

A. Overview 

The task defined in section III is solved by a single policy trained with model-free RL under a multi-stage extended version of the CaT formulation of section IV. The policy receives the noisy relative state of the target together with the servicer’s proprioception, and emits incremental joint-position commands for both manipulators and a wrench command for the propulsion system. The joint commands are tracked by an underlying joint-space PD controller running at higher fre-quency, and the wrench command is subject to the actuator lag of (2). No phase index is supplied, no controller is switched, and no grasp event is detected: the same network governs free-space closing, contact establishment, and detumbling. Both difficulties identified in section III-G, namely the opposition between the approach and capture objectives, and the phase dependence of the constraint set, are resolved here by a single device. A scalar proximity gate gp ∈ (0 , 1) , a smooth function of the servicer–target separation, is applied consistently at three distinct loci of the CMDP (Fig. 2): 1) the reward (section V-D), where gp and its complement

E. Gating the Action Space 

Gating the reward expresses a preference; it does not remove a capability. The second locus does. In (15) the manipulator command is scaled by the same gate, so that as gp → 0 the commanded displacement vanishes and the arms hold their configuration whatever the policy emits: far from the target, the manipulator sub-space of the action is in effect disabled. The reward gating does not make this redundant, for a reason specific to free-flying manipulation. The base masses 

150 kg and the target as little as 15 kg , so a policy that has learned only that reward accrues as d decreases has a second solution available through the momentum coupling of (1), and 10 

it is cheaper than thrusting the base: extend an arm, contact the target, and pull. The target accelerates inward, d decreases, the closing reward is collected, and meanwhile the debris has been given an uncontrolled velocity, no grasp has formed, and the base has been disturbed by the reaction. Reward shaping can penalize this exploit but cannot forbid it, and a randomly exploring policy may find it first and never leave it. Equation (15) removes it from the action space instead: while d is large the arms cannot reach out at all, so the only way to close is to move the base, and manipulator authority appears only once gp has risen, by which point the reward of section V-D rewards the arms for tracking the face rather than for reducing the separation. It is essential that both loci are driven by the same signal: sharing gp guarantees the manipulators acquire authority no faster than they acquire the objective it serves. section VI reports an ablation isolating this locus. 

F. Gating the Constraints 

The third locus concerns the constraint set, and addresses a difficulty that is a property of CaT itself rather than of the ADR task. 

1) The normalization problem: The normalization of (12) carries an implication that is benign in the single-regime tasks for which CaT was developed, and damaging here. Because one scalar scale is estimated from the largest violations a constraint produces anywhere in the task, the pressure that constraint exerts in a given situation depends not on the violation there alone, but on that violation relative to the worst it incurs anywhere . Consider a base-velocity constraint imposed as a single always-active term at the tight limit capture requires, 0.01 m s −1. The approach translates the base at ∼ 0.1 m s −1, a violation of ∼ 0.09 m s −1 that enters every batch, so (12) settles at cmax ≈ 0.09 . During capture the residual drift threatening the grasp is a violation of only 

∼ 0.01 m s −1; normalized, its magnitude is 0.01 /0.09 ≈ 0.11 ,and with pmax = 0 .25 the induced termination probability is 

δ = 0 .25 × 0.11 ≈ 0.03 . (23) The constraint that exists to enforce a reactionless base exerts essentially no pressure during capture: its normalization has been captured by the approach, and against those transients the drift that actually threatens the grasp appears negligible. It is not badly tuned; it has been diluted. This follows directly from (12) and arises in any multi-phase task whose phases load the same constraint at different magnitudes. 

2) Phase-gated constraint decomposition: The remedy fol-lows from the diagnosis: if one scale is being asked to serve two regimes, the constraint should be split so that each regime carries its own. Each base-velocity constraint is therefore replaced by a pair ,

cloose (s, a) = ∥vb∥ − vloose , (24) 

ctight (s, a) =  ∥vb∥ − vtight  · ⊮c, (25) where vloose ≫ vtight and ⊮c indicates end-effector contact. The loose term is always active and shapes the approach, capping the closing speed and forcing the servicer to decelerate rather than arrive ballistically. The tight term is identically zero out of contact, and therefore contributes nothing to its own batch maximum during the approach: its scale is estimated exclusively from contact-phase violations. That term now sees its 0.01 m s −1 violation against a scale of the same order, a normalized magnitude near unity, so with pmax = 0 .5

δ ≈ 0.5 × 1.0 = 0 .5, (26) more than an order of magnitude above (23) on the same physical violation. The tight limit is enforced where it matters; the loose limit continues to shape the phase where the tight one would be nonsensical. The same decomposition is applied to the target-velocity constraints, where the motivation is complementary. The target tumbles freely during the approach at rates far above any limit the mission would tolerate after capture, so an always-active constraint on target angular velocity would be violated mas-sively throughout the approach, for a condition the servicer has no physical means of influencing and, through the maximum in (11), would suppress the entire reward signal from the first step of every episode. Gating on contact restricts it to the regime in which the policy can act on it. The constraint gates are binary indicators, not the smooth sigmoids of section V-C, and the distinction is deliberate: the reward and action gates shape a continuous landscape differentiated through by the learning signal, whereas the constraint gates feed only the violation magnitude to (11), with no gradient taken through them. A binary indicator is therefore sufficient, and makes the tight term exactly zero out of contact, which is what keeps its normalization scale uncontaminated. A smooth gate would leak a small violation at every approach step, and that leakage, multiplied by the large approach-phase excursion, would reintroduce the contamination the decomposition exists to prevent.

Table IV lists the full constraint set. Constraints fall into three groups: 

Mission constraints bound the residual motion of the target after capture and the reaction transmitted to the base during pressing. These are the decomposed pairs of section V-F. 

Actuation constraints bound thruster force, reaction torque, and joint effort. These are set below the corresponding hard-ware limits: the joint-torque constraint is 5 N m against actu-ators capable of 39 and 13 N m , and the base force constraint is 5 N against 10 N of thruster authority. They do not encode what the hardware can survive; they encode the margin within which the mission is prepared to operate, and they leave the remaining authority as reserve. 

Safety constraints bound the contact force at the end effec-tors and prohibit contact between the target and the servicer base. The contact-force limit of 20 N is the upper bound of the corridor established in section III-C. H. Curriculum 

Training begins from a task the policy can solve and expands toward the task the mission requires. A single scalar 

λ ∈ [0 , 1] , advancing linearly with training and saturating at 

70% of the schedule, drives two ranges. 

Tumbling rate: The initial angular velocity of the target is sampled from a range that grows with λ, from a stationary target at λ = 0 to the full rate of section III-B at λ = 1 .

Initial separation: The initial distance is sampled from 

[d⋆, d ⋆ + 0 .5λ m] . The range expands ; it does not translate. Near-contact initial conditions are retained at every stage of training, so the policy continues to rehearse capture and detumbling while it is learning to approach, and does not forget the contact skills while acquiring the free-space ones. The expanding-range design has a consequence worth mak-ing explicit, because it frames what the approach phase contributes. At λ = 0 the servicer begins already on station against a stationary target: the task degenerates to the capture-and-detumble problem, with no approach to perform. The curriculum therefore teaches the contact-intensive task first, and introduces the approach phase smoothly afterwards. 

I. Training 

The policy is trained with a customized implementation of PPO [47] under the CaT objective (Eq. (10)), extended by the machinery in section V-F to enable multi-stage tasks, using 

4096 environments simulated in parallel on a single GPU [29], [30], [48]. Physics is integrated at 200 Hz and the policy queried at 50 Hz ; episodes last T = 30 s . The target mass is randomized over the range of section III-B at every reset, and the relative-state observations are corrupted according to Eq. (7). The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with three hidden layers of [256,256,256] units and tanh activations. Optimization uses Adam at a learning rate of 0.001 , with discount factor γ = 0 .995 , GAE parameter λ = 0 .95 , clip range 0.2, and entropy coefficient 0.005; 24 steps are collected per environment per update, over 2500 epochs and 4 mini-batches. The constraint normalization of Eq. (12) uses a decay rate τc = 0 .95 . Training takes approximately 50 minutes on a single desktop machine with an NVIDIA GeForce 3090Ti GPU. VI. E. Emergent Phase Structure 

The policy receives no phase index, evaluates no switching condition, and selects no controller: a single network governs the episode throughout. This raises a fair question: whether a policy modulated by a distance-dependent gate has merely had a hand-designed switch substituted for a learned one. It has not, and the distinction carries the contribution. The gates determine where , as a function of state, reward and manipulator authority are placed; the actions taken within that placement remain the policy’s own. Nothing in the proximity gate instructs the servicer to thrust toward the target, to arrest that motion at the stand-off rather than overshoot, to time the arms onto a rotating face, or to modulate two friction forces so as to detumble an unknown inertia while canceling its own base reaction. Each is a control problem the policy solves, not a branch it is switched through. Fig. 4 traces one representative episode: the successful trial whose initial tumbling rate and mass are closest to the population medians and whose initial separation lies in the upper quartile, so that a full approach is exercised. The regime boundaries drawn across the panels are defined not by the gates but by physical events: the onset of bilateral contact, and the onset of sustained tumble reduction. The accompanying video shows the same episode in motion, together with representative trials from the generalization scenarios of section VI-F. The approach appears in the distance, propulsion and joint-torque panels together. The servicer applies a burst of thrust, closes the separation, then cuts the command well before ar-rival, coasting onto the stand-off rather than braking against it. Nothing in the formulation requires this: the gate is a function of distance alone and issues no deceleration command, the closing reward peaks at the stand-off rather than at contact, and the actuator lag of (2) denies an instantaneous reversal. 1) Unknown Geometry: The capture strategy of section V is fixture-free: the end effectors establish a friction grasp by pressing on opposing faces. Whether that competence depends on the cube’s flat, equal faces is tested by evaluating the trained policy on two geometries never sampled during training: a cylinder of diameter 0.5 m and height 0.25 m (Fig. 5a), and a rectangular cuboid of 0.3 × 0.25 × 0.5 m (Fig. 5b), both carrying roughly 2.2 times the mean moment of inertia of the trained cube at equal mass. The policy network is unchanged; only the width parameter that places the end-effector command targets in section V-B3 is set to the known extent of the new body, so the transfer is zero-shot at the level of the policy. The cylinder succeeds in 99 .2% of trials and the cuboid in 

99 .7% , against 99 .5% on the cube. More telling is the phase breakdown: on both geometries the servicer completes the approach and establishes a bilateral friction grasp in every trial, seating the end effectors on a continuously curved surface, and on faces of an extent and separation never seen in training, as reliably as on the cubic faces. Safety is likewise unaffected, with no collision in any trial and the contact-force constraint violated marginally less often than nominally. One difference does appear. Over both geometries the contact-gated base-velocity constraints are violated roughly twice as often as nominally, consistent with a smaller and less predictable contact patch transmitting the reaction of the detumbling couple less cleanly than a flat face of known extent. The single loss of grasp observed across these two scenarios occurs on the curved geometry, where that patch is smallest. 

2) Unmodeled Inertial Asymmetry: Assumption A2 states that the inertial parameters of the target are unknown, and the method of section V performs no identification of them. Randomizing the target mass tests only part of that assump-tion: it scales the inertia tensor without altering its structure, and leaves the center of mass at the geometric center. The more demanding case is a displaced center of mass, since the servicer observes only the pose of the body, so a wrench applied on the geometric faces exerts an unpredictable torque about the true center of mass, coupling the target’s translational and rotational responses in a way they are not for a symmetric body. This is evaluated by displacing the center of mass by an offset drawn uniformly from ±0.05 m on each axis, giving displacements of up to 0.087 m : 69% of the cube’s half-side and 72% of the pressing moment arm. The effect on the outcome is small. The policy succeeds in 16 

99 .2% of trials against 99 .5% nominally, and completes the approach and establishes a bilateral grasp in every trial. What is most informative is how little the remaining statistics move: the residual target and base velocities are indistinguishable from the nominal values at every reported digit, the contact forces are unchanged, and the contact-gated base-velocity constraints rise only moderately. No new failure mode appears, and none involves a loss of contact or a safety violation. These results support the claim that the policy accommo-dates unknown inertial parameters by feedback rather than by identification. It regulates contact forces and target velocity closely enough that a substantial and unobservable inertial asymmetry is absorbed as a disturbance, without any estimate of where the center of mass lies. This distinguishes the approach from model-based capture strategies, which require the target’s inertial parameters before a stabilizing controller can be synthesized.

G. Ablations 

The design of section V places the proximity gate at three loci of the CMDP. This subsection varies each and evaluates the resulting policies under the protocol of section VI-A. Removing the reward gating replaces the complementary weighting of section V-D with a plain sum of the same terms; removing the action gating deletes gp from (15), so that joint increments are applied in full at any separation. The constraint decomposition of section V-F is replaced not by an unconstrained formulation but by the natural single-constraint alternative, in which one constraint per axis carries a limit interpolated between the loose and tight values according to the contact signal, ¯v = vloose (1 − ⊮c) + vtight ⊮c, a variant that is phase-aware in every respect except one, maintaining a single normalization scale where the decomposition maintains two. All configurations were trained with an identical budget and hyperparameters and evaluated over 1000 nominal trials (Table X). The two gating loci prove partially redundant but jointly necessary. With the decomposition retained, removing either alone costs little: 97 .3% without action gating and 92 .1% 

without reward gating, against 99 .5% for the full design. Re-moving both reduces success to 54 .6% . Either locus therefore suffices to induce the phase separation the task depends on. They are not equivalent, however: every configuration lacking reward gating expends an order of magnitude more propulsive effort ( 0.71 to 0.73 against 0.045 m s −1) and regulates the press markedly less well ( 8.7 to 8.8 N steady contact force against 11 .0 N ). The reward gate is what produces an efficient, well regulated capture; the action gate provides, in addition, a guarantee that holds irrespective of what the policy has learned. Their combined absence is also the only condition under which the grasp itself becomes unreliable: among the configurations retaining the decomposition, contact is lost before the end of the episode in 10 .8% of trials when both gates are removed, and in none whenever either is present. The constraint decomposition proves the least substitutable of the three. Replacing it with the interpolated single-limit constraint reduces success from 99 .5% to 23 .8% , the lowest of any configuration tested, and costs performance at every level of gating: with neither gate present, success falls from 

54 .6% to 36 .9% . The interpolated variant fails neither at the approach, which it completes in every trial, nor at the grasp, which it establishes in every trial and retains in all but 3.5% ;it fails at the residual, leaving the base at 0.0137 m s −1

and 0.0149 rad s −1 against 0.0028 and 0.0040 for the full design. This is the dilution predicted in section V-F: tightening a limit during contact is not sufficient by itself, because a single constraint carries a single normalization scale set by the approach transient. H. Comparison with Model-Based Methods 

This subsection places the preceding results in the context of the model-based and hybrid methods that represent the current state of the art in dual-arm capture and detumbling. A direct numerical comparison is not meaningful: none of these methods shares the servicer model, target set, success criterion, or simulator used here, and none has released an implementation, so any reproduction would compare against a reconstruction rather than the method itself. The comparison is therefore drawn along the axis a shared benchmark would obscure: the assumptions each method requires in order to operate (Table XI). Three such assumptions recur in the literature and are absent from the present method. The first is knowledge of the tar-get’s inertial parameters. Friction-based detumbling regulates the contact force against a target whose inertia is taken as known [32]; where it is not, it must be identified online or compensated adaptively [33], [34], and in every such case the estimate converges only after a dedicated excitation transient, during which the stabilizing controller cannot yet act on a correct model. The present policy performs no identification: section VI-F2 shows it absorbs a center-of-mass displacement of up to 72% of the pressing moment arm, entirely unmodeled, with no measurable change in residual precision. The second is a hand-designed decomposition of the mission into phase-specific controllers joined by switching logic. Ro-bust and time-optimal formulations plan or track a trajectory for a single phase and rely on separate laws for the others [11], [13], and even the hybrid learning method closest to this work composes a sliding-mode motion controller with a separately trained force loop [35]. The switching instant coincides with the contact discontinuity, which section I identifies as the point of greatest fragility; section VI-E shows the present policy crossing that boundary without a controller handoff and without an impulsive disturbance to the base, because the phase structure is internal to a single continuous policy rather than imposed by a switch. In this paper, we show that a single reinforcement-learning policy can execute the complete active-debris-removal mission end-to-end, with no grappling fixture, no identification of the target’s inertial parameters, and no hand-designed switch between phases. Observing only noisy relative state and its own proprioception, the policy drives a dual-arm free-flying servicer through the entire sequence, succeeding in 99 .5% 

of 1000 randomized trials while holding its contact-force, actuator, base-reaction, and collision constraints throughout. The enabling idea is a single proximity gate applied at three loci of a CMDP. At the reward, the gate and its complement form a partition of unity interpolating between the opposed objectives of closing and capture, so that the phase structure is induced by the task rather than imposed by a supervisor. At the action space, the same gate suppresses manipulator authority at range. This forecloses the grab-and-pull exploit available to a free-flying servicer whose target is the lighter body, and it does so as a hard guarantee rather than a learned preference. At the constraint set, we identified and resolved a normalization failure of CaT in multi-phase tasks: a constraint spanning phases that load it at different magnitudes has its normalization captured by the most violated phase, and is rendered inert where precision matters most. Decomposing it into an always-active loose term and a contact-gated tight term restores its authority. This failure mode and its remedy apply to any multi-phase CRL problem that aims to use CaT. The evaluation supports these claims on several independent axes. The policy transfers zero-shot to target geometries and centre-of-mass offsets never seen in training. It extrapolates across a tenfold range of target mass, up to a target twice the mass of the servicer, with no loss of success and a shorter settling time. When the tumbling rate is pushed beyond twice the fastest rate seen in training, it degrades gracefully rather than abruptly. Read together, these studies localize the binding difficulty of fixture-free capture: it is set by the bandwidth with which the manipulators can track and hold a moving contact patch, not by the inertia of the body they hold. Ablations confirm that each gating locus contributes and that the constraint decomposition is necessary: its natural single-constraint alternative fails as the normalization analysis predicts. Several limitations bound these conclusions. The results are obtained in simulation; a hardware demonstration is the necessary next step, and the sim-to-real gap for the contact dynamics on which the method depends is not characterized here. The residual failures, though benign, are not fully explained, and finer residual control, whether through a third contact point or momentum-aware placement of the two, is left to future work. The target is treated as a single rigid body, so flexible appendages, propellant slosh, and multi-body debris are beyond the present scope. Finally, the method enforces its constraints with high but not certified reliability: violations are driven to a small fraction of control steps and the hard collision constraint was never violated across the trials reported, but no worst-case guarantee is provided, and coupling learned capture with formal safety certification is an important direction. ADR demands phase structure, safety enforcement, and robustness to unknown targets. These results indicate that a single continuously gated policy, trained under constraints, can acquire all three at once, rather than assembling them from separately designed and separately switched components. The gating and constraint-decomposition mechanisms apply 19
