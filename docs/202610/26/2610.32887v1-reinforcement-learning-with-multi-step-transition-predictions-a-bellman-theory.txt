Abstract 

Multi-step predictions of future transition realizations can improve reinforcement learning, but their value remains difficult to quantify. Directly including these predictions in the state can lead to exponential state-space growth, making direct dynamic programming computationally expensive. We study discounted finite MDPs under a block-feedback protocol: at the start of each 𝐾 -period block, the decision maker observes 𝐾 -step probabilistic transition predictions for a subset of actions and adapts decisions to observed states within the block. We show that the optimal value satisfies a Bayesian Bellman equation on the original state space. This representation reveals that predictive information creates value by recursively changing the local order of maximization and expectation in the Bellman expansion. We formalize this effect through a Bellman–Jensen analysis and quantify the effects of prediction horizon, error, action coverage, and within-block feedback. We propose block-feedback Bayesian offline learning with online adaptation (BF–BOLA), a model-based algorithm for learning near-optimal policies, and establish upper and lower sample-complexity bounds that match up to logarithmic factors in the stated regimes. The results show that predictions can reduce transition learning to uncovered rows and that learning a near-optimal policy can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the Bayesian value function. 

Keywords: reinforcement learning, Markov decision processes, transition predictions, value of information, sample complexity 

# 1 Introduction 

Predictions of future system conditions are increasingly available in online decision making problems (Lim and Zohren, 2021). For example, energy storage operators use renewable-generation and electricity-price predictions (Ahmed and Khalid, 2019), portfolio managers rely on signals about future market conditions (Charpentier et al., 2021), and mobility and logistics systems use traffic, demand, and supply predictions (Haydari and Yılmaz, 2020). These predictions provide advance information about future uncertainty and can help decision makers adapt actions before the corresponding uncertainty is realized. Such problems are commonly modeled as Markov decision processes (MDPs), where a one-step transition kernel specifies the distribution of the next state given the current state and action. When the model is unknown, reinforcement learning (RL) provides methods for learning policies from data (Sutton and Barto, 2018; Puterman, 2014). Multi-step transition predictions provide additional information about upcoming transition realizations before actions are chosen. An action that appears less attractive under the base transition

model may offer a higher expected return given the prediction. Adapting actions to this information may therefore improve expected performance of reinforcement learning. However, incorporating such predictions into decision making is nontrivial. A direct approach is to append the current 𝐾 -step prediction to the state and solve the resulting augmented MDP. For probabilistic predictions, this can produce a high-dimensional, continuous state space. The number of discretized prediction states can grow exponentially with 𝐾 , which makes both policy representation and dynamic programming intractable. Moreover, optimal planning with multi-step transition lookahead can be NP-hard even when future transitions are revealed exactly (Pla et al., 2026a). State-space growth may also increase the statistical burden of learning, since the sample complexity of tabular RL scales with the number of state-action pairs (Azar et al., 2012; Gheshlaghi Azar et al., 2013; Agarwal et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023a). Beyond these computational and statistical challenges, it is also unclear how much predictive information actually improves decision making. In this work, we study discounted finite MDPs with transition predictions under a decision protocol we call 

block feedback . Time is divided into blocks of 𝐾 periods. At the beginning of each block, the decision maker observes probabilistic predictions for a subset of transition rows over the next 𝐾 periods. These predictions remain available throughout the block, while the current state is observed before each action is selected. Thus, actions can adapt to the realized state trajectory within the block while using the same multi-step prediction information. Within this framework, we study how multi-step transition predictions reshape the Bellman recursion and improve decision value, how to compute prediction-aware decisions efficiently, and how many samples are needed to learn a near-optimal policy. 

1.1 Main Contributions 

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. 

1.1.1 Low-Dimensional Bellman Representation 

We first establish a low-dimensional Bellman representation for reinforcement learning with multi-step transition predictions. A direct augmented-state formulation is exact, but it requires the value function to keep track of the currently observed prediction, leading to a large or even continuous state representation. In contrast, we show that under block feedback the optimal infinite-horizon value can be represented using only |S| state coordinates. Specifically, conditional on the realized prediction information, the decision maker solves an exact 𝐾 -stage dynamic program in which actions adapt to the states realized within the block. Averaging the resulting optimized value over the prediction distribution defines a Bayesian Bellman operator on the original state space. We prove that this operator is a 𝛾 𝐾 -contraction under the infinity norm and that its unique fixed point is exactly the optimal block-feedback value. The same recursion yields an optimal prediction-dependent state-feedback policy through backward induction. Thus, the policy may use high-dimensional prediction information online, while the Bellman fixed point remains low-dimensional.

1.2 Related Literature 

1.2.1 Model-Based RL 

Model-based reinforcement learning estimates the transition model and plans in the estimated MDP. Early work studied near-optimal planning under generative-model access (Kearns et al., 2002). For discounted finite MDPs, Azar et al. (2012) established minimax-optimal bounds for estimating 𝑄 ∗. Gheshlaghi Azar et al. (2013) also obtained policy-learning guarantees for sufficiently small 𝜀 . Subsequent work developed variance-reduced policy-learning algorithms (Sidford et al., 2018) and established the minimax optimality of planning in an empirical MDP (Agarwal et al., 2020). Li et al. (2020a, 2023a) overcame the sample-size barrier and extended minimax policy guarantees to the full meaningful accuracy range. Up to logarithmic factors, the minimax rate is eΘ |S||A|/(( 1 − 𝛾 )3𝜀 2), which characterizes the sample complexity of learning a near-optimal policy. Related work studies average-reward learning (Jin and Sidford, 2021). For episodic Multi-Step Transition Predictions 4

problems, efficient exploration has been studied in tabular MDPs (Azar et al., 2017) and in linear MDPs (Jin et al., 2020). Our learning problem retains this model-based approach but changes what must be estimated. Uncovered transition rows are estimated from environment samples, while covered rows are supplied by predictions observed during operation. Accordingly, the environment-sample complexity scales with the number of uncovered rows, eΘ |S|(|A| − |A − |)/(( 1 − 𝛾 )3𝜀 2), where A− is the set of actions covered by predictions. The learner also estimates the distribution of predictions from independent prediction samples. Learning-augmented RL uses 𝑄 -value predictions to guide decisions (Golowich and Moitra, 2022; Li et al., 2023b). Other work studies how transition-matrix predictions affect sample complexity (Lyu et al., 2025). Bayes-adaptive methods represent uncertainty about the model through a posterior (Duff, 2002; Ross et al., 2007; Guez et al., 2012). Our predictions instead provide additional information about upcoming transition realizations. 

1.2.2 Decision Making with Lookahead Information 

Lookahead is often used as a planning tool. Multi-step greedy methods use deeper planning to improve policies (Efroni et al., 2018, 2020; Winnicki et al., 2024). Adaptive lookahead adjusts planning depth to the current state (Rosenberg et al., 2023), while lookahead-bounded 𝑄 -learning constructs value bounds from sampled information-relaxation problems (El Shar and Jiang, 2020). These methods use lookahead for computation, without observing future transition realizations during execution. Predictions also guide decisions in optimization and control. Model predictive control repeatedly solves a finite-horizon control problem (Garcia et al., 1989). Online optimization uses predictions of future cost functions in problems with switching costs (Chen et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020b; Li and Li, 2020), with competitive guarantees also available for nonconvex costs (Lin et al., 2020). In linear-quadratic control, regret analyses study predictions of disturbances (Yu et al., 2020) and time-varying costs (Zhang et al., 2021). Perturbation analyses establish regret bounds for predictive control in linear time-varying systems (Lin et al., 2021) and extend to prediction errors and constraints in MPC (Lin et al., 2022). Predictive control in nonstationary MDPs uses predictions of future transition kernels and rewards (Zhang et al., 2025). More directly related are MDP models in which future information is revealed before action selection. Prior work studies the value of reward lookahead (Merlis et al., 2024) and develops Bellman equations and regret guarantees for one-step reward and transition lookahead (Merlis, 2024). Extending transition lookahead to multiple steps raises a computational challenge. Rolling predictions enlarge the information state, and exact planning can be NP-hard even with perfect predictions (Pla et al., 2026a). Merlis (2026) develops tractable policies with state-dependent block lengths under exact lookahead, together with block-boundary Bellman equations and online regret guarantees. Pla et al. (2026c) develop near-optimal planning and regret guarantees for rolling lookahead, with extensions to noisy predictions. Their planner runs in polynomial time for fixed lookahead depth, while its computational and storage costs grow exponentially with that depth. We study probabilistic transition predictions under block feedback, where predictions are refreshed only at block boundaries. The Bayesian value remains defined on the original state space. For any prediction horizon 𝐾 ,within-block planning requires 𝑂 (𝐾 |S| 2 |A|) operations per observed prediction, polynomial in both 𝐾 and the MDP size. We also establish minimax sample-complexity guarantees for policy learning and Bayesian value estimation. Exogenous information provides another connection. Exogenous-information models study learning with irrelevant, action-independent observations (Efroni et al., 2022), whereas exogenous-input MDPs use external inputs to drive the dynamics (Sinclair et al., 2023). Discounted MDPs with i.i.d. contexts observed before action selection admit an averaged Bellman equation on the original state space and minimax learning Multi-Step Transition Predictions 5

bounds (Pla et al., 2026b). Our model formalizes multi-step probabilistic transition predictions under block feedback, with residual transition uncertainty and partial action coverage. The same prediction guides several state-adaptive decisions within a block, rather than being refreshed every period. We develop the corresponding Bellman theory and discounted sample-complexity guarantees for offline policy learning and Bayesian value estimation. 

1.2.3 Information in Stochastic Decision Problems 

Our value analysis builds on the study of information in stochastic decision problems. Blackwell’s comparison of statistical experiments orders information structures (Blackwell, 1953). The stochastic-programming literature quantifies the value of observing uncertainty before decisions are made (Avriel and Williams, 1970; Huang et al., 1977). Information-relaxation methods obtain performance bounds by relaxing nonanticipativity constraints and adding penalties for anticipative decisions (Brown et al., 2010; Brown and Haugh, 2017; Balseiro and Brown, 2019). We instead quantify the value of transition predictions through their effect on the Bellman equation, particularly the ordering of expectation and maximization.

# 5 Sample-Efficient Learning under Block Feedback 

We now design a learning algorithm to realize the value of block feedback. Specifically, block-feedback Bayesian offline learning with online adaptation (BF–BOLA) learns a Bayesian value function offline from transition and prediction samples, and then adapts online to each prediction through the block-feedback recursion. We provide minimax-optimal sample-complexity guarantees for the resulting policy. We write 𝑉 𝜋 and 𝑉 ∗ for the Bayesian values of a block-feedback policy and an optimal block-feedback policy. We omit the prediction parameters when they are not being compared.

# 6 Numerical Experiments 

We use numerical experiments to evaluate the value of predictions and the learning performance of BF–BOLA. The experiments ask two questions. First, do transition predictions create value, and does block feedback recover the value lost by fixed-horizon commitment? Second, do the two data sources in BF–BOLA affect learning in the way predicted by the sample-complexity bounds? Figs. 1 and 2 answer these questions in controlled finite-MDP instances. Fig. 3 further illustrates prediction-aware decision making in a wind-storage control case study. 

6.1 Experimental Design 

Figs. 1 and 2 use a four-state production MDP with three actions and discount factor 𝛾 = 0.92. The states are unprepared, prepared for type 0, prepared for type 1, and completed output. Two operating actions prepare or execute their respective types, and the third action waits or ships completed output. Switching types requires reconfiguration, and failed execution preserves the existing preparation. Setup and execution costs are sampled uniformly from [0.015 , 0.025 ] and [0.045 , 0.055 ], respectively, and shipping revenue from 

[0.90 , 0.93 ]. Adding the same constant 0 .06 to every net reward places rewards in [0, 1] without changing policy comparisons. Each type has an independent, equally likely good or bad execution mode in every period. The corresponding success probabilities are sampled uniformly from [0.83 , 0.89 ] and [0.04 , 0.10 ]. At channel-noise level 

𝜂 ∈ [ 0, 1], each observed mode bit is flipped with probability 𝜂 /2, and the prediction reports the resulting posterior transition rows. The rows average to the same base kernel for every noise level and coverage set. Independence across periods ensures that ignoring predictions recovers the base MDP. Values and policies are computed by solving the corresponding Bellman equations. Fig. 1 uses 24 MDP instances, and Fig. 2 uses the first 10 of these instances with 12 independent training runs at each budget.

# 7 Conclusion 

This paper develops a Bellman framework for reinforcement learning with multi-step transition predictions. Under block feedback, predictions are used as temporary information within each block, while the long-run Bayesian value remains defined on the original state space. This yields a low-dimensional Bellman equation, a value-of-information analysis based on Bellman-Jensen and within-block feedback gains, and the BF–BOLA learning algorithm. The sample-complexity results show that prediction coverage reduces the Multi-Step Transition Predictions 21 

environment-sampling dimension and that near-optimal policy learning can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the corresponding Bayesian value function. Several directions remain open. Receding-horizon protocols provide richer information but generally require a larger information state, and developing equally sharp low-dimensional representations for such protocols is an important direction. More broadly, the results suggest that prediction-aware RL should be analyzed not only through model accuracy, but also through the timing, coverage, and statistical learnability of the predictive information.
