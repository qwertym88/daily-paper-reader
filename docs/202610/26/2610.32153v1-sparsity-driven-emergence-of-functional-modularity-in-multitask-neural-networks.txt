ABSTRACT 

Neural networks can learn multiple tasks, but how learning organizes shared computation into specialized and reusable components remains poorly understood. In this work, we show that sparsity-inducing regularization promotes functional modularity in multitask networks without predefined module boundaries. Using functional profiling in multilayer perceptrons and a lightweight transformer, we identified compact, task-selective subnetworks, termed functional tokens. Across rule-based task families, selectively retaining these tokens preserves their associated functions while reducing performance on other tasks. Such specialization develops preferentially in deeper layers during training, while earlier layers retain shared computation. Regularization strength and form shape module compactness and specialization, while task weighting alters functional allocation. Moreover, extracted functional tokens can be recombined through alternative pathways to reconstruct a learned composite computation without retraining, preserving their connectivity and learned weights. Our results suggest that sparsity-constrained optimization shapes not only parameter use but also the organization and reuse of learned computation. 

1 Introduction 

Deep neural networks have achieved wide success across perception, language, reasoning, and scientific discovery Bengio and LeCun [2007], Hinton et al. [2006], Goodfellow et al. [2016]. Despite substantial advances in architectural design He et al. [2015], Huang et al. [2018] and optimization Ruder [2017], the organizational principles that emerge during learning remain poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear how networks with distributed parameters and representations develop specialized functions while sharing and reusing computation across tasks. Biological intelligence provides a compelling example of such organization: functional modularity, characterized by partially independent yet interacting computational components, enables neural systems to support specialized processing, robustness, and compositional reuse across tasks Damicelli et al. [2019], Margalit et al. [2024]. Motivated by the computational advantages of modularity, previous machine-learning approaches have attempted to incorporate modular organization through explicit design. Neural module architectures introduce predefined functional components that can be selectively combined for different tasks Andreas et al. [2017], Braylan et al. [2015], while adaptive structural learning methods seek to optimize network architectures by modifying connectivity patterns Cortes et al. [2017], Liu et al. [2018]. Other approaches have explored structure-preserving constraints to incorporate prior organization into neural networks Celledoni et al. [2021]. More recently, sparse expert architectures have enabled large-scale neural networks to achieve conditional computation by routing inputs to specialized subnetworks Shazeer et al. [2017], Fedus et al. [2022]. Although these approaches demonstrate the utility of modular computation, they typically rely on architectural constraints, predefined components, or explicit routing strategies Sun and Guyon [2023]. arXiv:2610.32153v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 22, 2026 Modular organization, however, need not be specified in advance. Evolutionary studies have shown that modularly varying goals or selection to reduce connection costs can promote the emergence of modular networks Kashtan and Alon [2005], Clune et al. [2013]. More recently, work on multitask recurrent networks has linked emergent modularity to task demands, representational capacity, and connection-growth dynamics Wu et al. [2026]. Learned modular connection masks can also improve subsequent learning after weight reinitialization, particularly under capacity constraints Wu et al. [2026], demonstrating the utility of learned connectivity as a structural prior. Sparsity provides an optimization-level route to investigate how constraints on connectivity shape computation. Sparsity-inducing regularization encourages a network to use fewer connections without prescribing internal module boundaries. Sparse and structured regularization have been extensively studied for model compression, parameter selection, and pruning Wen et al. [2016], Scardapane et al. [2017], Ma et al. [2019], while analyses of learned features, task-selective representations and shared dynamical motifs have revealed structure in trained networks Agrawal et al. [2022], Li et al. [2024], Michaud et al. [2024], Yang et al. [2019], Driscoll et al. [2024]. The question here goes beyond network size: can sparsity help organize computation into task-selective components that preserve their associated functions and can be recombined with their learned parameters intact, without retraining? In this work, we show that sparsity-inducing optimization can drive the emergence of functional modularity in multitask neural networks without explicitly designed modular architectures. Across multilayer perceptrons and a light-weight transformer, we identify compact, task-selective subnetworks, which we denote functional tokens, that coexist with shared computation. Across task families, these tokens preserve their associated functions under selective retention. By systematically varying sparsity strength and regularizer form, tracking functional specialization during training, and analyzing endpoint representation geometry, we characterize how sparsity shapes depth-dependent functional reorganization. Furthermore, extracted functional tokens can be connected through different pathways to reconstruct a learned composite computation without retraining or changing their learned parameter values. These findings connect sparse optimization to the organization and reuse of learned computation, highlighting machine-learning systems as models for uncovering general principles of computational organization Levine and Tu [2024]. 

2 Results 

2.1 Task-selective functional modules emerge under sparse multitask learning 

To examine how computation is shared and specialized across tasks, we used a controlled setting where a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with three fully connected ReLU hidden layers was trained to perform five related Binary Sequence Calculation tasks, without task-specific hidden branches or explicit modular routing (Fig. 1a, Methods). The task set comprised four primitive arithmetic transformations, a + b, ab , a2, and b2, and one composite transformation a(a + b).Each shared input pair carried targets for all five rules, whose predictions occupied separate output blocks. The network was trained jointly on all rule losses from random initialization under sparsity-inducing Lγ regularization, with γ = 1 

as the default (Methods; Supplementary Note 5.1). To map functional contributions across tasks, we individually silenced scalar weights and measured the resulting change in each rule’s output segment (Fig. 1b). Each scalar weight served as an elementary token. Rule-specific masks were constructed at a fixed extraction quantile, τ = 0 .75 (Methods). Each selected weight was assigned an exact rule-set signature specifying the rules for which it met this criterion. We define a functional token for a rule or rule set by retaining the weights selected by the corresponding mask or union of masks while silencing the remaining weights simultaneously across all profiled weight matrices, including the output weights. Biases and other parameters outside the masking set remained unchanged (Methods; Supplementary Notes S3 and S4). The resulting weight assignments revealed a depth-dependent organization (Fig. 1c). Earlier hidden-layer weight blocks showed broader multi-rule support, whereas deeper blocks were enriched in sharper single-rule signatures and signatures involving fewer rules. This pattern was reproducible across independently trained networks. We next examined whether retaining these supports preserved their associated computations. Rule-wise relative accuracy was defined as the retained subnetwork’s accuracy divided by that of the corresponding full network on the same task. Single-rule functional tokens preserved substantial relative accuracy on their target rules, while performance on the other rules was reduced (Fig. 1d). This selective preservation therefore provided a functional test of the extracted supports distinct from the individual-weight output perturbations used to define them. 2.2 Functional modularity persists across task families, profiling methods, and model architectures 

Having identified task-selective functional modules in the Binary Sequence Calculation setting, we next asked whether this organization depended on task semantics or input format, profiling criterion, or model architecture. We first trained the same shared MLP architecture on two additional task families. Sparse Pattern Logic replaced arithmetic transformations with five Boolean-style rules, whereas MNIST Digit-Pair Arithmetic retained the arithmetic rule structure but presented pairs of digit images as inputs, coupling image-based input processing to arithmetic prediction (Methods; Supplementary Notes 5.2 and 5.3). Across independently trained networks for each task family, the extracted functional tokens preserved higher relative accuracy on their associated rules than on omitted rules (Fig. 2a,b; Binary Sequence Calculation baseline in Fig. 1d–f; Methods). Residual performance on omitted rules varied across task families, reflecting differences in computational overlap among the underlying transformations. Thus, the extracted subnetworks captured both task-selective and shared structure. We next replaced exhaustive output ablation with randomized Taylor output-sensitivity profiling, a computationally less expensive criterion that estimates the squared first-order output perturbation associated with removing a scalar weight (Methods; Supplementary Note S3). The row-normalized mean relative-accuracy matrix for single-rule Taylor-derived tokens peaked on the corresponding evaluated rules (Fig. 2c). Functional tokens extracted using the same score-quantile criterion retained high relative accuracy on their associated rules and showed reduced performance on other rules (Fig. 2d). Thus, the two profiling criteria recovered consistent patterns of target-task sufficiency and off-task selectivity, indicating that the observed modular organization was not specific to a single importance measure. Finally, we trained a TinyTransformer on the same five-rule Binary Sequence Calculation task set (Fig. 2e; Methods; Supplementary Note 2). Applying the Taylor-based profiling and retention procedure to scalar weight coordinates yielded functional tokens that preserved the corresponding target functions, with lower performance on omitted rules (Fig. 2f). The extent of performance reductions varied across tasks. This architectural control extended the observation of task-selective functional organization beyond fully connected MLPs. 

2.3 Sparse regularization shapes the compactness and specialization of functional supports 

Having shown that task-selective functional organization persists across the tested settings, we next asked whether sparsity merely accompanies modularity or actively controls its emergence. Specifically, we interrogated the effects of the regularization strength l and the regularization exponent γ in the multitask MLP. We first compared Lasso regularization strength l (γ = 1 ) with the non-regularized control ( l = 0 ), re-extracting functional tokens from each trained network using the same ablation-based, within-rule score quantile criterion. For each l condition, three complementary quantities were assessed: target-rule relative accuracy, the retained scalar weight fraction, and the importance-weighted modularity statistic M (Eq. (13); Fig. 3; Methods). Without sparsity regularization, the extracted functional tokens retained comparatively large and diffuse supports and showed incomplete preservation of their target functions. Introducing L1 regularization at l = 1 substantially improved target-rule relative accuracy while retained-weight fractions remained similar to those in the unregularized condition. Increasing the regularization strength to l = 5 markedly reduced these fractions, with target-rule relative accuracy remaining close to the full-network level (Fig. 3a). At l = 0 , the retained-weight ratio was close to one quarter, primarily reflecting the 75th-percentile extraction rule. The modularity score M provided a complementary measure of how selectively these weights contributed to different rules. M incorporates the magnitude of the profiled contributions and measures their concentrations on individual rules (importance-weighted normalized inverse participation, NIP; Methods; Eq. (13)). Increasing l from 0 to 5 raised M

in the second and third hidden-layer weight blocks, followed by a plateau at l = 10 (Fig. 3c). The first block did not show the same monotonic response. Higher M reflected more rule-concentrated importance profiles among the selected weights (Supplementary Note S3). We next asked whether these effects depended on the form of the regularizer. At fixed l = 5 , we compared Lγ penalties with γ ∈ { 0.5, 1, 2} (Methods; Supplementary Note 2). The L0.5 and L1 penalties produced compact functional tokens that preserved target-rule performance and showed stronger deeper-block specialization, whereas the L2 penalty yielded substantially larger retained-weight fractions, lower target-rule relative accuracy, and lower M in deeper blocks (Fig. 3b,d). A local support-recovery analysis provides a conditional framework for interpreting both the dependence on regularizer form and the layer-specific response (Supplementary Note 1). Approximating hidden-layer computation or parameter-Jacobian effects by a multitask linear surrogate, the analysis identifies conditions on signal strength, feature coherence, and local approximation error under which sparse task supports can be recovered or locally stabilized. 2.4 Depth-dependent functional specialization develops during training 

To determine how task-selective functional influence developed during optimization, we tracked the importance-weighted modularity score M at five saved checkpoints spanning 1,000–5,000 training epochs under Lasso strengths 

l ∈ { 0, 1, 5, 10 } (Fig. 4a). At each snapshot, scalar weights were Taylor-profiled and masks were recomputed within each hidden-layer weight block using the same rule-wise score-quantile criterion ( τ = 0 .75 ). Each condition comprised 30 independent runs. The trajectories were strongly dependent on both depth and regularization strength. Without regularization, median M

increased only modestly and reached 0.278, 0.375, and 0.277 in the three blocks at epoch 5000. At l = 1 , specialization accumulated more clearly in the second and third blocks, whose final medians were 0.440 and 0.481. Stronger regularization produced a more pronounced increase in the deepest block: between epochs 1,000 and 5,000, median 

M rose from 0.516 to 0.883 at l = 5 , and from 0.731 to 0.874 at l = 10 . In contrast, the second block attained intermediate values and approximately plateaued, whereas the first block remained comparatively weakly specialized. Thus, task-selective importance progressively concentrated in the deepest block rather than increasing uniformly across the network. This layer-dependent temporal separation mirrors the final depth-dependent organization: early transformations retained broader multi-rule influence, whereas the deepest weight block progressively consolidated task-selective importance. To characterize the representations accompanying this organization, we analyzed the latest saved epoch with γ = 1 

(Fig. 4b–e; Supplementary Note 6). Pairwise-correlation distributions were estimated at l ∈ { 0, 1, 5, 10 } from four independent runs per condition, whereas PCA and centered kernel alignment (CKA) analyses used 30 runs per condition over the extended sweep l ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 , 15 }.Pearson correlations between pre-activation unit responses changed in a layer-dependent, non-monotonic manner (Fig. 4b). The clearest increase in correlations near zero occurred in the first hidden layer at l = 1 . At stronger regularization, deeper layers instead redistributed more correlation mass toward intermediate values. PCA spectral entropy revealed a complementary change in the distribution of representational variance (Fig. 4c). Across the analyzed post-ReLU hidden and output representations, the pooled median entropy decreased from 3.279 at l = 0 to 2.280 at l = 15 , indicating that representational variance became concentrated into fewer dominant directions. Linear CKA further showed that sparsity altered the division of representational roles across layers (Fig. 4d,e). Similarity among hidden-layer representations remained high across the sweep, peaked at moderate regularization, and declined modestly at larger l. By contrast, pooled hidden-output CKA increased from 0.418 at l = 0 to 0.619 at l = 15 .Hidden representations therefore retained broadly compatible geometry while becoming progressively aligned with the rule-conditioned output space. Together, the training trajectories show that sparse optimization preferentially strengthens task-selective influence in deeper weight blocks. Endpoint representation analyses associate this organization with concentrated variance and stronger hidden-output alignment, rather than uniform decorrelation across layers. 2.5 Task-biased optimization reshapes multitask performance and functional allocation 

Having examined how sparse regularization shapes functional organization, we next asked whether preferentially optimizing one task under sparse regularization improves its performance at the expense of other tasks, and how these changes relate to functional specialization. Without changing the network architecture or training data, we introduced per-rule weighting into the multitask objective:

γ = 1 , and each weighting condition was compared with an equal-weight control across 30 runs (Fig. 5). The full-network response depended on the emphasized rule (Fig. 5a). Upweighting rules 2 or 5 improved mean accuracy not only on the target rule but also on all four non-upweighted rules. By contrast, emphasizing rules 3 or 4 improved target-rule accuracy while reducing mean accuracy on the other rules. Upweighting rule 1 reduced performance on both the target and non-target rules. Preferential task weighting therefore produced distinct outcomes, ranging from cross-task gains to target-specific gains accompanied by losses elsewhere. To relate these performance changes to functional organization, we re-extracted functional tokens independently from each trained network using the same ablation-based score-quantile criterion. We quantified each token by its retained scalar weight fraction and its specialization margin, defined as its target-rule relative accuracy minus its mean relative accuracy on the other four rules (Supplementary Note 7). Support changes extended across rule-defined tokens: emphasizing rules 2 or 5 increased retained-weight fractions broadly, whereas emphasizing rule 1 reduced them. Rules 3 and 4 produced small increases in their own target-token supports, with mixed increases and decreases in non-target supports (Fig. 5b). Thus, the observed performance trade-offs did not simply correspond to expansions of the target token accompanied by the contraction of the others. Specialization margins further distinguished functional selectivity from preferential task performance. Conditions emphasizing rules 2 and 5 yielded margins of approximately 0.16–0.19 across the rule-defined tokens, whereas margins were substantially smaller under the rule-3 and rule-4 conditions that exhibited cross-task performance costs (Fig. 5c). Changes in target accuracy and retained-weight fraction were positively associated across the five target-rule means (Pearson r = 0 .73 ; Fig. 5d) and across 150 repeat-matched target-rule responses ( r = 0 .78 , nominal p < 10 −4;Fig. 5e). Target-performance gains therefore tended to accompany larger selected supports, but were not restricted to conditions with high functional selectivity. The pooled association comprises five upweighted-rule conditions with 30 repeat-matched responses per condition (Supplementary Note 7).

3 Discussion 

Our results demonstrate that functional modularity can emerge under sparsity-constrained multitask learning, with compact, task-selective supports coexisting with shared computation. This organization recurs across the tested task families, profiling criteria, and architectures, while the MLP analyses reveal a systematic depth dependence. These findings complement work on explicit modular architectural design Andreas et al. [2017], Sun and Guyon [2023] and task-driven modular organization Wu et al. [2026] by identifying sparse optimization as a control of functional allocation and testing the preservation and reuse of learned computation. Our analyses distinguish support compactness, functional preservation and specialization. Under the tested conditions, sparsity-inducing penalties produced smaller and more selectively organized supports than L2 regularization. Drawing on insights from Lasso consistency Zhao and Yu [2006], the local support-recovery analysis distinguishes sparse support formation from magnitude shrinkage and identifies conditions on signal strength, feature coherence, and approximation error under which recovery or local stabilization is possible (Supplementary Note 1). Its dependence on effective coordinates offers a possible interpretation of the depth profile: nonlinear transformations may make task-relevant effects more readily separable in later blocks, while early transformations retain shared computation. Training trajectories show that task-selective importance progressively concentrates in deeper blocks rather than increasing uniformly across the network. Separately, endpoint representation analyses associate this organization with concentrated variance and stronger hidden–output alignment, alongside layer-dependent, non-monotonic changes in neuronal correlations. These observations connect functional allocation to questions of feature geometry Li et al. [2024] and learned computational structure Cagnetta and Wyart [2024], Chughtai et al. [2023], and resonate with neuroscientific accounts of modular specialization Damicelli et al. [2019]. Task-biased optimization further distinguishes preferential task performance from functional specialization. Conditions with cross-task gains exhibited higher specialization margins than those with target–non-target performance trade-offs. Support changes also extended beyond the emphasized token rather than consistently following a target-expansion, non-target-contraction pattern. Cross-task costs therefore cannot simply be attributed to induced over-specialization. Instead, these results motivate evaluating functional selectivity jointly with performance across the task set, rather than treating greater task priority as a direct means of creating more isolated modules. Methodologically, the functional-token framework connects importance profiling with tests of computational utility, complementing attribution-based interpretability approaches Marcinkeviˇ cs and Vogt [2023], Michaud et al. [2024]. Retaining selected weights while simultaneously masking all profiled weight matrices, including output weights, preserved substantial target function and reduced off-target performance; biases and other unmasked parameters remained unchanged. Compositional reuse provides a further test: extracted functional tokens from a common mother network reconstruct a learned composite rule without retraining while preserving their masks and inherited weights. This evaluates reuse of learned computation, distinct from testing the learning performance of subnetworks trained in isolation under modular connection masks Wu et al. [2026]. These recomposition experiments support the functional reuse of the identified tokens. Within the controlled task setting examined here, these properties support interpreting functional tokens as optimization-dependent and intervention-validated candidate sub-computations, while also showing that their reuse remains constrained by intermediate-interface compatibility and the selected computational decomposition. Several limitations define the scope of this interpretation. Rule boundaries, primitive operations, composite relations, and task-specific readouts are specified by design, leaving open whether comparable supports can be recovered when tasks are implicit or component roles depend on context. Extraction depends on the intervention target, score normalization, and mask-selection criterion, and scalar-weight analyses may miss higher-order interactions or redundant pathways. The theoretical analysis should likewise be read as a local support-recovery argument for simplified linear surrogates. A stronger account would need to connect feature coherence, exact sparsity, representation geometry, and training-time dynamics in a single framework, and to specify when sparsity promotes separability versus when it destroys useful shared structure. Extending the framework to larger models should also test stability across extraction settings and datasets, compare weight-, path-, and circuit-level interventions, and evaluate targeted functional editing beyond masking. 

4 Methods 

4.1 Model Architectures 

We conduct experiments using two representative neural architectures: a multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a lightweight transformer model ( TinyTransformer ). 13 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 22, 2026 

4.1.1 Multitask MLP 

The multitask MLP consists of three fully connected hidden layers, each containing 300 ReLU units (Fig. 1a). Each training sample comprises a shared input pair and labels for all selected tasks, and the output layer concatenates R

rule-specific predictions corresponding to R distinct tasks. In the default setting, R = 5 , and the tasks consist of related arithmetic or logical transformations applied to the same input pair. All tasks are introduced simultaneously and optimized jointly from random initialization, without curriculum learning, sequential task presentation, or task-specific pretraining. Hidden activations are used for representation analyses, whereas functional-token extraction is performed on scalar weight coordinates in the trained parameter matrices. Sparsity is imposed using Lγ regularization with 

γ ∈ { 0.5, 1, 2}, with γ = 1 used as the default unless otherwise stated. The task loss is averaged over the equally sized rule outputs, and the regularization penalty is the sum of the mean (|w| + ε)γ within each trainable non-bias weight matrix, with ε = 10 −8. Additional architectural and training details, including the exact objective, are provided in Supplemental Information, Section S2. 

4.1.2 TinyTransformer 

To test whether functional modularity generalizes beyond MLPs, we also consider a lightweight transformer encoder (Fig. 2e). The model embeds the input into a compact sequence representation, applies a lightweight transformer encoder, and maps the pooled representation to the same multitask output format used by the MLP experiments. The TinyTransformer control is trained on Binary Sequence Calculation. As in the MLP experiments, all tasks are trained jointly from random initialization, without curriculum learning or sequential task presentation. Functional-token extraction is performed on scalar weight coordinates. The TinyTransformer analysis includes 23 independent runs trained with batch size 128. Full implementation details are provided in Supplemental Information, Section S2. 

4.2 Task Design and Random Multitask Training 

We use several rule-based multitask families to test whether functional tokens emerge across different input formats and rule semantics (Supplemental Information, Section S5). In all cases, a single shared neural network receives one input sample and predicts the concatenated outputs of the selected rules. Unless otherwise stated, experiments use the first five rules of the selected task family as the default multitask setting ( R = 5 ). For Binary Sequence Calculation tasks, each input consists of two binary vectors of length P = 7 . The complete task family contains six arithmetic rules. Rules 1–4 are primitive transformations–addition, multiplication, a2, and 

b2–whereas rules 5 and 6 are composite transformations, a(a + b) and b(a + b), which reuse primitive arithmetic structure. The default setting jointly trains rules 1–5, placing four primitive tasks and one composite task in the same shared network. Sparse Pattern Logic tasks use the same paired binary-input format but replace arithmetic with elementwise logical operations. The full rule family contains AND, OR, XOR, NOT of the first input, identity of the first input, and identity of the second input; the default setting again uses the first five rules. MNIST Digit-Pair Arithmetic tasks mirror the Binary Sequence Calculation rule structure but use digit-derived inputs, testing whether the same extraction framework remains informative beyond direct synthetic binary variables. The TinyTransformer control uses the Binary Sequence Calculation setting with the same five default rules. For each independent trial, examples are randomly sampled and split into training and validation sets. Labels for all selected rules are generated on the same samples, and all task losses are optimized jointly from random initialization. Additional implementation details are provided in Supplemental Information, Section S2.

S1.6 Why the endpoint γ = 1 is included and why γ > 1 differs 

The endpoint γ = 1 is included because the Lasso penalty has a subgradient interval at zero: 

∂|b| b=0 = [ −1, 1] . (S.56) This interval is exactly what allows the inactive KKT condition in Eq. (S.21) to hold with bβj = 0 . Thus γ = 1 can produce exact zeros and support recovery under the irrepresentable and beta-min conditions above. For 0 < γ < 1, the derivative ddb |b|γ = γ|b|γ−1 sign( b), b̸ = 0 , (S.57) is singular as b → 0. This singularity makes zero locally stable against sufficiently small inactive perturbations, which is why the nonconvex proof uses local domination by P

|vj |γ rather than a convex KKT certificate. For γ > 1, neither mechanism is present. There is no Lasso subgradient interval at zero, and the derivative is finite at zero; for γ = 2 , it is exactly zero at zero. Small empirical correlations can then be represented by small nonzero coefficients instead of being thresholded to exact zeros. S2.2 TinyTransformer 

The TinyTransformer provides a lightweight non-MLP architecture for testing whether functional-token extraction depends on fully connected hidden layers. Each paired binary input is split into left and right halves, and both halves are projected into a shared 32-dimensional token space. The resulting two-token sequence is processed by two encoder-style self-attention blocks with feedforward width 64. The final representation is obtained by mean-pooling the two tokens and passing the pooled vector through a linear prediction head with RP outputs. In the robustness simulation, the TinyTransformer is trained on the same Binary Sequence Calculation rules as the MLP, using rule indices 0–4. The encoder is trainable ( train_encoder=True ), so both the token encoder and output head are optimized jointly. Functional tokens are constructed from scalar weight coordinates in the trained parameter matrices, profiled and retained with the same definitions used for the MLP experiments.

S5 Task Family Definitions

This task family applies arithmetic rules to pairs of binary input patterns. 

Input. Two binary vectors x, y ∈ { 0, 1}P are sampled as paired binary patterns. In the robustness simulation, P = 7 ,5,000 unique pairs are sampled without replacement from the 2P × 2P possible pairs, and the model receives the concatenated vector [x; y] ∈ { 0, 1}2P .

Output. Each selected rule produces a binary vector of length P . With R selected rules, the outputs are concatenated into 

Y ∈ { 0, 1}RP .

2. Multiplication: ab 

3. Square of a: a2

4. Square of b: b2

5. Mixed multiplication: a(a + b)

6. Alternative interaction: b(a + b)

S8 Compositional Reconstruction of Functional Tokens 

S8.1 Source networks and component functional tokens 

For each sparsity condition, a mother MLP was first trained jointly on rules 1–5 of the Binary Sequence Calculation task family under Lγ regularization, with γ = 1 . The sparsity-strength parameter l therefore denotes the Lasso strength used during training of the mother network, rather than a regularization term applied during recomposition. For each of four displayed regularization strengths and 30 independent trials, the saved 300-unit mother model was profiled again using scalar-weight ablation and the masking procedure described above. The 120 fresh analyses produced masks for addition, multiplication, square and composite rule 5. Each mask and its learned parameters were then held fixed while evaluating the direct rule-5 control and the two recomposition pathways, giving 360 evaluations. Recomposition does not retrain or fine-tune the components, and it does not merge their masks. Each functional token retained the architecture and learned parameter values inherited from its mother network, while parameters excluded by its functional mask were set to zero. The masks of different functional tokens were not combined through a union of scalar weight coordinates. Instead, each token remained a separate masked MLP, and recomposition was implemented by explicitly connecting the output of one token to the input of another. No parameters were retrained or fine-tuned during this procedure, and no additional L1 penalty or other optimization objective was applied after the component tokens were connected. The resulting reconstructions therefore constitute zero-shot compositions of fixed functional subnetworks. 31 A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 22, 2026

S8.7 Auxiliary training and extraction cost 

The auxiliary benchmark compares three jointly trained five-task models (seeds 830–832), those models followed by weight-token extraction, and 15 separately trained single-task controls (five per seed).
