## Abstract 

Neural architecture search (NAS) on a new task is expensive because every candidate must be trained to be evaluated. Cross-task NAS reduces this cost by reusing knowledge from previously searched tasks, but existing methods assume ei-ther dense performance tables on the source tasks, a sin-gle source task, or no source evaluations at all. In prac-tice, a team that has searched before holds sparse search histories : several past tasks, each with a small and differ-ent subset of evaluated architectures. We study cross-task NAS from such incomplete histories. We first show that stan-dard transfer heuristics break down in this regime, and that the two cheap sources of prior knowledge available to a practitioner—sparse historical logs and zero-cost proxies— fail on different targets: proxies on dense-prediction tasks, sparse logs on tasks that are negatively correlated with the sources. We then propose SHINE, a risk-robust transfer strategy that (i) starts the search from a portfolio covering the champions of each imputed source task and (ii) pretrains a pairwise ranker jointly on the sparse source tasks and on zero-cost proxies treated as pseudo-tasks, before adapting it to the target inside a weak-predictor search. SHINE is not uniformly better on average than a strong proxy-only ranker; its value lies in substantially mitigating the severe failures of either knowledge source while retaining compet-itive average performance. On TransNAS-Bench-101 with 50 target evaluations and 50 evaluated architectures per source, it mitigates the worst failure of each source (Micro autoencoding: worst-10% rank 290 with proxies alone vs .43; Macro room layout: 465 with logs alone vs . 45), and matches published methods in typical performance (rank of mean 2.00 on Macro vs . 2.25 for TRP and, on the four metric-compatible targets, better than Arch-Graph on both spaces). Over 13 cell-space targets, including seven trained from scratch on new datasets and four targets run only after the analysis plan was fixed, SHINE improves over the proxy-

## 1. Introduction 

Neural architecture search (NAS) has produced competitive networks for classification, dense prediction and many other vision problems [7, 18, 21, 33]. Its central obstacle remains cost: the quality of an architecture is only known after it has been trained, so each search spends most of its budget on evaluations. The cost is paid again for every new task, even when the search space and the training protocol are unchanged, because the ranking of architectures shifts from task to task [6, 31]. 

Cross-task NAS addresses this by transferring knowl-edge from previously searched tasks [5, 11, 13, 15]. Exist-ing approaches differ in the kind of source knowledge they assume. Benchmark-driven analyses and direct-transfer baselines assume dense source tables, i.e . every architec-ture has been evaluated on every source task [6]. Arch-Graph [11] pretrains a relation predictor on a single source task. TRP [15] avoids source evaluations altogether and pretrains on zero-cost proxies [1, 19]. None of these matches the situation of a practitioner who has searched be-fore: such a team owns sparse search histories —logs from several past tasks, each covering only the few dozen to few hundred architectures that happened to be trained on it, with little overlap between tasks. This paper asks how to best use such incomplete histo-ries when searching on a new task under a small evaluation budget. We find that the question is not answered by ex-isting heuristics. Aggregating source rankings into a con-sensus prior, a common default, performs poorly because high-performing architectures are task specific. Covering the champions of each source task is a far stronger strategy when source tables are dense, but its advantage collapses 1arXiv:2610.32179v1 [cs.LG] 23 Oct 2026 when each source is evaluated on only m=50 architectures (Sec. 3.1). Zero-cost proxies are a natural complement, as they are available for every architecture at negligible cost, yet we show that they are unreliable precisely on the dense-prediction tasks where transfer matters most (Sec. 5.3). We propose SHINE ( Sparse-History IN formed s Earch), a simple method that unifies the two cheap sources of knowledge. SHINE treats every sparse source task and every zero-cost proxy as a ranking task over the same ar-chitecture space and pretrains a pairwise ranker on all of them jointly. The search starts from a portfolio that cov-ers the champions of each source task after imputation, and it adapts the ranker on the target inside a weak-predictor search loop that ends with an exploitation phase. SHINE requires neither dense source tables nor task embeddings, and its overhead is negligible compared with a single ar-chitecture evaluation. We position SHINE as a risk-robust 

transfer strategy rather than a uniformly superior one: in typical runs it is on par with the better single knowledge source, and its benefit is that it substantially mitigates the severe failures that each source suffers on some target. Our contributions are: • We formulate cross-task NAS from sparse search histo-ries , a setting that reflects how search knowledge accu-mulates in practice, and show that consensus priors and source-portfolio heuristics fail in it. • We propose SHINE, which combines a source-champion portfolio with multi-task pairwise ranker pretraining over sparse source tasks and zero-cost pseudo-tasks. • We show that historical logs and zero-cost proxies fail on different targets, and that combining them mitigates the severe failures of either, reduces tail risk relative to prox-ies, while typical-case performance stays on par with the better single source. • On TransNAS-Bench-101 [6], SHINE matches pub-lished methods in typical performance (better than Arch-Graph [11] on the metric-compatible targets, on par with TRP [15] on Macro); in a pooled analysis over 13 tar-gets, seven of them trained from scratch on new datasets, with the analysis plan fixed before the last four were run, SHINE significantly improves over the proxy-only ranker at the task level in both mean and worst-case per-formance; MedMNIST-NAS-Bench [23] further validates the historical-log component. 

## 2. Related Work 

Predictor-based NAS. Performance predictors reduce the number of trained architectures by ranking candidates before evaluation [24, 27, 29]. BANANAS [24] showed that path-based encodings substantially improve predictors on cell search spaces; weakNAS [29] iteratively fits weak pre-dictors and progressively narrows the sampling region; and simple local search is a strong baseline on tabular bench-marks [25]. Pairwise and listwise ranking losses [4] are pre-ferred over regression because search only needs the order of architectures. SHINE uses the same search template but focuses on what the predictor is pretrained on. 

Cross-task and transferable NAS. TransNAS-Bench-101 [6] provides seven vision tasks over two shared search spaces and showed that direct architecture transfer is of-ten worse than random search. CATCH [5] meta-learns a controller with task context, MetaD2A [13] generates archi-tectures from dataset embeddings, and EMT-NAS [16] and KTNAS [32] exchange knowledge between tasks searched simultaneously. Arch-Graph [11] pretrains a pairwise re-lation predictor on one source task with Task2Vec em-beddings [2] and fine-tunes it on 30 target evaluations. TRP [15] pretrains a relativistic predictor on zero-cost proxy votes and fine-tunes it on a few dozen target eval-uations. Multi-Predict [3] and HELP [14] transfer predic-tors across tasks or devices with few samples. Unlike these works, we assume several sparsely evaluated source tasks and show how to combine them with proxies. 

Zero-cost proxies. Zero-cost proxies score untrained net-works from a single minibatch [1, 17, 19, 22]. NAS-Bench-Suite-Zero [12] found that their reliability varies strongly across search spaces and tasks. Proxies have also been used as input features of single-task predictors [26]. We confirm their unreliability on dense-prediction tasks and use them as auxiliary pretraining tasks alongside sparse source logs rather than as a ranking. 

Warm-starting from previous tasks. In hyperparameter optimisation, configurations that performed well on previ-ous datasets are used to initialise the search [8, 28], often as a greedy portfolio [10, 20], and source surrogates can be ensembled with ranking-based weights [9]. SHINE adopts the portfolio idea for initialisation and extends it to sparse sources by imputation. 

## 3. Cross-Task NAS from Sparse Search Histo-ries 

Setting. Let A be a finite search space of N architectures and let yj (a) denote the quality of architecture a ∈ A on task j, oriented so that larger is better. A search history 

consists of S source tasks. For source s we observe only a subset Os ⊂ A with |O s| = m ≪ N and the correspond-ing values {ys(a)}a∈O s ; the subsets of different sources are drawn independently. In addition, K zero-cost proxies 

z1, . . . , z K can be computed for every architecture at negli-gible cost. On a new target task t, an algorithm sequentially selects architectures to train; after B evaluations it returns 2the best evaluated architecture. No information about yt is available except through these B evaluations. 

Evaluation. Following Arch-Graph [11], we report the global rank of the returned architecture within A (1 is best), averaged over target tasks and random seeds. For compara-bility with published tables, which report the metric value of the returned architecture averaged over trials, we also report the global rank of that average value. Each target is held out in turn and the remaining tasks act as sources (leave-one-task-out). 

3.1. Why naive transfer fails with sparse histories 

Before introducing our method we examine two natural heuristics on TransNAS-Bench-101 (setup in Sec. 5). Con-sensus ranks architectures by their mean percentile rank over sources. Champion portfolio greedily selects architec-tures to minimise the summed regret over source tasks [20, 28], which effectively visits the best architecture of each source first. With dense source tables, the champion port-folio is remarkably strong: after 50 target evaluations its average rank is 11.0 on Macro and 15.2 on Micro, close to Arch-Graph (5.2 and 12.2), whereas consensus reaches only 90.5 and 44.2. Top architectures are thus task specific, and averaging hides them. However, when each source reveals only m=50 random architectures, the portfolio degrades to an average rank of 24.3 (Macro) and 44.3 (Micro), and a rule that selects architectures with high cross-source dis-agreement collapses entirely (122.6 and 516.2). A method for sparse histories must therefore generalise beyond the few observed source architectures and must not rely on the sources alone.

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Setup 

Benchmarks. TransNAS-Bench-101 [6] contains seven tasks derived from Taskonomy [31]—object and scene clas-sification, room layout, jigsaw, semantic segmentation, sur-face normals and autoencoding—evaluated on a cell-based Micro space (4,096 architectures) and a Macro skeleton space (3,256 architectures). Following Arch-Graph [11], jigsaw is used only as a source and the remaining six tasks are targets; for each target all other tasks are sources. For in-dependent validation we use the public MedMNIST-NAS-Bench [23], a tabular benchmark on four MedMNIST v2 classification tasks [30] (BreastMNIST, PneumoniaMNIST, DermaMNIST, BloodMNIST) in which every architecture is trained from scratch for 100 epochs with three seeds. Its Tiny space contains 768 architectures from three macro families and its Small space 5,016 architectures with ad-ditional templates, attention and down-sampling choices. Search observes validation AUROC, and we report the test AUROC of the architecture selected by validation. 

Protocol. Unless stated otherwise, the target budget is 

B=50 evaluations, the same as Arch-Graph, and each source task reveals m=50 randomly chosen architectures. Zero-cost proxies are the twelve proxies of NAS-Bench-Suite-Zero [12] computed on the object-classification task, as in TRP [15]. Five of them (fisher, grad-norm, grasp, snip, plain) use the labels of one minibatch of 32 im-ages; to avoid leaking target labels, we use proxies com-puted on scene classification when object classification is itself the target, and report the original setting as a con-trol in Sec. 5.2. (All Taskonomy tasks share the same im-ages.) In the real-training experiments (Sec. 5.7) the proxies never see target data. Structural encodings are BANANAS-style path encodings for the Micro cell [24] and per-layer down-sampling and widening indicators for Macro. Re-sults are averaged over 20 seeds that resample the source logs and the search randomness (10 seeds for the source-density sweep and the budget sweep). SHINE was de-veloped on TransNAS-Bench-101 and all hyper-parameters were frozen before the final runs; MedMNIST-NAS-Bench was not used during development. Code, source-log sam-ples and all real-training records will be released. The 20 seeds of a target share the same task, so seed-level tests only capture robustness to search randomness, not generalisation to new tasks. For TransNAS-Bench-101 comparisons we therefore report the number of targets (out of six) on which a method is better after averaging over seeds, a task-level paired Wilcoxon test (whose smallest attainable two-sided 

p-value with six targets is 0.031) and, where useful, a 95% hierarchical bootstrap interval of the mean difference (re-sampling targets, then seeds); ablation p-values are Holm-corrected. We also report the mean of the worst 10% of runs (worst-10% ) as a robustness measure. 

Baselines. We compare with the published results of single-task NAS (random search, REA [21], weak-NAS [29]), transferable NAS (CATCH [5], transfer variants of weakNAS, Arch-Graph [11]) and TRP [15]. The difference lies in the tail: the worst 10% of ZC-only runs average rank 34.8 on Macro and 152.1 on Micro, vs . 20.9 and 88.9 for SHINE, and its single worst run on Micro ranks 545th of 4,096 (SHINE: 185th). on Micro), but its sources differ: on Micro it appears on four of six targets and survives leaving out any single target (worst-10% 30.4–98.6 vs . 102.3–172.8), whereas on Macro it comes entirely from room layout, without which the two methods are on par (worst-10% 6.9 vs . 6.5). A hierarchical bootstrap over targets and seeds puts 78% (Macro) and 90% (Micro) of its mass on a lighter tail for SHINE, but its 95% intervals include zero; per-target numbers are in Tab. 8 (sup-plement); the pooled analysis in Sec. 5.7 adds seven real-training targets to increase task-level power. Sources-only fails catastrophically on Macro instead (worst 10%: 120.2, worst run: 778th). Combining both knowledge sources sub-stantially mitigates the severe failures of either. 

Proxy leakage control. With the original object-classification proxies, which see 32 target labels when object classification is the target (as in TRP), the results barely change: six-target means 4.30/17.70 (Macro/Micro) for SHINE vs . 5.42/25.21 for ZC-only, rank of mean 2.25/9.00 vs . 1.75/14.00. 5.4. Ablations 

Tab. 3 isolates the components of SHINE. On Macro, re-moving ranker pretraining or the portfolio worsens all six targets; with only six targets, however, no ablation remains significant after Holm correction at the task level, so we read these results as consistent directions rather than estab-lished effects. Removing either knowledge source sharply worsens the worst-case runs. On Micro, no component has a consistent effect across targets; notably, a ranker that uses the same features and portfolio but no pretraining is better on average (14.07, better on four of six targets), suggesting that on cell spaces the pretrained prior can be too rigid for some targets. From a risk perspective, however, no variant has a lighter tail than SHINE on both spaces: every variant that improves the Micro tail increases the Macro worst-10% by at least 68%, and we keep the configuration frozen be-fore the final runs rather than select one per space. Deciding adaptively when to trust pretraining is a natural extension; an exploratory online gate (Sec. B) did not reliably separate the two cases with only 50 evaluations. 

5.5. Target budget and source density 

Fig. 2(a,b) varies the target budget. On Micro, the gap be-tween SHINE and ZC-only is largest when evaluations are scarce: at B=25 the six-target mean rank is 39.9 vs . 57.3, although SHINE is better on only three of six targets. On Macro, ZC-only is better at B=25 (12.6 vs . 23.2), mostly because of room layout (49.5 vs . 108.7); on the four metric-matched targets the two are on par (5.8 vs . 5.6). At B=100 

both methods find near-optimal architectures (Macro 2.9 vs .3.4, Micro 12.8 vs . 12.6). Fig. 2(c) varies the source den-sity: SHINE is robust to m between 20 and 200, with the best Micro result at m=200 (14.7) and no consistent trend on Macro. 

Realistic search logs. So far each source revealed a uni-formly random subset of architectures. Real histories are bi-ased towards good architectures, because they are the trajec-tories of earlier searches. We therefore replaced each source log by the 50 architectures evaluated by a predictor-guided search (Extra-Trees weak predictor, same search template) on that source; for one seed on Macro, these logs lie on av-erage at the 65th–85th percentile of their task (full results in Tab. 7, supplement). SHINE is robust to this shift: its mean rank changes from 4.21 to 3.78 on Macro and from 17.84 to 20.63 on Micro, neither difference being significant even at the seed level ( p=0 .56 and 0.40 ), and it remains better than the ZC-only ranker in mean rank and worst-10% on both spaces (Micro worst-10%: 110.8 vs . 152.1). Biased logs mostly help the sources-only ranker on Macro (14.26 

→ 3.64, worst-10% 120.2 → 18.0), because past searches usually contain each source’s champions; with such logs, histories alone become as strong as SHINE on Macro. On Micro, the rank of the mean value on the four matched tar-gets rises to 11.00, slightly behind Arch-Graph (10.25).

## 6. Limitations and Conclusion 

Limitations. Our main experiments use tabular bench-marks, which allow exhaustive evaluation and controlled leakage-free analyses; our real-training validation (Sec. 5.7) is limited to pools of 300 architectures, short schedules, a single training seed and 64 ×64 inputs, and its Macro pools do not discriminate between methods. The number of tar-gets is small: six per TransNAS space, and the 13 pooled targets share four underlying datasets, so task-level tests have limited power and are not fully independent. Hyper-parameters were developed on TransNAS-Bench-101; we therefore validate on MedMNIST-NAS-Bench, which was never used during development. On Micro, SHINE still trails the reported results of TRP. Finally, SHINE assumes that past tasks share the search space of the new task; trans-ferring across different search spaces is left to future work. 

Conclusion. We studied cross-task NAS from sparse search histories, the form in which search knowledge ac-tually accumulates. Consensus priors and source portfo-lios that work with dense tables fail in this regime, whereas zero-cost proxies, though available everywhere, are unreli-able on dense-prediction tasks. SHINE unifies both kinds of cheap knowledge by pretraining a pairwise ranker on sparse source tasks and proxy pseudo-tasks and by initial-ising the search with a champion portfolio. SHINE is best understood as a risk-robust strategy: it substantially mit-igates the severe failures that each knowledge source has on some target, reduces tail risk relative to proxies, while retaining competitive average performance. Its advantage over proxies alone is small on the six TransNAS targets but significant at the task level when pooled over 13 cell-space targets, whereas histories alone are on par with it on cell spaces but fail severely on the macro space. Deciding adap-tively how much to trust each source of prior knowledge, which could also recover the typical-case gains of simpler variants on cell spaces, is a promising direction. We hope that framing transfer around incomplete histories will make cross-task NAS easier to deploy where past searches are al-ready available.

## A. Implementation details 

Encodings. For the Micro cell (four nodes, six edges with operations {zero, skip, 1×1 conv, 3×3 conv }) we use one-hot edge operations plus a BANANAS-style path encod-ing [24]: each of the four input-to-output paths is active if it contains no zero operation and is encoded by the one-hot index of its operation sequence ( 3 + 9 + 9 + 27 features), complemented by the number of active paths, the number of 3×3 convolutions on active paths and a parameter-count surrogate. For Macro (four to six layers of types 1–4, where even types double the width and types > 2 halve the res-olution) we use per-layer one-hot types, per-layer down-sampling and widening indicators, their cumulative sums, depth, and the mean positions of down-sampling and widen-ing layers. All features are standardised. 

Zero-cost proxies. We use the twelve proxies of NAS-Bench-Suite-Zero [12] (fisher, flops, grad-norm, grasp, ja-cov, l2-norm, nwot, params, plain, snip, synflow, zen) com-puted on object classification, rank-normalised to [0 , 1] .

Imputation. Each sparse source is imputed by ridge re-gression ( α=10 ) on the first-order encoding and all pairwise products, fitted to the percentile ranks of its m observed ar-chitectures. 

Compute. One full search ( B=50 ) takes about 77 s on an RTX 5090 shared by 12 processes; the ZC-only ranker takes 54 s and the tree-based variant under 1 s. 

## B. Exploratory: an online gate between pre-trained and target-only rankers 

The ablation in Tab. 3 suggests that pretraining helps on Macro but can be too rigid on Micro. After observing the fi-nal results, we therefore tested a prequential gate and fixed its rule and pass criteria before running it. At every round we fit both the pretrained ranker (A) and a ranker with the same features and initialisation but without pretraining (B).
