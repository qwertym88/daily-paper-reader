# SEVI: Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning 

Shaekh Mohammad Shithil and Lantao Liu 

Abstract —Real-world reinforcement learning has enabled contact-rich manipulation tasks to be trained directly on physical robots, but unsafe interactions during online learning remain a key barrier to practical deployment. We propose Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning (SEVI), a framework for reliability-aware safety intervention during real-world policy learning. SEVI introduces an Evidential Safety Critic that estimates both future constraint-violation risk and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward pass. During online learning, SEVI evaluates each task-policy action before execution; if the action is predicted to be unsafe or the prediction is insuffi-ciently supported by evidence, SEVI overrides it with an offline-trained recovery policy that guides the robot toward a lower-risk region. We evaluate SEVI on two simulated manipulation tasks and a real-world USB insertion task under in-distribution and distribution-shifted conditions. Across task-learning and safety-critic baselines, SEVI achieves the strongest trajectory-level safety prediction, the highest final task success, and the lowest unsafe-event counts. SEVI also reduces unsafe events by 13.4–57.1% relative to recovery-based safe RL while preserving low-latency execution-time filtering. The project page is available at: https://sevi-safe.github.io/ .

Index Terms —Safe RL, Manipulation, Safety, Uncertainty. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning (RL) holds the promise of enabling robots to acquire complex manipulation skills through trial and error. Recent real-world RL systems have shown that contact-rich manipulation tasks can be trained directly on hard-ware within a practical time budget. These systems typically rely on demonstrations, sample-efficient off-policy learning, learned rewards, and carefully designed controllers [1], [2]. However, training RL on physical robots remains challenging because exploration can lead to unsafe interactions, such as excessive contact forces, object damage, workspace violations, or failures requiring human intervention. Therefore, as real-world RL becomes increasingly sample-efficient, a central remaining challenge is to reduce unsafe interactions during online training. This challenge is particularly pronounced in complex tasks, where safety depends on perception, object geometry, contact dynamics, and the evolving task policy [3]. Existing real-world RL pipelines provide an effective foundation for online learning with prior data and off-policy updates. However, they do not explicitly address the autonomous intervention problem: determining when a proposed task action should be rejected and replaced by safe recovery behavior . One way to

Recovery-based safe RL methods instantiate this idea by switching to a recovery policy when a learned safety critic predicts future constraint violations [4]. A key limitation is that these intervention decisions rely on the reliability of the learned safety estimate, which may degrade under partial observations or in state-action regions poorly represented in prior data. Existing approaches to uncertainty-aware safety attempt to detect unreliable predictions under distribution shift [5]. World-model-based safety filters use learned latent dynamics to imagine future outcomes and identify potential out-of-distribution (OOD) failures [6]. Although this is a promising direction, it relies on accurate long-horizon prediction and sufficient coverage of safety-critical interactions. Although sampling-based uncertainty methods can improve reliability estimation, ensembles [7] and Monte Carlo dropout [8] require multiple models or repeated forward passes, which increases latency for real-time robot control [9]. To address this problem, we propose Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning (SEVI), a framework for uncertainty-aware autonomous intervention during online real-world RL. SEVI introduces an Evidential Safety Critic (ESC), which models safety prediction as evidence accumulation and estimates both future constraint-violation risk and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward pass. Unlike sampling-based uncertainty methods, the ESC does not require ensembles or repeated stochastic inference, making it suitable for low-latency safety decisions. During online training, SEVI eval-uates each action proposed by the task policy using both predicted risk and prediction reliability. If the action is pre-dicted to be unsafe or the prediction is insufficiently sup-ported by evidence, SEVI overrides the task action with an offline-trained recovery policy that guides the robot toward a lower-risk region as illustrated in Fig. 1. We evaluate SEVI on two simulated manipulation tasks and a real-world USB insertion task with a Franka robot. The experiments show that SEVI improves safety prediction under distribution shift, provides better-calibrated uncertainty than dropout and ensemble baselines, and reduces unsafe events during online real-world RL while maintaining strong task performance. Real-world RL. Efficient learning from limited physical interaction remains challenging in the presence of high-dimensional observations, sparse rewards, and practical reset constraints. Recent progress has combined demonstrations or prior data with sample-efficient off-policy RL, enabling poli-cies to be trained directly on physical robots within practical time budgets [1]. Sample Efficient Robotic Reinforcement Learning (SERL) provides a full-stack real-world RL pipeline that integrates robot control, reward inference, asynchronous training, and Reinforcement Learning with Prior Data (RLPD) off-policy updates for manipulation tasks [2]. These systems demonstrate that real-world RL can be practical when algorith-mic and system-level components are carefully integrated. For complex manipulation tasks, online training can still require long hardware sessions with continuous human monitoring, resets, or corrective intervention. This supervision burden limits scalability, especially when unsafe behavior can occur during exploration. SEVI targets this bottleneck by adding an autonomous safety layer that rejects risky or unreliable task actions and invokes recovery during online training. 

Safe RL. Classical safe RL commonly formulates safety through constrained policy optimization [10], while control-theoretic approaches use control barrier functions or Hamilton–Jacobi reachability to enforce safety [11], [12]. Predictive and latent safety filters extend safety reasoning to learned models and high-dimensional observations [6]. Such approaches often rely on predefined constraints, dy-namics models, or explicit safety specifications. Recovery-based methods are more closely related to SEVI, as they override risky task actions before unsafe states are reached [4]. However, these methods typically trigger intervention from predicted risk alone, which may become unreliable under par-tial observations, policy-induced distribution shift, or limited data coverage. SEVI additionally considers the reliability of the safety estimate when determining whether recovery is required. 

Uncertainty and Failure Prediction. Uncertainty estima-tion is widely used to identify unreliable predictions in learned robotic systems. Monte Carlo dropout and deep ensembles estimate epistemic uncertainty through stochastic inference or model disagreement [7], [8]. These methods are effective, but they require repeated forward passes or multiple models, which increases latency for real-time robot control. Recent failure-detection methods monitor deviations from successful behavior. For example, FAIL-Detect formulates runtime failure detection as sequential OOD detection and uses conformal pre-diction to detect failures from successful rollouts alone [13]. Such methods improve runtime awareness, but they mainly serve as monitors and do not directly provide autonomous intervention for online RL. Evidential learning provides a sampling-free alternative for uncertainty estimation. Instead of sampling over network weights or model instances, it models prediction as evidence accumulation and estimates uncertainty from the parameters of an evidential distribution [9]. Building on this formulation, our Evidential Safety Critic predicts both future violation risk and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward pass. This design connects uncertainty estimation to action selection: recovery is triggered when an action is either high-risk or insufficiently supported by evidence. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

We introduce Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning (SEVI), a modular framework for uncertainty-aware safety intervention during online real-world RL. As shown in Fig. 2, SEVI combines three components: an Evidential Safety Critic for risk and uncertainty estimation, an offline-trained recovery policy for corrective intervention, and a task policy trained with sample-efficient off-policy RL using prior data. At runtime, each action proposed by the task policy is evaluated before execution. If the predicted risk or epistemic uncertainty exceeds a threshold, the proposed action is replaced by a recovery action. This design separates task learning from safety intervention while modifying only the executed action.

C. Integration with Online Real-World RL 

In our implementation, the task policy πtask is trained with RLPD within the SERL framework [2]. The task learner uses prior demonstrations, off-policy actor-critic updates, and online replay in the standard SERL pipeline. SEVI leaves the task reward, actor objective, critic update, and replay sampling procedure unchanged. At each timestep, the task policy proposes an action atask  

> t

∼ πtask (· | st). The executed transition is stored in the online replay buffer and reused for task-policy updates. Thus, the task policy continues to optimize the original task objective, while the collected data are biased toward lower-risk interactions. SEVI is intended as a practical execution-time safety layer, similar in spirit to Recovery RL and runtime shielding. Since both the safety critic and recovery policy are learned from finite data, SEVI does not provide formal guarantees of zero violations. Instead, its goal is to reduce unsafe interactions during online real-world RL while preserving the sample efficiency and task-learning behavior of the underlying RL algorithm. V. R ESULTS

A. Evaluation Setup 

Baselines. We evaluate SEVI with two groups of baselines. The first group measures closed-loop safe task learning and includes SERL [2], SAC [15], SAC-C [10], SQRL-style re-covery [4], [16], SEVI without uncertainty, and full SEVI. SERL and SAC evaluate task learning without execution-time recovery filtering, while SAC-C evaluates constraint-penalized policy learning. The SQRL-style recovery baseline uses a point-estimate safety critic trained with the SQRL future-violation risk objective, but uses the same offline-trained recovery policy as SEVI. This keeps the recovery behavior fixed and isolates the effect of the safety critic used for intervention. SEVI without uncertainty uses the evidential critic but triggers recovery only from predicted mean risk, while full SEVI triggers recovery using both predicted risk and epistemic uncertainty. The second group evaluates safety prediction and uncertainty estimation independently of closed-loop task performance. We compare the ESC against a point-estimate SQRL-style critic [16], MC Dropout [8], and Deep Ensembles [7]. These baselines use comparable observation-action inputs and safety-label supervision, but differ in how prediction reliability is represented. This allows us to sepa-rately evaluate the closed-loop benefit of uncertainty-aware recovery and the standalone quality of the proposed safety critic. 

Platforms and Environments. We evaluate SEVI on two visual manipulation tasks in MuJoCo and one hardware task using a Franka Research 3 arm. In the Block Plucking 

task, the robot must remove the middle block from a three-block stack while keeping the top block supported by the bottom block as shown in Fig. 3. The policy observes two 

128 × 128 RGB views from front and wrist cameras together with a 16 D state consisting of the 3D TCP position, 3D TCP linear velocity, gripper open/close state, and the 3D Cartesian positions of the three cubes; each episode runs at 

50 Hz for a 300 -step horizon. In Transport Nut , the robot must transport a square nut and insert it onto a target peg. 6

Safety violations are defined as intervention-requiring events, including dropping the nut, moving it outside the reachable workspace, or causing insertion jamming due to large pose misalignment. For hardware evaluation, the USB Insertion 

task requires the robot to pick up a USB connector and insert it into a target slot while avoiding keep-out regions on the motherboard and excessive contact forces. The hardware policy observes dual 128 × 128 RGB views with a 19 D state consisting of the 6D TCP pose (position and orientation), 6D TCP velocity (linear and angular), gripper open/close state, 3D TCP force, and 3D TCP torque, and executes at 10 Hz for a 120 -step horizon. Distribution shift is evaluated through visual and task-configuration perturbations in simulation, and through distractor objects and cable-layout variations in the USB Task. All simulation training and real-robot experiments are run on a workstation with an Intel Core i9-14900KF CPU, 64 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB memory, running Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS. 

Evaluation Metrics and Protocol. In simulation, each method is trained with 5 independent random seeds, and the final policy from each seed is evaluated over 20 rollouts, yielding 100 evaluation rollouts per method for each task. For hardware evaluation, each method is trained in three independent 3-hour real-world online RL sessions on the USB Insertion task, corresponding to 9 hours of hardware training per method. For each run, the final policy is evaluated over 50 hardware rollouts, while unsafe events are counted over the entire online training session. Following real-world RL evaluation practice, we report the success rate of the final policy over 50 evaluation rollouts, while unsafe events and recovery interventions are counted over the entire online training session. This separates final task performance from the safety cost incurred during real-world policy improvement. We report three groups of metrics. Following prior runtime failure-prediction protocols [13], [17], we report trajectory-level TPR, TNR, balanced accuracy (BA), normalized detection time (DT), and inference latency. BA is computed for each seed as BA = 12 (TPR + TNR) , and then reported as the mean and standard deviation across seeds. For uncertainty quality, we re-port confidence AUROC/APR, OOD AUROC/APR, expected calibration error (ECE), and Brier score [18]. For closed-loop execution, we report task success rate, total unsafe events during training, recovery intervention rate, and cycle time. Closed-loop simulation and hardware results are reported as mean and standard deviation across five and three independent runs, respectively.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning (SEVI), a modular framework that decouples task-policy learning from execution-time safety intervention. SEVI combines an Evidential Safety Critic, which estimates future constraint-violation risk and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward pass, with an offline-trained recovery policy that intervenes when proposed actions are predicted to be unsafe or insufficiently supported by evidence. Across two simulated manipulation tasks and a real-world USB insertion task, SEVI improves safety prediction under distribution shift and reduces unsafe interactions while maintaining task performance. The current framework has several practical limitations. Recovery behavior is not explicitly optimized for local or motion-efficient correction, and repeated task–recovery switching may occur near failure boundaries during early stages of policy learning. In addition, the learned safety components do not provide formal guarantees of zero violations. Safety supervision is derived from trajectory outcomes rather than oracle labels for every state–action pair. In simula-tion, failures are detected using privileged task signals such as object poses, object drops, workspace violations, and insertion failures. In real-world experiments, trajectories are manually annotated by marking the first failure timestep. Once a failure occurs, all subsequent timesteps are labeled unsafe for safety-critic training, allowing the critic to learn future-risk targets through temporal propagation. For Block Plucking , success is achieved when the middle yellow block is removed from the three-block stack, the top red block remains supported by the bottom blue block, and the extracted yellow block is not dropped and is at least 7 cm away from the blue block in the xy -plane. A safety violation is labeled if the yellow or red block is dropped, or if the red block loses support or is displaced by more than 10 cm from the blue block in the xy -plane. For Transport Nut , success requires transporting the square nut and inserting it onto the target peg. Safety violations include nut drops, workspace departures, and insertion jamming due to large pose misalignment. For USB Insertion , success is determined using TCP pose thresholds of 2 mm in x, y , 5 mm in z, and 0.15 rad in orientation, optionally combined with a vision-based reward classifier. Safety violations are defined by keep-out-region breaches and excessive external contact force. For evaluation, unsafe events are counted separately from timestep-level training labels. A continuous failure segment may contribute multiple unsafe-labeled timesteps to the safety-critic dataset, but it is counted as one unsafe event unless the robot is reset and a new failure occurs. A. Evidential Safety Critic 

The Evidential Safety Critic (ESC) is implemented as a twin-critic architecture adapted for safety-risk prediction. Each critic takes the observation–action pair (ot, a t) as input and outputs the Normal–Inverse–Gamma parameters 

(mt, ν t, α t, β t), which parameterize the predictive distribution over the latent safety value and its uncertainty. For visual tasks, front and wrist RGB observations are encoded using a frozen ResNet-v1-10 encoder with shared weights. The resulting visual embeddings are concatenated with projected proprioceptive and action features and passed to the evidential critic head. For target computation and action filtering, we conservatively aggregate the two critics by taking the maxi-mum predicted risk and maximum epistemic uncertainty. C. Recovery Policy 

The recovery policy πrec is trained offline using TD3+BC on teleoperated recovery trajectories collected near task-specific failure boundaries. The recovery policy is not trained to solve the full task; instead, it learns local corrective behaviors that move the robot from near-failure configurations back toward safer regions where the task policy can resume execution. This local objective has a narrower state–action distribution than full task completion, making it feasible to learn from fewer demonstrations. The recovery objective combines value-based learning with behavior cloning regularization. XV. A DDITIONAL RECOVERY -B EHAVIOR EVALUATION 

To assess whether the benefit of uncertainty-aware gating depends on a particular corrective response, we evaluate two qualitatively distinct recovery behaviors on the real-world USB Insertion task: Hit-and-Recover , where the robot disengages from an unintended contact configuration before returning con-trol to the task policy, and Stuck-and-Recover , where the robot escapes or repositions from a stalled configuration before task execution resumes. Representative hardware demonstrations are provided in the supplementary video. To isolate the effect of the gating criterion, we keep the task policy, ESC, calibrated thresholds, recovery mechanism, and hardware setup fixed, and compare risk-only gating against risk-plus-epistemic-uncertainty gating. We perform three in-dependent evaluation runs for each condition and report mean ±standard deviation. As shown in Table XI, risk-plus-uncertainty gating improves task success and reduces unsafe events under both recov-ery behaviors. These results indicate that the advantage of uncertainty-aware gating is not specific to a single corrective motion: the gate determines when recovery is invoked, while the recovery behavior determines how the robot moves away from an unsafe or unreliable configuration.
