Abstract 

Robust reinforcement learning (RL) is critical for reliable real-world deployment, where policies may fail under diverse shifts and disruptions. Existing robust RL algorithms offer a rich set of mechanisms and principles, yet two major barriers limit their broader potential: limited algorithm reusability and an unclear empirical capability boundary. We introduce RobustRLlib , an algorithm-centric library and benchmark that addresses both challenges. It integrates 17 representative robust online, offline, and safe RL algorithms under a unified interface for substantially improved reusability, together with an enhanced evaluation platform spanning diverse real-world shifts and advanced humanoid and vision-language-action tasks. Systematic evaluation over 273 perturbation configurations provides an up-to-date capability map of current robust RL algorithms, revealing where they remain effective and where substantial robustness gaps persist. As an open-source, user-friendly platform, RobustRLlib offers a reproducible and extensible codebase for applying existing methods to new problems and developing new robust RL algorithms. The code and tutorial are available at https://robustrl-lib.github.io .

# 1 Introduction 

Despite remarkable advances in reinforcement learning (RL), its real-world deployment remains limited by robustness and safety concerns, particularly in high-stakes domains such as autonomous driving (Ding et al., 2023b), clinical trials (Liu et al., 2015), robotics (Li et al., 2021), and semiconductor manufacturing (Kozak et al., 2023). Policies trained in idealized environments can easily fail under distribution shifts or disruptions affecting various stages of the interaction, such as observations (Han et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021; 2020), actions (Huang et al., 2017), or environment dynamics (Iyengar, 2005; Pinto et al., 2017), induced by diverse factors such as sim-to-real gaps (Pinto et al., 2017), model uncertainty 2(Bertsimas et al., 2019), noise, or adversarial attacks (Klopp et al., 2017; Mahmood et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020). Robust RL therefore aims to develop policies that remain reliable under diverse types of disruptions and shifts, or their combinations in real-world scenarios (Arthur, 1991; Bertsimas et al., 2019; Tversky and Kahneman, 1974). A broad range of robust RL algorithms has shown strong performance in their specific type of disrupted application scenarios. More importantly, existing methods provide a rich set of robustness mechanisms and principles, including adversarial training (Pinto et al., 2017; Rigter et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021), dynamics randomization (Peng et al., 2018), robust value updates (Panaganti et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022), and data augmentation (Ding et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2020b), offering substantial promise across a far broader range of applications and shift scenarios. However, realizing this broader potential faces two fundamental barriers: algorithm reusability and sys-tematic characterization of the capability boundary . First, existing robust RL algorithms are fragmented across separate implementations and training pipelines, lacking a unified library or interface through which practitioners can readily try and compare a broad collection of robust RL methods on a new application. Second, the empirical capability boundary of current robust RL remains unclear: for a given application and distribution shift, we lack a systematic understanding of how well existing methods perform, when they remain effective, and where they fail. This is largely because the methods are typically evaluated in distinct environments and shift scenarios, making cross-method comparison difficult and performance beyond their original settings poorly understood. To address these two gaps, we introduce RobustRLlib , an algorithm-centric robust RL benchmark that brings together (1) a robust RL method library with reusable implementations to substantially lower the barrier to applying existing methods to new problems, and (2) a comprehensive evaluation pipeline across tasks and diverse real-world shift scenarios. Complementing existing robust RL benchmarks that primarily focus on developing evaluation tasks and shift scenarios (Dulac-Arnold et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2025; Zouitine et al., 2024), RobustRLlib focuses on making robust RL algorithms systematically accessible, comparable, and extensible, while providing a broader evaluation framework (Comparison in Appendix A.1). Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • A robust RL algorithm library. We integrate existing 17 representative robust online, offline, and safe RL algorithms, understanding and systematically organizing them by their learning methods, intended target shifts, and base algorithms for applicators’ easy reference. It provides a reusable foundation for applying existing methods to new applications and developing new robust RL algorithms. • An enhanced evaluation platform. Our extensible disruptor toolbox covers a broad range of dis-tribution shifts, including MDP-element shifts in observations, actions, dynamics, and reward/cost, as well as control-loop latency and task-semantic shifts. It supports diverse shift modes, including stochas-tic, adversarial, and parametric perturbations with non-stationary schedules, enabling both isolated and compound-shift evaluation. The same disruptor interface further extends to massively parallel training in Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025) and advanced vision–language–action (VLA) robotic tasks (Intelligence et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). • An up-to-date capability map of robust RL algorithms. Evaluation over 273 perturbation con-figurations reveals that robust methods outperform standard references on average, but both groups deteriorate markedly as shifts intensify. A method’s claimed target shift does not reliably predict its empirical strengths, gains vary across tasks and shift sources, with online methods showing larger incre-mental effects than offline ones. By connecting diverse algorithm features to their measured strengths, this map provides guidance for designing robust RL methods for deployment shifts. 

# 2 Related Work 

Robust RL problems under diverse deployment shifts. Robust RL addresses deployment shifts affecting different components and stages of the agent–environment interaction, and prior work can be categorized accordingly. Oikarinen et al. (2021); Zhang et al. (2021; 2020) address observation shift , which perturbs policy inputs through sensing errors or external attacks. Tan et al. (2020); Tessler et al. (2019) address action shift , which perturbs or replaces executed actions. Dynamics shift , involving uncertainty in 3or changes to the transition kernel, is addressed either by optimizing worst-case performance over ambiguity sets (Goyal and Grand-Clement, 2023; Iyengar, 2005; Nilim and El Ghaoui, 2005; Shi and Chi, 2024; Shi et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Wiesemann et al., 2013; Xu and Mannor, 2010) or by introducing random or adversarial perturbations to physical parameters and external forces (Mankowitz et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2018; Pinto et al., 2017; Rajeswaran et al., 2016). Ma et al. (2019); Queeney and Benosman (2023) address 

reward and cost shifts through constraint-aware learning, and reward-poisoning analysis. Beyond these MDP components, Bouteiller et al. (2021); Peng et al. (2018); Tan et al. (2018) address latency shift , involving delays or irregularities in observation and action timing. Ding et al. (2023a); Tobin et al. (2017) address 

semantic shift , involving changes in appearance or task-relevant relationships among visual cues, objects, and goals. In practice, real-world scenarios may involve multiple shifts, such as changes in dynamics parameters, process noise, and observation noise (Mandlekar et al., 2017), or non-stationary shifts that evolve during deployment (Lecarpentier and Rachelson, 2019). Since robustness to one shift does not necessarily extend to others or their combinations, this motivates developing algorithms for co-occurring shifts and evaluating them under a unified framework. 

Robust RL algorithm taxonomy. Robust RL algorithms promote robustness through two main ap-proaches: modifying the learning objective or algorithm ( learner-centric ) and modifying the training data distribution ( data-centric ). For robust online RL , learner-centric methods seek robust policies through op-timization against an adversary (Zhang et al., 2021; 2020), worst-case robust value objectives (Li et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2022), smoothing the policy over perturbed observations (Sun et al., 2024), or causal-inspired data augmentation to handle spurious correlated shifted scenarios (Ding et al., 2023a). Data-centric methods can collect trajectories by interacting with shifted environments through physical parameter ran-domization (Peng et al., 2018), model ensembles (Mankowitz et al., 2019; Rajeswaran et al., 2016; Tanabe et al., 2022), or by applying adversarial disturbances (Pinto et al., 2017). For robust offline RL , learner-centric methods address model uncertainty and limited data coverage through robust Bellman updates over an uncertainty set of transitions (Blanchet et al., 2023; Panaganti et al., 2022; 2023), and mitigate value overestimation by smoothness regularization over perturbed states (Yang et al., 2022). Data-centric methods instead augment or reweight fixed datasets by generating samples from adversarial environments (Bhardwaj et al., 2023; Rigter et al., 2022), mixing diffusion-generated synthetic data with real data (Lu et al., 2023), or downweighting corrupted samples (Yang et al., 2024). In particular, generative-model methods, as a data-centric subfamily, learn dynamics models from offline data and generates plausible future transitions or multi-step trajectories for robust policy learning (Berdica et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024; Foffano et al., 2026). Furthermore, robust safe RL extends robust policy learning to jointly maintain task performance and satisfy cost constraints under uncertainty (Liu et al., 2022; Queeney and Benosman, 2023; Wang et al., 2022). In this work, RobustRLlib integrates 17 representative algorithms spanning robust online, offline, and safe RL, systematically organizes them by algorithm features, and evaluates them across diverse shift sources and modes under a unified protocol. It provides a reusable foundation for applying existing methods to new applications and developing new robust RL algorithms. 

Related RL benchmarks. Existing RL benchmarks can be broadly categorized into data-oriented bench-marks , which primarily provide fixed datasets for algorithm development (Fu et al., 2020; Gulcehre et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2022); task-oriented benchmarks , which offer standardized tasks and environments for de-veloping and evaluating RL algorithms, ranging from continuous control to advanced robotics (Duan et al., 2016; Ji et al., 2023; Pumacay et al., 2024; Sferrazza et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2020a); and algorithm-oriented libraries and benchmarks , which provide reproducible implementations, training infrastructure, and pipelines for existing RL algorithms, serving as a foundation for downstream applications, fair comparisons, and fur-ther algorithm development (Hu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2018; Raffin et al., 2021; Weng et al., 2022). In particular, for robust RL, closely related task-oriented benchmarks include RRLS, which fo-cuses on continuous control (Zouitine et al., 2024), and Robust-Gymnasium, which covers a broader range of control and robotics tasks (Gu et al., 2025). Complementing these efforts, this work introduces RobustRLlib ,the first unified algorithm-oriented library and benchmark for robust RL. It provides a reusable foundation for applying existing methods to new applications and developing new robust RL algorithms, while enabling easy comparisons with existing methods. In addition, RobustRLlib extends the considered deployment shifts to include latency and semantic shifts, and expands evaluation to advanced high-dimensional robotic tasks. 4There exist other benchmarks that address related settings, such as off-dynamics transfer, real-world oper-ating stressors, and adversarial robustness (Dulac-Arnold et al., 2020; Lyu et al., 2024; Mohan et al., 2026). 

Sim-to-real gap in robotics. The sim-to-real gap is a longstanding challenge in robotics, where mis-matches between simulation and real-world hardware can substantially degrade the performance of policies trained in simulation. Prior work addresses this gap through various approaches, including dynamics ran-domization (Andrychowicz et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2018), actuator and latency modeling (Tan et al., 2018), and online adaptation to changing dynamics (Kumar et al., 2021). These considerations have been explored in whole-body humanoid control (Gu et al., 2024; He et al., 2024), safety-critical robotics (As et al., 2026), real-time VLA policies that overlap execution and inference (Black et al., 2026), and demonstration-driven policies for contact-rich manipulation (Sharma et al., 2026). However, many existing approaches are task-specific and address particular sources of sim-to-real mismatch. In this work, we provide a unified robust RL algorithm library with 17 representative algorithms covering diverse tasks and sim-to-real gaps. These algorithms and their underlying design principles have the potential to support broader robotics applications while reducing manual iteration before hardware deployment. 

# 3 RobustRLlib : An Algorithm-Centric Robust RL Benchmark 

RobustRLlib is organized around algorithm attribution : it records how a robust RL method changes learning, which shift the method claims to address, and which base algorithm realizes the method. The disruptor toolbox then instantiates the claimed target shift independently of the base learner, while the evaluation platform measures both common robustness and method-specific specialization. This separation makes each result traceable to an algorithmic feature and an evaluation condition rather than treating robust RL as a single undifferentiated class.

3.1 Robust RL algorithm library construction 

The library has three tracks: offline RL learns from fixed data, online RL learns through continued environ-ment interaction, and safe RL adds cost constraints, including robust-safe methods implemented here in the online setting. Within the offline and online tracks, robustness primarily enters through two routes: learner-centric methods change how experience is interpreted or optimized, while data-centric and environment-centric methods change the distribution available for learning. 3.1.2 Robust online RL algorithms 

Online robust RL methods preserve continued interaction with the environment but differ in where robustness enters training. We distinguish two primary routes. Learner-centric methods modify the policy input and learning objective while retaining the nominal physical transition law. Environment-centric methods instead interact with deliberately perturbed environments to collect training rollouts. Whether a method updates on-policy or off-policy remains an important implementation choice, so we record it through the base algorithm. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) and SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) serve as the standard base algorithms in our experiments. 

Learner-centric methods. These methods train the policy to make stable decisions using adversarial observations, local regularization, or causal counterfactual experience. • ATLA: observation adversary. ATLA (Zhang et al., 2021) alternates updates between the policy and a learned adversary that perturbs its observations. • ATLA-SA: state-adversarial regularization. ATLA-SA (Zhang et al., 2021) adds recurrent history and SA-MDP regularization to stabilize decisions under perturbed observations. • RSC: causal counterfactual replay. RSC (Ding et al., 2023a) infers causal dependencies and rewrites experience counterfactually to reduce reliance on spurious semantic features. 

Environment-centric methods. These methods build robustness through training trajectories generated under varied physical environments or external disturbances. • RARL: external force adversary. RARL (Pinto et al., 2017) perturbs the environment with bounded forces selected by an adaptive adversary. The protagonist and adversary train in a two-player game, concentrating experience on the policy’s current vulnerabilities. • DR: domain randomization. DR (Peng et al., 2018) samples physical parameters across training episodes, exposing the policy to a prescribed envelope of transition dynamics. 

3.1.3 Robust safe RL algorithms 

Robust safe RL methods seek to maximize worst-case expected return while ensuring cost-constraint satis-faction across a prescribed dynamics uncertainty set. • RAMU: risk-sensitive reward and cost targets. RAMU (Queeney and Benosman, 2023) applies risk measures over transition-model uncertainty to both reward and cost Bellman targets. • SPiDR: robust safety via penalized costs. SPiDR (As et al., 2026) measures disagreement among next-state predictions from domain-randomized models and penalizes uncertain safety outcomes. 

3.2 Expandable disruptor toolbox 

Robust RL preserves the underlying task objective of standard RL by maximizing cumulative return but extends the single nominal MDP into a family of robust MDP with different types of shifts. These shifts intervene in different components of the agent–environment interaction, such as observations, actions, tran-sition dynamics, rewards or costs, latency, and task semantics. The resulting robust RL problem therefore seeks a policy that performs reliably across these shifted interaction conditions.

3.2.2 Shift modes 

A mode says how an intervention is built, and is one field of the specification tuple (source, factor, mode, magnitude, resampling rule, optional schedule, seed): the source says where the intervention enters the 8agent–environment interaction (Section 3.2.1), the factor says what it perturbs (e.g., gravity or friction), the 

magnitude says how far it departs from nominal, the resampling rule and optional schedule say how it evolves 

within an episode, and the seed makes its random draws reproducible. Each instantiated tuple defines one 

perturbation configuration . We retain the stochastic interface of Robust-Gymnasium (Gu et al., 2025), and extend it with several new modes as below: • Stochastic. Samples signal errors or parameters from specified distributions. • Adversarial. Computes bounded perturbations from the current states to counter the policy. • Parametric. Applies a specified assignment, scale, offset, or translation at a severity. • Non-stationary. Varies a mode’s severity over time or triggers intermittent disturbances. • Composition. Stacks compatible shifts into reproducible multi-shift scenarios. Together, these controls support both individual shift and compound shift scenarios. The appendix A.2 provides the operators, magnitudes, resampling rules, and composition procedure. 

3.2.3 Expandable to advanced robotics tasks 

Because a shift is fully described by its specification tuple, a new backend needs only one adapter to the simulator’s own primitives. We validate this on two deployment-oriented platforms. For Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025), the adapter realizes transition, action, and latency shifts on the vectorized GPU environment and exposes a joint-limit cost channel for every method’s evaluation, so unconstrained and constrained learners are compared on one safety quantity. For a VLA policy (Intelligence et al., 2025) fine-tuned on the bimanual demonstrations (Zhang et al., 2026), the adapter injects appearance, proprioception, action, latency, and physical shifts into a closed-loop simulator.

4.3 Extension to advanced robotic tasks 

We extend the benchmark from standard control to advanced robotics tasks, attaching the same disruptor interface through task-specific adapters, covering a high-dimensional humanoid, a contact-rich manipulator and a VLA control task. 

Robust safe methods can ensure both performance and constraint satisfaction under shifts. 

We compare PPO, DR-PPO (Schwarke et al., 2025) and ATLA-PPO against the robust safe methods RAMU-PPO and SPiDR-PPO on Unitree G1 locomotion and Franka drawer manipulation in Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025). Figure 8 shows the two highest-scoring policies on G1 survive about a quarter under action delay; while on the drawer DR-PPO matches SPiDR-PPO’s 99 .6% performance with 0% of its episodes inside the joint-limit cost budget against SPiDR-PPO’s 84%. The results indicate that only robust safe methods especially SPiDR-PPO, keep both metrics high on both tasks, so a higher performance is not the evidence that a method is ready for sim-to-real deployment. 

Performance sensitivity differs sharply across shift sources, with semantic shifts more impactful in VLA tasks. We fine-tune π0.5 on the insert-mouse-battery task (Figure 11) and drive it under eight shift factors. At the largest measured level the policy still remains 0 .95 of its nominal performance under gravity but only 0 .33 under image blur. Blur at its largest level moves only 13% of the image but drops the policy to a third of nominal, while image brightness moves the same frames at seven times and costs almost nothing. # 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce RobustRLlib , an algorithm-centric library and evaluation platform for robust RL under deployment shifts. It integrates 17 robust RL algorithms with 6 standard RL baselines, organized by algorithm feature, claimed target shift, and base algorithm. A shared interface evaluates MDP-element shifts alongside control-loop latency and task semantics through individual and compound shift scenarios and extends to advanced robotics tasks. Under a unified evaluation over 273 perturbation configurations, robust methods as a group outperform standard RL references. Individual and compound shift evaluations further reveal each algorithmic feature’s strengths and capability boundaries. Therefore, RobustRLlib provides a platform to support more comprehensive evaluation and guide the design of future robust RL methods.

A.2.1 Shift source: Latency 

We treat latency as a composable deployment-shift source within this unified robust RL evaluation frame-work. Specifically, we expose observation delay, action delay, and control-period variation through ˜ ot =20 I(Ht, t − δo), ˜at = at−δa , s t+1 ∼ P (κt)0 (· | st, ˜at), capturing delayed sensing, delayed execution, and variable control rates, respectively. The three operators act at different points of one decision and are implemented accordingly. • Observation latency. A timestamped history Ht is queried at t − δo using linear interpolation, so sub-step delays are not rounded away (Tan et al., 2018). If the requested time precedes reset, the history returns the reset observation. • Action latency. An integer buffer executes the command from δa control steps earlier, ˜ at = amax( t−δa,0) ,using the initial command to warm-start the buffer. • Control-period variation. Following Peng et al. (2018), we set ∆ tt = ∆ t0 + ξt, where ξt ∼ Exp( λ); 

λ is drawn per episode and ξt per decision. One command is held for κt = round(∆ tt/h sim ) simulator substeps, changing its duration time. 

A.2.2 Operator details 

• Stochastic. Signal noise is redrawn per step, whereas bias persists for an episode and dynamics param-eters are sampled at reset. loguniform samples in log space to balance reciprocal multipliers. Latency draws act on command buffers, timestamped observations, or action-hold duration. • Adversarial. The observation operator searches for an input that changes the current actor’s output within radius ϵ; it requires a differentiable feed-forward actor. The three action operators respectively oppose the chosen command, rotate a 2-D command, or suppress its goal-directed component. Commands are clipped to the action space before perturbation. • Parametric. These operators apply a specified value rather than draw one. set handles parameters whose nominal value is zero, while translate expresses a displacement in physical units. Semantic operators change appearance or task referents. • Non-stationary. A schedule changes the shift magnitude over time, whereas latency operators change when signals enter the control loop. push creates an intermittent event. For feedback delay , interval release sums rewards over k steps, while shifted release delivers each reward k steps later; both flush pending feedback at episode end. • Composition. An ordered list specifies wrapper order explicitly. Each specification uses its own seeded random stream, making compound conditions reproducible.

B.2 Evaluation grids 

The main robust RL algorithms evaluation grid varies one shift factor at a time across online and offline tasks, grouping its non-nominal settings into four task–factor-specific shift levels (Table 4). The grid is fixed and same for every algorithm: 220 non-nominal conditions, 110 online and 110 offline, plus the nominal setting of every factor. The individual shift grid instead varies intervention modes within a shift source, allowing method-level specialization to be diagnosed (Table 5). Finally, the compound grid activates physical, control, sensing, and timing factors jointly in reference-derived Hopper and Pusher tasks (Table 6). Thus, the three grids measure robustness breadth, shift-specific behavior, and robustness under interacting deployment factors, respectively. The advanced tasks bring their own grids, and both place every method on the same conditions. On Isaac Lab the conditions are positioned relative to the reference envelope (He et al., 2024) of Tables 8 and 9: G1 has 31 per seed, six inside that envelope, eighteen on its boundary, five beyond it, and flat and rough nominal. Drawer has 16, two controls and fourteen beyond the reference ranges, each moving one axis while the rest keep their training randomization. The VLA evaluation tests eight shift factors across five shift sources (Figure 9). Each factor has its own Q1–Q4 ladder, ordered outward from its nominal setting. # D Algorithms 

The library holds six standard RL baselines without any robust design, thirteen robust RL algorithms, and two robust safe algorithms. They are organized by the taxonomy of Section 3.1. 

D.1 Offline algorithms 

D.1.1 Standard RL baselines. IQL IQL (Kostrikov et al., 2021) avoids querying out-of-distribution actions altogether: a value function is fitted to the critic by expectile regression, the twin critics bootstrap from that value function, and the actor is extracted by advantage-weighted regression. 

TD3+BC TD3+BC (Fujimoto and Gu, 2021) adds a behavior-cloning term to the TD3 actor loss and normalizes states. The term is balanced against the critic by dividing by the mean absolute Q over the batch. 

MOPO MOPO (Yu et al., 2020b) learns an ensemble dynamics model, branches short rollouts from dataset states, and penalizes the rollout reward by the model’s predicted uncertainty before running SAC on the mixture of real and model data. 

SynthER SynthER (Lu et al., 2023) fits a diffusion model to logged transitions and trains an unmodified offline learner on a much larger sample from it. We count it as data augmentation rather than a generative method: it samples individual transitions to enlarge the dataset, where FWM and PLR-PVL sample multi-step futures conditioned on the current state and turn them into critic targets. 27 D.1.2 Learner-centric algorithms. RFQI RFQI (Panaganti et al., 2022) replaces the fitted-Q target by a worst-case Bellman backup over a total-variation uncertainty set of radius ρ around the empirical transitions; the inner maximization is solved through a dual variable fitted per batch, and the actor and critic follow the release’s BCQ-style architecture. 

RORL RORL (Yang et al., 2022) makes an SAC learner conservative through smoothing: the critic and policy are regularized to vary little within an ϵ-ball of dataset states, and Q-values at perturbed states are penalized by the disagreement of a ten-critic ensemble. 

ATLA-IQL The offline ATLA adaptation (Zhang et al., 2021) keeps ATLA’s principle, a learned observa-tion adversary alternated with the learner, but the adversary is trained by a differentiable objective on the critic by minimizing the minimum Q at the policy’s action under the perturbed state since no environment is available. 

D.1.3 Data-centric algorithms. RSC-IQL RSC-IQL (Ding et al., 2023a) applies RSC’s counterfactual rewriting to logged data where a causal mask over state dimensions is fitted from the dataset, and transitions are rewritten by permuting or perturbing the dimensions the mask marks as non-causal. 

RAMBO RAMBO (Rigter et al., 2022) augments the dataset with short rollouts from a learned dynamics ensemble and replaces the uncertainty penalty by adversarial training of the model: the ensemble is updated to lower the critic’s value of the policy’s transitions while a maximum-likelihood term keeps it close to the data. 

D.1.4 Generative model. ROMB ROMB (Chen et al., 2025) adapts a learned world model against the policy under a constrained maximin objective: the model is pushed toward transitions that lower the policy’s value while a supervised loss keeps it on the data, and the policy is trained on rollouts from the adapted model. 

FWM FWM transfers the multi-step future modeling of Diffusion World Model (Ding et al., 2024) to a flow-matching (Lipman et al., 2022) world model: instead of a one-step bootstrap, the critic regresses an 

h-step return whose future is sampled from the generative model conditioned on the current state, action and return-to-go. D.2.1 Standard RL baselines. SAC SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) is the off-policy reference: twin critics with a clipped double-Q target and a stochastic actor whose objective adds an entropy bonus with automatically tuned temperature. 

PPO PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) is the on-policy reference: a clipped probability-ratio surrogate on trajectories from the current policy, with generalized advantage estimation. 

TRPO TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) replaces PPO’s clipped surrogate by an explicit KL trust region solved with conjugate gradients. It appears only as the base of RARL-TRPO. 

D.2.2 Learner-centric algorithms ATLA ATLA (Zhang et al., 2021) trains a policy against an optimal observation adversary in the state-adversarial MDP (Zhang et al., 2020): the adversary is itself an RL agent that outputs a bounded perturba-tion of the observation, is rewarded by the protagonist’s negative return, and is updated in alternation with the protagonist. 

ATLA-SA ATLA-SA (Zhang et al., 2021) adds the SA-PPO regularizer (Zhang et al., 2020) to ATLA: a KL penalty between the policy at a state and at the worst-case neighbor found by stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics, and a recurrent (LSTM) policy with a 100-step history so that the agent can infer a persistent perturbation from context. 

RSC RSC (Ding et al., 2023a) learns a causal graph and dynamics model over the state variables from replayed experience, then generates counterfactual transitions by intervening on the variables the graph marks as non-causal for the reward. Mixing these into replay removes the spurious correlations a policy would otherwise exploit. 

D.2.3 Environment-centric algorithms. RARL RARL (Pinto et al., 2017) casts robustness as a two-player zero-sum game: an adversary applies bounded external forces to the robot and is trained to minimize the protagonist’s return, and the two are updated in alternating blocks. We implement RARL-PPO and RARL-TRPO in our library. 

DR Domain randomization (Peng et al., 2018; Tobin et al., 2017) samples simulator parameters from fixed ranges at every episode so that the policy meets a distribution of dynamics during training. We implement DR-PPO and DR-SAC in our library. 

D.2.4 Robust safe methods. RAMU RAMU (Queeney and Benosman, 2023) applies a risk measure over sampled next-state perturba-tions to both the reward and the cost targets; the original is defined for TD learners, and the row reported here is a PPO adaptation that applies the same transform to PPO’s value targets. 

SPiDR SPiDR (As et al., 2026) trains a constrained policy under domain randomization and adds a pessimistic cost penalty proportional to the disagreement of next-state predictions across sampled dynamics; it runs with the authors’ solver and a joint-limit cost budget. 29 E Training Settings 

E.1 Training protocol 

We train each method with five independent seeds to its budget in Table 14. Unless otherwise noted, we freeze the final checkpoint and evaluate each policy for 20 episodes per condition using the same evaluation seed. RFQI retains its native best-checkpoint selection rule. Our default protocol trains on the nominal task and introduces shifts only when evaluating the frozen policy. Reward shift and latency delivery are exceptions: they affect learning feedback but cannot alter the actions of a frozen policy. We therefore apply these shifts during training and evaluate the resulting policies on the nominal task. The Isaac Lab and VLA studies are reported under their own protocol, and the departures are the following. Isaac Lab tasks use three training seeds and score 128 episodes per condition. They match the retained primary training interactions within each task to the budget in Table 15. The five methods share one base learner, so the comparison is between mechanisms rather than between releases. And they do not hold the shift grid out from training: PPO and RAMU-PPO train on the nominal specification, whereas DR-PPO, ATLA-PPO and SPiDR-PPO each train inside a randomization envelope. Table 8 and Table 9 show the envelopes applied in our benchmark, which is transferred from a humanoid sim-to-real pipeline (He et al., 2024). The VLA task finetunes a single π0.5 model on demonstrations, and evaluated by joint-space tracking against the recorded demonstration.

The darker column is the base-relative gain :each method with a matched base learner is compared with that base algorithm under the same conditions, and the differences are averaged within the family. Standalone recipes without a matched reference learner, including RFQI, RORL, and RAMBO, are excluded; the number of included methods is reported as n in Table 41. The lighter column is the standard-pool gain : the full family’s mean minus the mean of all six Standard RL references. It therefore reflects both the robustness mechanism and the strength of its underlying learner. Brackets denote 95% seed-level stratified bootstrap intervals from 10 ,000 replicates. Each training seed contributes its mean over 20 evaluation episodes, and seeds are resampled within each (method, task) stratum, with the same draw reused across all conditions of that task. Methods and base algorithms are separate runs and are resampled independently. The two different comparisons indicate that both online families retain positive base-relative gains (+5 .5and +6 .6), whereas offline gains are smaller. The generative family offers the cleanest comparison: all three methods use IQL, yielding a +9 .8 gain over the standard pool but +2 .5 over IQL. G.2 Analysis of task capability: On-policy vs Off-policy 

On-policy methods update a policy using trajectories collected by that policy or a recent version of it, as in PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). Off-policy methods can also learn from data generated by earlier or different policies, often through experience replay, as in SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018). This distinction concerns which data can be used for an update. Figure 13 shows that the evaluated off-policy methods generally achieve higher scores on the MuJoCo locomotion tasks, while on-policy methods are stronger on PointMaze-UMaze and CarRace. In locomotion, frequent reward feedback allows replay-based learners to reuse informative transitions across many updates, which may contribute to the off-policy methods’ advantage. PointMaze requires discovering goal-reaching trajectories, while CarRacing couples control with visual input; updates from recent on-policy rollouts may be more useful in these settings. 

G.3 Results of advanced robotic tasks 

Figure 14 indicates that across the Isaac Lab tasks, SPiDR-PPO most consistently balances performance and safety: it maintains high success while satisfying the cost budget on Drawer, and leads on both return and survival under G1 action delays. These results show why high return alone is insufficient for sim-to-real evaluation. Robust-safe methods must be assessed for constraint satisfaction alongside task performance.
