# Abstract 

The goal of Quality-Diversity Reinforcement Learning (QD-RL) is to find a population of high-performing policies that solve a decision-making problem in diverse ways. Despite recent progress, QD-RL methods predominantly rely on evolu-tionary operators–including black-box optimiz-ers, mutations, and crossovers–to facilitate explo-ration and induce diversity. Scaling these methods becomes increasingly costly as policies and behav-ior spaces grow larger. Building on the Soft QD view of quality-diversity optimization, we intro-duce Re pulsive Population Policy Optimization (RePPO ), which adapts PPO to jointly optimize a compact population of diverse, high-quality poli-cies using a population-dependent, dynamically shaped reward. On standard continuous-control tasks, RePPO achieves a strong quality-diversity tradeoff compared with state-of-the-art QD-RL methods. Ablations further characterize this trade-off by examining how each component affects in-dividual quality and collective diversity. To assess RePPO’s scalability, we extend our evaluation to learning from pixels and fine-tuning large lan-guage models. In pixel-based tasks, where large policies make most traditional QD-RL methods infeasible, RePPO maintains quality on par with standard PPO while achieving substantially higher diversity. Its successful application to language model fine-tuning further demonstrates, for the first time, that QD-RL methods can scale to this domain. Together, these results show that directly optimizing compact populations with RePPO pro-vides a scalable approach to QD-RL across in-creasingly complex domains.

# 1. Introduction 

Standard RL algorithms typically seek one policy that maxi-mizes expected return, even though many tasks admit quali-tatively different strategies. A humanoid can move forward by walking, hopping, or dragging itself (Batra et al., 2024); a manipulator can reach an object from different angles (Hu-ber et al., 2023; 2025), and an autonomous vehicle can make progress while expressing different driving tendencies (Shi-roshita et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). Retaining these strategies allows us to compare how different behaviors achieve high reward and makes the available alternatives ex-plicit. Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization formalizes this broader objective by seeking solutions that combine high task performance with diversity under a user-defined behav-ior descriptor (Pugh et al., 2016). The descriptor specifies which aspects of behavior should distinguish the solutions, making the desired diversity an explicit part of the learning problem. The scalability of QD-RL depends on both how diverse policies are discovered and how the resulting collection is represented. The dominant approach, exemplified by MAP-Elites (Cully et al., 2015), partitions behavior space into cells and stores the highest-quality solution found in each cell. This makes behavioral coverage explicit, but ties the representation of diversity to a discretization: finer grids cre-ate more cells to fill, and the number of cells grows rapidly with descriptor dimension. These costs become substantial when each policy is a large neural network. Recent methods have improved search efficiency by incorporating deep RL updates (Pierrot et al., 2022; Faldor et al., 2023; Batra et al., 2024), yet diversity remains organized around an archive and often a separate evolutionary search procedure. We therefore ask whether quality and diversity can instead be optimized directly over a compact population using standard RL updates. A continuous objective over a finite population offers a way to pursue this goal without organizing the search around archive cells. We build on Soft Quality-Diversity opti-mization (Hedayatian & Nikolaidis, 2026b), which mea-sures the behavioral coverage induced by the population through smooth contributions from individual solutions. Soft QD captures this redundancy through a tractable lower bound that rewards individual quality and penalizes high-quality pairs that are behaviorally close. The population size can then be chosen directly, with diversity expressed through interactions among its members. Applying this ob-jective to RL, however, requires optimizing both return and behavior without differentiating through the environment. We show that standard policy gradients can optimize this population objective through a reward shaped by the current population. The key observation is that additive Markovian descriptors (Batra et al., 2024) express behavior as the ex-pected cumulative value of per-step signals. A weighted combination of these signals can therefore serve as a reward for moving the behavior in a chosen direction. RePPO uses this formulation as a starting point, adapt-ing its task and diversity terms for stable PPO training while preserving a consistent task-improvement signal. Our evaluation examines both the quality-diversity trade-off under a fixed interaction budget and the scalability of direct population training. Standard continuous-control tasks provide a comparison with established archive-based QD-RL methods. Atari games extend this evaluation to learning directly from pixels in a standard discrete-action domain. Lastly, zero-RL fine-tuning of LLMs on mathe-matical reasoning tasks further tests the approach in large language model reinforcement learning. Component abla-tions complement these experiments by examining how the algorithm’s design choices affect training and the balance between individual quality and collective diversity. Our contributions are: 1. We derive a population-dependent shaped reward whose policy gradient matches each policy’s gradient of the Soft QD lower bound for additive Markovian behavior descriptors. This connects a continuous QD objective to standard policy optimization in RL. 2. We introduce RePPO, a practical extension of PPO that jointly trains a compact policy population without behavior-space discretization or evolutionary operators. We develop controls for stable population training and ablate their role in balancing quality and diversity. 3. We show competitive quality-diversity performance on continuous-control benchmarks, and demonstrate scalability to pixel-based Atari policies and RL fine-tuning of LLMs. The language model experiments provide the first demonstration of QD-RL for large language model fine-tuning. 

# 2. Related Work 

2.1. Quality-Diversity objectives and search 

QD research has developed both representations of behav-ioral coverage and search procedures for discovering high-quality solutions. MAP-Elites (Cully et al., 2015) represents coverage through a partition of behavior space and retains the highest-quality solution found in each region. This sep-arates the task objective from the behavioral dimensions along which diversity is sought (Pugh et al., 2016). Al-ternative archive structures address limitations of regular grids; for example, centroidal Voronoi tessellations allow the number of archive regions to be chosen independently of descriptor dimension (Vassiliades et al., 2017; Mouret, 2023). Adaptive search and gradient information have substan-tially improved the efficiency of this archive-based frame-work. CMA-ME combines covariance-adaptive search with archive improvement (Fontaine et al., 2020), while CMA-MAE introduces adaptive acceptance thresholds to improve exploration (Fontaine & Nikolaidis, 2023). Scalable vari-ants reduce the cost of covariance adaptation for neural controllers (Tjanaka et al., 2023a). Differentiable QD uses gradients of both quality and behavior descriptors to guide search in the joint objective–behavior space (Fontaine & Nikolaidis, 2021). These advances show how the structure of a QD problem can inform its search operators. Soft QD provides a continuous population objective that supports direct gradient-based optimization (Hedayatian & Nikolaidis, 2026b). Its SQUAD algorithm optimizes a tractable lower bound through quality-weighted interactions between population members. We inherit this objective and develop policy-gradient estimators and a practical RL training procedure. 

2.2. Quality-Diversity in reinforcement learning 

QD-RL has advanced through both scalable evolutionary search and the incorporation of deep RL updates. ME-ES scales archive-based search to neural policies using evo-lution strategies (Colas et al., 2020). PGA-ME combines genetic variation with off-policy actor gradients (Nilsson & Cully, 2021). DCG-ME extends this approach with a descriptor-conditioned critic and distills archived behaviors into a single descriptor-conditioned policy (Faldor et al., 2023). DCRL-ME further advances this line of methods by injecting specialized versions of the descriptor-conditioned policy into the offspring population (Faldor et al., 2025). QD-PG combines quality and diversity policy gradients to improve exploration and sample efficiency (Pierrot et al., 2Repulsive Population Policy Gradients for Behaviorally Diverse Reinforcement Learning 

2022). These methods demonstrate different ways to use experience from policy evaluation to guide the QD search. On-policy optimization and parallel execution provide fur-ther routes to improving QD-RL. Work on approximating objective and descriptor gradients brought differentiable QD to RL settings (Tjanaka et al., 2022). PPGA uses PPO to estimate these gradients through Markovian measure prox-ies, and an evolutionary search over gradient coefficients guides archive improvement (Batra et al., 2024). RePPO builds on the use of RL updates for quality and behavior, with population interactions determined by a continuous QD objective. QD-RL also includes approaches that represent diverse be-haviors through a conditioned policy. QDAC, for instance, jointly learns high-performing skills using successor-feature critics under behavioral constraints (Grillotti et al., 2024). Its skill-conditioned actor provides a shared representation of many behaviors. RePPO instead treats a finite population of policies as the optimization variable with interactions derived from the Soft QD lower bound. 

2.3. Diverse policies and behavior representations 

Direct policy-population optimization has also been stud-ied through diversity objectives outside the QD formula-tion. DOMiNO optimizes occupancy-based diversity while maintaining per-policy near-optimality constraints, and ex-presses diversity gradients through intrinsic rewards (Za-havy et al., 2022). DvD uses a determinant-based population diversity criterion (Parker-Holder et al., 2020), while DIPG encourages differences between policy-induced trajectory distributions using maximum mean discrepancy (Masood & Doshi-Velez, 2019). RePPO’s distinction lies in deriv-ing quality-weighted interactions from a QD objective over given behavior descriptors. Skill discovery and descriptor learning address complemen-tary aspects of behavioral diversity. DIAYN learns dis-tinguishable skills through an information-theoretic objec-tive (Eysenbach et al., 2018), APS combines exploration with successor-feature learning (Liu & Abbeel, 2021), and METRA develops a metric-aware objective for scalable un-supervised skill discovery (Park et al., 2024). Moreover, connections between skill discovery and goal-conditioned RL can provide further approaches to learning behavioral alternatives (Choi et al., 2021). Lastly, unsupervised QD ap-proaches address the representation of diversity by construct-ing behavior descriptors from sampled trajectories (Grillotti & Cully, 2022; Hedayatian & Nikolaidis, 2026a). Our focus is the population optimization problem given a descriptor set. The policy-gradient construction applies when that de-scriptor admits the additive representation specified below, independent of how they are obtained.

# 4. Repulsive Population Policy Optimization 

RePPO jointly trains a population by using the policies’ relative qualities and behaviors to shape their PPO updates. Each iteration collects experience, summarizes the popu-lation, and determines how each policy should change its behavior to complement the others. These directions be-come weights on behavior advantages, which are combined with the task advantage in the actor update. We first derive this connection from the Soft QD objective, then explain how RePPO constructs the population interaction and incor-porates it into PPO.

# 5. Experiments 

Our experiments test whether directly optimizing a compact population yields a favorable balance of quality and diver-sity, and whether this approach scales to domains where archive-based QD-RL becomes impractical. We compare RePPO with established QD-RL methods on continuous-control benchmarks, then turn to two settings where archives of policies become costly or impractical: pixel-based Atari policies and LLM fine-tuning for mathematical reasoning. Appendices G and H add results on pixel-based locomotion and on LunarLander. 

Benchmarks. The continuous-control benchmark consists of four Brax locomotion tasks (Freeman et al., 2021) com-monly used in QD-RL: Ant, HalfCheetah, Humanoid, and Walker2D (Batra et al., 2024). Fitness, the quality f of Section 3.2, is the episode return, and the descriptor records the fraction of time each foot touches the ground, giving four dimensions for Ant and two for the other tasks. For Atari, we use four games from the Arcade Learning En-vironment (Bellemare et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2018): Boxing, Asterix, Seaquest, and Ms. Pac-Man. For each task, fitness is the game score, and two-dimensional descriptors record where the agent plays, its distant to opponent (in Box-ing), and the order in which it clears the maze (in Ms. Pac-Man). For LLMs, we fine-tune two base models, Qwen3-1.7B (Team, 2025) and Granite-4.1-3B (IBM Granite Team, 2026), on MATH problems (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with a binary correctness reward, and evaluate them on MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2024), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). Baselines. In every domain, we compare against Inde-pendent PPO (Ind-PPO), which uses RePPO’s implementa-tion, population size, and hyperparameters with the diversity weights pk set to zero, so that the comparison isolates the ef-fect of repulsion. It is equivalent to training N PPO policies each with a different seed. In continuous control, we addi-tionally compare against archive-based methods represent-ing the main families of QD-RL: PPGA (Batra et al., 2024), which uses PPO for differentiable QD; PGA-ME (Nilsson & Cully, 2021) and DCRL-ME (Faldor et al., 2025), which add TD3-based variation (Fujimoto et al., 2018) to MAP-Elites; and the black-box Sep-CMA-MAE (Tjanaka et al., 2023a). In Atari, since the aforementioned methods become prohibitively inefficient and costly, we compare against Re-fQD (Wang et al., 2024), which shares one representation network across its archive. For LLMs, we compare against the base model and two prompting baselines in which each one of the members is the base model with a different style instruction, either combining extreme values of several de-scriptors or emphasizing a single one. 

Evaluation. After training, we evaluate every policy anew to estimate its fitness and mean descriptor, using 256 

episodes in continuous control, 32 full games in Atari, and 8

samples per test problem for LLMs. Archive-based methods return thousands of policies, many of which fail to solve the task. Because of this, we discard policies whose fitness is below 10% of the best policy on that task and evaluate the remaining policies. On Ant, we use a 25% threshold, since stationary policies with varied contact patterns collect enough survival bonus to clear the lower bar. We measure quality by the mean fitness of the retained policies and di-versity by the Vendi Score of their descriptors (Friedman & Dieng, 2023), which can be read as the effective num-ber of distinct behaviors in the population. Their product, the quality-weighted Vendi Score (qVS) (Nguyen & Dieng, 2024), serves as a combined measure of quality and diver-sity. We also report each population’s best fitness before filtering, and vary the threshold in Appendix C.4.

RePPO trains N = 32 policies for 2.05 B environment steps in total, the same budget as Ind-PPO, PGA-ME, DCRL-ME, and Sep-CMA-MAE, while PPGA uses 2.74 B steps. PPGA’s budget can only be controlled indirectly through its number of iterations which is why it has a higher budget. RePPO attains the highest qVS on all four tasks by com-bining the quality of PPO training with diversity that rivals archive-based search (Figure 1, top). The baselines fall on either side of this balance. Ind-PPO trains strong policies, but without repulsion they converge to the same behavior. Archive-based methods cover behavior space broadly, but with their budget spread over thousands of cells, most of their policies remain weaker. RePPO’s population balances the two. On Humanoid, it is about twice as diverse as any archive at a mean fitness close to that of the fittest one. Ant proves to be the hardest task for QD baselines, with many of them struggling to find high-performing policies and in-stead diversifying low-quality behaviors. This is particularly evident when we look at the highest fitness among the pop-ulations that each method finds, where these methods trail RePPO and Ind-PPO substantially (Table 4). 5.2. Pixel-based policies in Atari 

RePPO and Ind-PPO train N = 8 policies for 10 Msteps each, following the standard PPO configuration for Atari (Huang et al., 2022). RefQD uses a comparable budget of 86 .4 M steps and maintains an archive of 1000 cells. Its training configuration and hyperparameters largely follow the published code. RePPO retains the quality of independent PPO training while making its populations substantially more diverse (Figure 1, bottom). Its mean scores are close to or above Ind-PPO’s in every game and its diversity is higher than both baselines, culminating in substantial gains in qVS over both baselines. RefQD’s archives cover behavior space broadly, but their policies score far below those trained with PPO. Diversity can also uncover strategies that independent train-ing misses. In Seaquest, every policy trained by Ind-PPO, and every competent policy in RefQD’s archives, patrols near the sea floor (Figure 2). Repulsion pushes some of RePPO’s policies toward shallower water, and in four of the five seeds, the best policy of the population operates just below the surface, where it outscores every Ind-PPO policy. Here, diversity improves not only the population as a whole but also its best member. Appendix E.4 visualizes the behaviors in all four games.

# 6. Conclusion and Outlook 

We introduced RePPO, a Quality-Diversity algorithm that di-rectly trains a compact population of reinforcement learning policies. Starting from the Soft QD objective, we showed that additive Markovian behavior descriptors turn each pol-icy’s population gradient into an ordinary policy gradient under a shaped reward, which RePPO optimizes with PPO. On continuous-control benchmarks, RePPO attains a better balance of quality and diversity than established QD-RL methods. Because its cost scales with the population size rather than with the resolution of an archive, it extends to pixel-based Atari policies and to LLM fine-tuning, where it makes populations substantially more diverse at nearly the quality of independent training. RePPO is designed for optimizing compact populations of useful policies rather than exhaustive coverage of behav-ior space, for which archive-based methods remain better suited. It also requires descriptors that admit additive forms, and it diversifies whatever these descriptors measure: in our LLM experiments, hand-designed descriptors produced differences mostly in presentation rather than in strategy. Addressing these limitations calls for learned or preference-derived descriptors, adaptive bandwidths and population sizes, and hybrids with external archives. More broadly, shaping each policy’s reward by the pop-ulation is useful whenever one optimum is not enough, from adaptation to changing conditions to generative mod-els whose adapters express different preferences or styles. In all these settings, diversity can be encoded as a reward induced by the geometry of a population.

Here, we enumerate them while Appendix D examines the task coefficient, the cap, and the local interactions empirically. 1. Smoothed episode statistics. The qualities and descriptors are replaced by exponential moving averages of completed-episode returns and episode-level descriptors, which reduces noise in the interaction at the cost of a small lag. Following standard practice, the advantages use discounted GAE as a surrogate for the gradient of these undiscounted statistics. 2. Shifted qualities. # B. Evaluation protocol 

Re-evaluation. After training, every policy is evaluated anew, and these evaluations define all reported numbers. In continuous control, each policy runs 256 episodes with deterministic actions. In Atari, each policy plays 32 full games, over all lives and up to the cap of 27000 agent steps. Finally, LLM members are evaluated on 8 samples per test problem. Fitness is the mean undiscounted return and the behavior descriptor is the mean episode descriptor over these evaluations. 

Quality filter. For each task, let f ⋆ be the fitness of the best policy found by any method in any seed. Each population or archive retains the policies whose fitness is at least 0.1f ⋆ (0.25 f ⋆ on Ant), and all metrics except best fitness are computed on the retained policies. The filter is applied identically to every method, and archives are otherwise used as they are. Note that in the LLM experiments, we remove no members.

We ablate RePPO’s components and hyperparameters on Humanoid. Each variant changes one setting of the main Humanoid configuration ( Cmax = 1 , ℓ2 = 0 .1, K = 8 , N = 32 , symmetric repulsion, Wk = 1 ) and runs for three seeds. The default is represented by the first three seeds of the main runs. All metrics follow Appendix B, with the quality threshold of the main comparison. D.1. Structural choices 

We first examine two structural choices: the unit task coefficient of Section 4.1, and symmetric interactions, in which every pair of neighbors repels each other regardless of their qualities. 

Exact task coefficient. Using the exact coefficient Wk of (8) causes training to collapse in every seed. Policies perform significantly worse with the best policy reaching a fitness of only 282 ± 230 . When several high-quality policies share a region of behavior space–which happens often early in training–the second term of Wk outweighs the first and the task coefficient becomes negative. The policy is then rewarded for lowering its return, and once it reaches low-return behaviors, such as falling immediately, PPO does not recover. Fixing Wk = 1 preserves a consistent task-improvement signal and leaves behavioral separation to the descriptor term. 

Asymmetric repulsion. In this variant, a policy is repelled only by neighbors of higher quality, so that the best policies are never displaced by weaker ones. The best policy is unaffected ( 8409 ± 201 versus 8336 ± 68 ), but the rest of the population is both less fit and less diverse, lowering qVS from 39212 to 34086 . Symmetric interactions let every overlapping pair separate, which spreads the population more evenly. 

D.2. Interaction strength and scale 

Together, the repulsion cap Cmax and the kernel bandwidth ℓ2 shape how the population spreads through behavior space, but they do so in different ways (Figures 10 and 11). 

Repulsion cap. The cap controls how strongly diversity enters each update, with Cmax = 0 recovering Ind-PPO. Raising it from 0.1 to 1 increases the Vendi Score from 3.92 to 6.33 and the spread from 0.60 to 0.91 , while mean fitness stays between 5700 and 6200 . Beyond Cmax = 2 , diversity saturates and the stronger diversity signal begins to compete with task learning: at Cmax = 8 , mean and best fitness fall to 4691 and 6817 . qVS peaks at the default of 1, with Cmax = 2 

close behind. The cap therefore mainly controls how far the population expands, and needs to be large enough to spread the policies but not so large that repulsion dominates the update. 

Kernel bandwidth. The bandwidth sets the scale over which policies interact and thus the configuration the population settles into. With ℓ2 = 0 .02 , interactions are highly local, and the population remains compact (spread 0.70 , Vendi Score 

4.80 ) and fit (mean fitness 6703 ). Increasing the bandwidth pushes policies apart until the spread saturates near 1.0 at 

ℓ2 = 0 .5, where the Vendi Score peaks at 6.84 . F.1. Task and data 

We train on the 5584 problems of levels 3–5 of the MATH training set (Hendrycks et al., 2021) whose reference solution contains a boxed answer, as in SimpleRL-Zoo (Zeng et al., 2025). For checkpoint selection, we sample 384 problems of the same levels from the MATH test set, excluding MATH-500. The test set combines MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2024) ( 500 

problems), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022) ( 272 ), and the English text-only part of OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024) (675 ). The base models are Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Team, 2025) and Granite-4.1-3B-Base (IBM Granite Team, 2026). Every problem is accompanied by the instruction “Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed {} .”, given as the system message of the chat template for Qwen3 and after the problem in a plain question–answer format for Granite. 

Fitness. A solution scores r = 1 if it ends within 4096 tokens and its last boxed expression matches the reference under Math-Verify, and r = 0 otherwise. The training reward standardizes this score as (r − μ0)/σ 0, using its mean and standard deviation on the base model’s first batch.

F.3. Baselines and evaluation 

The prompting baselines prepend a style instruction to each problem. In the corners baseline, the five instructions combine extreme values of all four descriptors, chosen so that every pair of members differs in at least two of them. For example, “Solve the problem by writing Python code, verify your answer with an explicit check, explain mostly in words, and write in flowing paragraphs without headers or bullet lists.” In the emphasis baseline, four members each emphasize one descriptor, such as “Present the solution as a clearly structured markdown document, with headers and a bulleted or numbered list of steps.”, and the fifth combines markdown with verification. At test time, every member samples 8 solutions per problem at temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95 , and the base model samples 

40 , matching a population’s total. pass@1 averages correctness over members and samples. Population pass@ 5j draws j

samples from each member and is computed with the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021). Identifiability is the held-out accuracy of a logistic-regression classifier on TF-IDF features of word unigrams and bigrams, trained to predict which member wrote a solution on half of the problems and tested on the other half.

H.1. Computational Resources 

The continuous-control, Atari, pixel-based, and LunarLander experiments were distributed over the following workstations: • AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3990X, 64 GB RAM, 2 NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs; • AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3990X, 64 GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX 4090; • Intel Core i7-12700F, 16 GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX 3060; • a shared server with NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. A RePPO run on Atari requires about 4 GB of GPU memory. Each LLM population was trained on five NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, one member per GPU, each running both generation with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) and training. Training a population took 21 h for Qwen3 and 64 h for Granite, or about 100 and 320 GPU hours, and Ind-PPO required similar resources.
