Abstract 

In this study, we propose a dual-branch evolutionary framework that simul-taneously performs Neural Architecture Search (NAS) and Hyperparameter Optimization (HPO) for Reinforcement Learning (RL) in Autonomous Driving (AD) tasks. While NAS has shown strong potential in automating architecture design for Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), its application to RL remains limited, especially in scenarios that require joint optimization of architectural and training dynamics. Building on our prior work on Evolutionary Multi-Objective NAS (EMNAS), the proposed framework evolves discrete network architectures and continuous hyperparameters in parallel under a unified multi-objective search strategy. In this setup, NAS targets the convolutional layers of the RL pol-icy network - responsible for visual feature extraction, while HPO focuses on optimizing critical RL hyperparameters. To accelerate convergence and improve generalization, we incorporate parallel evaluation and a teacher-student policy transfer mechanism. Experiments in a high-fidelity driving simulator show that the joint NAS-HPO framework consistently outperforms standalone NAS, yield-ing policies with higher rewards, fewer parameters, and improved stability. These results highlight the benefits of jointly optimizing model structure and training dynamics for scalable and effective RL agent design.

# 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful framework for Autonomous Driving (AD), enabling agents to learn sophisticated decision-making and control poli-cies in complex environments. However, designing effective RL agents requires carefully crafting both the neural network architecture and the associated training hyperpa-rameters, which together determine the agent’s performance, sample efficiency, and computational cost. Traditionally, these design decisions are made manually, relying on expert intuition and extensive trial-and-error tuning, which are time-consuming and often suboptimal. To automate and scale this process, we previously introduced the Evolutionary Multi-Objective Neural Architecture Search (EMNAS) framework [1], building on EEEANet [2]. EMNAS employs a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to evolve compact convo-lutional architectures for the policy network of a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) based RL agent. By integrating early-exit evaluation, Optimized Transfer Learning (OTL), and parallelized training, EMNAS demonstrated superior performance over manually crafted models [1]. However, its search space was limited solely to architec-tural configurations, neglecting the significant impact of RL hyperparameters on RL convergence and stability. In this paper, we extend EMNAS into a dual-branch evolutionary framework that jointly optimizes neural network architectures and training hyperparameters. Hyper-parameter and Neural Architecture Search (HPNAS) framework combines discrete architecture evolution with continuous hyperparameter tuning using polynomial muta-tion and Simulated Binary Crossover (SBX), enabling coordinated search across both spaces. Neural Architecture Search (NAS) targets the convolutional layers of the RL policy network, which are responsible for visual feature extraction, while Hyperpa-rameter Optimization (HPO) tunes RL hyperparameters such as the learning rate, batch size, discount factor and time horizon. Initial experiments demonstrate that the HPNAS strategy yields consistent improvements in cumulative reward, model com-pactness, and training stability over architecture-only baselines. A Bayesian surrogate optimization model is being developed to learn the relationship between architecture and hyperparameter configurations and their performance. Although not yet inte-grated in this work, it is designed to guide later stage search by predicting promising configurations and is discussed further in the outlook. 

## 1.1 Autonomous Driving Task: 

The AD task [3] is performed in a simulated environment built using the Unity 3D engine [4], where multiple agents are trained with the Proximal Policy Optimiza-tion (PPO) algorithm [5]. Agents navigate a three-lane oval track with the goal of learning smooth, collision-free driving while avoiding abrupt maneuvers. Each agent 2receives semantic segmentation images from a forward-facing camera and predicts future trajectory points, which are executed by an external black-box vehicle con-troller responsible for throttle, brake, and steering commands. The reward function promotes accurate trajectory prediction, penalizes collisions and lane deviations, and encourages stable motion with minimal jerk. Figure 1 illustrates the simulated Unity3D environment used for agent training. This setup provides a realistic yet controlled platform for evaluating reinforcement learning strategies and their generalization across training runs. In this work, we focus on optimizing the convolutional network architecture and PPO hyperparame-ters underlying the policy to enhance driving performance and training efficiency in the RL-based AD task.

# 2 Related Work 

NAS has evolved through RL-based search [6], gradient-based optimization [7], and Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) [8]. While gradient-based methods such as DARTS [7] improve efficiency by making architecture selection differentiable through continuous weighting of candidate operations, they often converge to locally optimal designs and are restricted to differentiable objectives. RL-based NAS frameworks [6] have shown promising results but require extensive computational resources due to sequential pol-icy evaluations. In contrast, EAs provide a more flexible search paradigm capable of exploring large, non-differentiable spaces while maintaining population diversity, 3making them well-suited for complex architecture optimization problems. Early meth-ods such as NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (NEAT) [9] demonstrated the ability of genetic algorithms to evolve both network topology and parameters. Later studies, including AmoebaNet [8], showed that evolutionary search can out-perform reinforcement learning and random search strategies in large-scale vision tasks. Building on these ideas, Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs) were introduced to balance accuracy, computational cost, and network size. Examples include NSGA-Net [10], which generates a Pareto front of efficient architectures, and Early Exit Evolutionary Neural Architecture Search (EEEANet) [2], which combines MOEAs with early-exit initialization using the NSGA-II algorithm [11] to improve convergence. Despite their effectiveness, most EA and MOEA-based NAS frameworks remain confined to supervised learning, offering limited scalability and adaptability to reinforcement learning tasks where sample efficiency and stability are critical. In recent years, researchers have recognized that architectural choices and hyperpa-rameter settings are interdependent, motivating the development of joint optimization approaches that integrate NAS with HPO [12, 13]. Such methods have shown that jointly tuning both components can lead to more stable and higher-performing models, as hyperparameters directly influence architecture behavior during training. Bayesian optimization-based strategies [14] and weight-sharing techniques [15] have been pro-posed to accelerate search by reusing knowledge across configurations. Evolutionary approaches for joint NAS–HPO have also been explored in convolutional network set-tings [16, 17]. These methods demonstrated the potential of integrating architectural and hyperparameter optimization, using evolutionary multi-objective strategies such as non-dominated sorting and tournament selection to balance competing objectives. However, they were primarily designed for static supervised learning tasks, where evaluations are deterministic and data distributions remain fixed. Such frameworks do not address the unique challenges of reinforcement learning, including stochastic rewards, non-stationary environments, and the need for scalable exploration across parallel agents. In this context, our work introduces a dual-branch evolutionary framework that jointly optimizes architectures and RL-specific hyperparameters. By combining a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)-based NASNet-style representation [18] with parallel population training and transfer learning mechanisms, the proposed approach achieves scalable and sample-efficient optimization in high-dimensional RL environments, bridging the gap between HPO, NAS and RL. 

# 3 HPNAS Methodology 

The proposed HPNAS framework extends our earlier EMNAS system [1] by introduc-ing a dual-branch evolutionary approach that jointly optimizes neural architectures and reinforcement learning hyperparameters. As illustrated in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, the framework comprises two coordinated components: the EMNAS-based architecture optimization branch, which evolves convolutional network structures, and the hyper-parameter optimization branch, which tunes PPO-related parameters such as learning 4rate, batch size, and discount factor. Both branches operate within a unified multi-objective evolutionary process, sharing fitness evaluations to jointly enhance policy performance and model efficiency.

Using a multi-objective genetic algorithm, EMNAS evolves candidate architectures to simulta-neously maximize PPO cumulative reward while minimizing computational cost and model complexity. 3.1.2 Multi-objective Evolutionary Algorithm 

The optimization of CNN architectures in EMNAS is formulated as a multi-objective problem, aiming to maximize reinforcement learning performance while minimizing 6computational cost and model complexity: min f1(x), f 2(x), . . . , f k(x)s.t. x ∈ X (4) In EMNAS, k = 3, and the objective vector is defined as in Equation 1. A population of candidate architectures is evolved over multiple generations, where each individual is trained and evaluated using the defined objectives. Fitness values are then ranked using the NSGA-II algorithm [11], which applies non-dominated sorting and crowd-ing distance to preserve diversity among Pareto-optimal solutions. This enables the search to discover a balanced set of architectures that achieve high PPO reward while remaining lightweight and computationally efficient. Equal weighting is applied to all objectives to ensure fair comparison across the trade-off surface.

3.1.4 Optimized Transfer Learning (OTL) 

To improve sample efficiency and ensure stable convergence across generations, EMNAS employs an Optimized Transfer Learning (OTL) mechanism. In this approach, student architectures inherit weights and policies from the best-performing model of the previous generation through behavior cloning [19], allowing new populations to begin training from a well-initialized policy rather than from scratch. At the end of each generation, expert trajectories DE = {(st, a t)}Tt=1 are collected from the top policy πE (a | s), where st ∈ S is the state (or observation) at time t,

at ∈ A is the action, and T is the trajectory length. Each architecture in the next generation is initialized by supervised imitation of this expert using the behavior cloning loss: 

LBC (θ) = E(s,a )∼D E [− log πθ (a|s)] (8) This ensures that both new offspring and surviving individuals begin PPO training from a consistent policy baseline, maintaining a fair optimization trajectory across the population. OTL also retains the optimizer state from the previous generation, including the final learning rate and PPO clipping value, ensuring a smooth continu-ation of training. By reusing expert data and policy initialization, OTL reduces PPO training time while maintaining stability and fairness across evolving architectures. In summary, EMNAS combines a compact genotype encoding, NSGA-II based multi-objective optimization, and efficiency mechanisms (EEPI and OTL) to discover CNN architectures that jointly optimize RL performance and computational cost. This forms the basis for the dual-branch HPNAS extension. 

## 3.2 Dual-Branch Extension: Joint NAS and HPO 

While EMNAS originally targeted architecture search, RL performance is also strongly influenced by hyperparameter choices. We therefore extend EMNAS into a dual-branch framework that jointly evolves architectures and hyperparameters using a coupled genetic algorithm. 83.2.1 Hyperparameter Search Space 

Based on the hyperparameter optimization and sensitivity analysis presented in Chap-ter ?? [3], we initially considered six RL hyperparameters for optimization. To reduce the dimensionality and computational cost of the joint search, PPO epochs and the entropy coefficient were fixed at 3 and 0.01, respectively. The remaining four: batch size, discount factor, learning rate, and time horizon form the HPO search space in this work, as they contributed most to performance variation [3].The final set of selected hyperparameters and their corresponding ranges are summarized in Table 2. The chosen ranges are informed by previous studies in PPO-based reinforcement learning [5, 20] and empirical observations in AD environments, ensuring coverage of commonly effective values while maintaining search feasibility.

Our dual-branch framework jointly evolves network architectures and hyperparam-eters, employing a discrete encoding for NAS and a continuous representation for HPO. Both branches are evaluated using the same multi-objective fitness function as described in EMNAS (Section 3.1), which balances reward, FLOPs, and parameter count. In the initial generation, hyperparameters are uniformly sampled within prede-fined ranges (see Table 2). From the second generation onward, the best-performing configuration is retained through a survival mechanism, and from the remaining popu-lation, offspring are generated using crossover or mutation with equal probability (0.5 each). To maintain diversity, 10% of new individuals are sampled purely at random.

Together, these operators enable efficient joint search over architecture and training dynamics, supporting scalable and effective RL agent discovery. 10 4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate the proposed dual-branch evolutionary framework across multiple autonomous driving experiments, designed to test its effectiveness in jointly opti-mizing architectures and hyperparameters under resource constrained RL settings. The experiments are conducted in the Unity3D simulation environment described in Section 1.1, where multiple agents are trained using the PPO algorithm. Each candi-date is evaluated with a multi-objective fitness that balances policy reward, FLOPs, and parameter count, consistent with EMNAS [1]. The evolutionary algorithm employs a crossover probability of 0.5 and a mutation probability of 0.1 for the architecture branch, while the hyperparameter branch uses a higher mutation probability of 0.5 to encourage exploration in continuous search spaces. A survival rate of 20% (up to four top-performing individuals retained per generation) is applied across both branches. To maintain diversity, 10% of offspring are generated through uniform random sampling. We schedule both the crossover and mutation probabilities, and the per-generation distribution index of crossover and mutation, to shift from exploration in early generations to exploitation in later ones. Candidate policies are trained and evaluated under low-fidelity settings to reduce com-putational cost, including 40 PPO epochs, input resolution of 84 ×84 ×3, and a network depth of 8. The depth corresponds to eight stacked cells alternating between normal and reduction cells, where the discovered architectures define the internal operations and connections within each cell type. These cells are then repeated to construct the final policy network. As we employ relative ranking within each generation, evaluation bias is reduced despite the low-fidelity settings. The architecture search is bounded using an EEPI threshold of 5 million parameters [1]. As in prior work, we employ an optimized transfer learning strategy where student models are initialized via behavior cloning [19] from the best policy of the previous generation [1]. We run six experiments with the following (generations, population size) settings: (6, 6), (10, 4), (10, 10), (10, 15), (15, 15), (15, 20). All evaluations are parallelized across NVIDIA Tesla H100 GPUs. We benchmark against the original EMNAS baseline [1] and do not compare against the standalone hyperparameter tuning baseline from previous work [3], as those models were trained for the full number of RL epochs and are incompatible with the low-fidelity setting used here. Finally, the winning architecture discovered through the dual-branch search is fully trained and compared with the results of the NAS-only and HPO-only baselines to evaluate the overall performance gains achieved by joint optimization.

# 6 Conclusion 

We proposed HPNAS, a dual-branch evolutionary framework for jointly optimiz-ing neural architectures and hyperparameters in reinforcement learning. Experiments 13 across multiple search budgets show that HPNAS consistently outperforms standalone NAS in both cumulative reward and model efficiency. While NAS can occasion-ally discover high-reward models early, it tends to overparameterize due to the absence of hyperparameter adaptation. These findings highlight the effectiveness of joint optimization in achieving compact, high-performing RL policies, particularly in larger-scale experiments. 

## Outlook 

As ongoing work, we aim to integrate a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) surrogate model to improve sample efficiency during the final stages of evolution. The surro-gate learns to predict PPO reward from joint encodings of neural architecture and hyperparameters. Once trained on NAS–HPO–reward triplets, the surrogate model enables conditional optimization [22] by inferring the optimal hyperparameter con-figuration that maximizes predicted reward for a given fixed architecture encoding. While the current implementation only gathers training data, future versions will acti-vate the surrogate in the last 20% of the search to guide sampling towards promising configurations once sufficient predictive accuracy is reached.
