# Enhancing Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotic Motion Planning via Task Decomposition 

Yuanzhi He, Victor Romero-Cano, Jiayu Xu, Juan David Hernández, Gualtiero Colombo 

School of Computational and Mathematical Sciences Cardiff University, United Kingdom 

Abstract —Training robotic manipulation policies in simulation is widely adopted due to safety and cost efficiency; however, tasks involving obstacles remain under-performing and often converge slowly under Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). To address this challenge, we propose a task decomposition framework for DRL that integrates Transfer Learning (TL) with Reward Redesign (RR) to improve learning efficiency and robustness in obstacle environments. The TL strategy selectively transfers only the first-layer critic parameters across structurally related tasks, since that layer captures general state–action representations while deeper layers encode reward- and obstacle-specific value geometry; RR decomposes tasks into staged sub-objectives to provide denser optimization signals. Experiments on the PyBullet Panda platform show consistent gains in obstacle settings. On Push with obstacle, our method achieves 0.83 ±0.05 ,outperforming the strongest baseline (Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC), 0.77 ± 0.06 ) by approximately 7.8% . On Slide with obstacle, performance reaches 0.95 ± 0.02 , exceeding Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) (0.84 ± 0.06 ) by about 11% success rates. In addition to higher final performance, forward transfer reduces convergence time by up to 70% in obstacle tasks, and RR shortens sparse-reward Pick-and-Place training from roughly 0.75 M to 

0.20 M steps ( ≈ 73% reduction). The best policy is deployed on a real Franka Emika FR3 robot as a qualitative feasibility demonstration. 

Index Terms —Deep Reinforcement Learning, Motion Planning, Transfer Learning, Task Decomposition 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robotics research has increasingly shifted toward simulation environments rather than training directly on real robots [1]. This trend is mainly driven by the high cost of trial-and-error in real-world settings, safety risks to physical hardware, and the time-consuming and labor-intensive nature of collecting large-scale interaction data. In contrast, simulation platforms enable low-cost, risk-free experimentation with highly control-lable scenarios, making them well suited for large-scale and repetitive training processes [2], [3]. In this study, we adopt the PyBullet Panda environments, whose robot arm model closely matches the kinematic structure and joint constraints of real manipulators such as the Franka arm [4], building on the goal-conditioned robotics benchmarks established in prior work [5], [6]. Despite the success of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in robotic manipulation tasks such as grasping, pushing, and object transportation, increasing task complexity often leads to a rapid growth in training cost and model instability. particular, the solution of tasks that require the robot to jointly solve manipulation and collision-free path planning problems enlarges the observation and action spaces. Under such conditions, training policies from scratch typically de-mands a large number of interaction samples, substantial com-putational resources, and often suffers from slow or unstable convergence [7]–[9]. We address this as obstacle-aware, goal-conditioned manipulation policy learning, in which the policy must reach a goal, interact with the object, and avoid the col-lision region introduced by an obstacle, all learned implicitly from interaction rather than from an explicit geometric planner. To address these challenges, we propose a task decomposi-tion framework that integrates Transfer Learning (TL) with Reward Redesign (RR) to improve learning efficiency and performance in a set of obstacle tasks. The policy is first trained on a relatively simpler source task to acquire funda-mental knowledge. The learned knowledge is then transferred to a more complex target task by reusing selected network parameters. In pick-and-place environments, we further re-design multi-step task rewards by decomposing them into multiple staged feedback signals corresponding to intermediate sub-tasks, where rewards are assigned to the approaching, grasping, and transporting stages. We evaluate the proposed framework on three manipulation tasks: Push, Pick-and-Place, and Slide, each with obstacle-free and obstacle settings, where a cubic obstacle was introduced in the obstacle environments. Snapshots of obstacle environments are illustrated in Fig. 1. Through systematic comparisons, we show that few-shot TL together with RR improves success rates and reduces training time across most task settings, with one exception, Pick-and-Place with obstacle, which we discuss in detail. Although first-layer transfer, reward shaping, and task de-composition build on established ideas, our emphasis is on a minimal and controlled combination of them and on under-standing when such transfer helps and when it does not. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:  

> •

We propose a unified task decomposition framework for DRL that combines few-shot TL and RR to enhance learning efficiency and performance for obstacle-aware robotic manipulation tasks.  

> •

We introduce a controlled transfer strategy that reuses only the first-layer critic parameters across tasks, and analyze why this layer is the appropriate transfer target arXiv:2610.35494v1 [cs.RO] 26 Oct 2026 through a transfer-depth comparison (first-layer vs. all-layer vs. from scratch).  

> •

We design a staged reward formulation that decomposes multi-step task objectives into interpretable sub-task re-wards, accelerating convergence without compromising final task performance.  

> •

We conduct extensive evaluations on multiple manipu-lation tasks in both obstacle-free and obstacle environ-ments, including a systematic study of forward, double, reverse, and neutral transfer, and demonstrate real-world feasibility through a deployment on a Franka Emika FR3 robot. Section 4 presents and discusses the experimental results in both simulation and real-world environments. Sec-tion 5 concludes the paper and discusses potential future work. II. R ELATED WORKS 

TL aims to reuse knowledge acquired from a source task to facilitate learning in a related target task, thereby reducing training cost and improving performance [10]–[12]. In robotic manipulation, TL is particularly valuable due to the high sample complexity of DRL and the substantial interaction cost required to learn complex contact-rich behaviors. Specifi-cally, few-shot TL enables partial fine-tuning while preserving reusable representations learned in upstream tasks [8]. In robotic manipulation, several studies have explored param-eter reuse and representation transfer to improve learning efficiency [13]–[15], drawing on multi-task representation learning [16]. Few-shot and zero-shot transfer strategies based on selective parameter sharing have demonstrated improved convergence in manipulation tasks by reusing low-level state– action representations [15]. However, most existing approaches primarily focus on obstacle-free or structurally similar environ-ments, and less attention has been given to understanding how transfer behaves under geometric constraints or progressively increasing task complexity [9], [11], [17]. In recent years, DRL has emerged as a mainstream ap-proach for robotic manipulation tasks [7], [9]. DRL algo-rithms are generally categorized into on-policy and off-policy methods [18]. On-policy algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [18], are known for their robustness in policy optimization, although they tend to be less sample-efficient. In contrast, off-policy algorithms, including Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) [19], Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) [20], Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) [21], and Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) [22], offer greater sample efficiency by leveraging experience replay and reusing previously collected transitions. This property makes them particularly suitable for large-scale tasks that require prolonged interaction with the environment. To address the challenges posed by sparse rewards and large action spaces, [23] has introduced Hindsight Experience Replay (HER), which replaces the originally intended goal with the goal actually achieved in a given episode before storing it in the replay buffer. This technique significantly improves learning efficiency in sparse-reward settings. We build on TQC as our backbone, as it is the strongest baseline in our experiments. Foundation models such as CLIP [24], and DINO [25], pre-trained on large-scale visual or multi-modal datasets, are increasingly applied in robotics primarily as perception backbones and high-level task representations, rather than as low-level control policies. Recent work also uses vision-language models as task planners and grounds object-centric motion from human demonstration videos [26], [27]. Their main advantage lies in generalizability and sample efficiency, enabling robots to adapt to new objects and environments; however, domain shift between pre-training data and real-world conditions can degrade performance, and computational overhead may limit real-time deployment. Our framework targets a common bottleneck in this setting: training a DRL policy from scratch for complex, obstacle-rich tasks requires substantial interaction data and long training time. The transfer-based approach we adopt improves sample efficiency through structured knowledge reuse. III. T ASK DECOMPOSITION FOR DEEP REINFORCEMENT

These transfer settings are defined as follows: 

Forward Transfer refers to transferring parameters from a simpler source task to a more complex target task. This includes both single-step forward transfer (also referred to as 

Single Transfer ), where weights and biases of the first layer of critic networks are directly transferred from task τs to τt

(e.g., Reach →Slide or Push →Slide), and multi-step forward transfer (also referred to as Double Transfer ), where param-eters are transferred sequentially across multiple tasks (e.g., 

τ1 → τ2 → τ3), enabling progressive knowledge accumulation across tasks of increasing structural complexity. The objective is to reuse generalizable state–action representations learned under less constrained reward geometry to facilitate learning in more complex environments. 

Reverse Transfer performs the opposite direction (complex 

→simple), allowing us to examine whether representations learned under harder geometric and obstacle constraints can benefit simpler manipulation tasks. 

Neutral Transfer refers to the situation where transferring parameters from a source task does not lead to performance improvement on the target task compared to training from scratch. This may occur when discrepancies in the reward structure or task geometry cause the transferred representations to be insufficiently aligned with the target objective, partic-ularly when task requirements differ (e.g., different object configurations).

A. Simulation 

We evaluated the proposed task decomposition framework in the PyBullet Panda simulation environment on three manip-ulation tasks: Push , Pick-and-Place , and Slide . Each task was implemented in two settings: obstacle-free and obstacle. In obstacle environments, a single cubic obstacle was introduced to increase task complexity. The obstacle center was sampled as 

x ∼ U (0 , 0.4) , y ∼ U (−0.2, 0.2) , z = 0 .05 ,

with a minimum separation constraint of 0.1 m from both the object and the goal to avoid trivial configurations. The obstacle is a fixed-size cube whose position is randomized every episode, while its geometry and count are held fixed. We compared against four off-policy DRL baselines: DDPG [19], TD3 [20], SAC [21], and TQC [22]. Unless otherwise specified, all DRL baselines shared identical hy-perparameters, including a learning rate of 0.001 , batch size of 256 , discount factor γ = 0.95 , soft update coefficient 

τ = 0.05 , and a replay buffer size of 1M; the policy network was implemented as a three-layer MLP with 512 

units per layer, and 2 critics were used for TQC. For sparse-reward baselines, the HER mechanism adopted the ‘future’ goal selection strategy with 4 sampled goals per transition, which was employed to improve sample efficiency. HER is enabled for all sparse-reward settings and disabled whenever the dense redesigned reward (RR) is used, so that sparse and dense settings are never mixed within a single run. Only the total number of training steps was adjusted according to task difficulty. Specifically, Push was trained for 1M steps, Pick&Place for 1M (obstacle-free) and 2M (obstacle) steps, Slide for 3M (obstacle-free) and 4M (obstacle) steps, and Reach for 20 K steps. These shared hyperparameters were tuned for the strongest baseline (TQC) and reused for com-parability, so the comparison is controlled rather than tuned per algorithm; for transfer, the reported convergence-speed gains count target-task steps only, with source-task pretraining as an additional cost that is most economical when a single source is reused across several targets. Each experiment was repeated over 3 independent runs with different random seeds, reporting average success rates and standard deviations. All simulated experiments were run on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU, and a supercomputer with an NVIDIA L40S GPU. The one exception is Pick&Place with obstacle, where performance variance increases across all methods and the transfer configuration ( 0.57 ± 0.25 ) remains below TQC ( 0.67 ±0.22 ); we examine this case in detail below. Pick-and-Place with obstacle is the hardest setting we study, and the only one where the first-layer transfer configuration does not exceed the TQC baseline. Two factors account for this. First, the task couples grasping, transport, and collision avoidance, so a first-layer representation learned on a simpler source (e.g. Reach) encodes approach behavior that, under an obstacle, can bias exploration toward collision-prone approach directions, a neutral-to-negative transfer effect rather than a helpful prior. Second, the setting is high-variance: both TQC (±0.22 ) and the transfer configuration ( ±0.25 ) show large run-to-run spread over 3 seeds, so the 0.10 mean gap falls within the noise and is not statistically conclusive. Importantly, this shortfall is specific to transfer: on the same task the reward-redesign variant reaches a higher plateau (around 0.80 –0.83 ;Fig. 4), comparable to or above TQC, so the framework remains competitive once the appropriate mechanism (RR rather than transfer) is used. Controlled first-layer transfer thus helps when source and target share compatible geometry (Push, Slide) but offers no benefit, and can mildly hurt, when an obstacle reshapes the feasibility region relative to the source; in that regime RR is the more dependable mechanism. To attribute the gains to the design choices, we compare transfer depth on the same backbone and reward: first-layer-only transfer (ours), all-layer critic transfer, and training from scratch. Table III quantifies first-layer transfer against training from scratch, listing the approximate number of training steps required to reach a task-specific success threshold and the final training-curve plateaus, read from Figs. 3–4. Transferring all critic layers, in contrast, consistently slowed convergence and lowered final success relative to training from scratch in all tasks we tested. A plausible explanation is that the deeper layers encode value estimates tailored to the source task’s reward and obstacle configuration, so reusing them initializes the critic with values that are inappropriate for the target task, while the first layer mainly provides a generic state–action encoding (Section 3.2).

We additionally observe that combining RR with TL does not consistently improve performance in obstacle settings. This is because TL may introduce subopti-mal representations when task geometry differs significantly, leading to neutral transfer. In such cases, RR alone can provide more stable learning signals than RR+TL. Unlike the obstacle-free case, reward shaping does not consistently increase the final success rate under geometric constraints, although improvements may be observed in individual runs. 

B. Real-world experiment 

We validate the proposed framework through a real-world experiment on a Franka Emika FR3 robot to assess its applicability to practical manipulation tasks. To ensure safe deployment and mitigate potential risks, a task-specific sim-ulated environment is first constructed in PyBullet, which closely replicates the initial end-effector and object config-urations of the real setup, including object geometries and spatial arrangements. The single best-performing trajectory is selected in simulation and subsequently executed open-loop on the physical robot. Transferring trajectories from simulation to the real robot is challenging, particularly due to discrepancies in the kinematic structure and degrees of freedom between simulated and real systems. To alleviate this issue, a 7-DoF robotic arm model is employed in simulation to align joint-space representations with those of the FR3 robot. Furthermore, to address uncertainties arising from discretized trajectory intervals, linear interpolation is applied to segments with large joint displacements, ensuring smooth execution while satisfying the robot’s kinematic and safety constraints. This paper presents a unified task decomposition framework that integrates controlled few-shot TL with staged RR to improve the sample efficiency and robustness of robotic ma-nipulation under geometric constraints. Experimental results show that the proposed framework improves final success rates by up to 7.8% and 11% on obstacle-aware Push and Slide tasks, respectively, while RR accelerates Pick-and-Place train-ing by up to 73% . The results indicate that the effectiveness of TL depends on task structure and transfer direction. In Push and Slide, TL provides clear gains in convergence speed and final performance, with double transfer further improving performance through progressive structural alignment, while reverse transfer does not degrade performance in simpler tasks. In contrast, for Pick-and-Place with obstacles, TL alone does not provide clear improvements due to geometric mismatch. In this setting, RR is the more reliable mechanism, accelerating early-stage learning, and combining TL with RR still yields substantial training acceleration even when TL alone is not beneficial. More generally, RR is particularly effective in multi-step tasks such as Pick-and-Place, while its impact on final performance under strong geometric constraints is less consistent. The proposed first-layer transfer protocol enables controlled reuse of general state-action representations while avoiding interference from task-specific value structures. The framework is further validated on a real Franka Emika FR3 robot in an obstacle-aware Pick-and-Place setting as a quali-tative sim-to-real feasibility demonstration. Future work will focus on systematically increasing envi-ronmental complexity and strengthening the evaluation. We plan to scale experiments to more random seeds with signif-icance testing and a full per-seed transfer-depth and reward ablation, and to extend the current task settings by varying both the number and geometric properties of obstacles and manipulated objects, including scenarios with diverse obstacle shapes (e.g., cylindrical, irregular, and composite geometries), and objects with varying sizes and contact surfaces. Such structured variations will allow us to evaluate how TL and RR scale under progressively constrained state–action spaces and more intricate collision geometries. We further plan to replace the open-loop real-robot demonstration with closed-loop on-robot policy roll-outs over many randomized trials, reporting real-world success rate, failure statistics, and baselines.
