## Abstract 

Multi-objective reinforcement learning is a fundamental problem for modern AI, with applications spanning post-training, reasoning models, and robotic control. A central challenge is to learn policies that balance competing objectives, such as helpfulness versus honesty in safety and alignment, or speed versus energy in robotics. The optimal policy can vary sharply with objective-weighting combina-tions, and the extent of this variation depends crucially on the degree of conflict among objectives, making per-preference policy learning sample-inefficient. In this paper, we introduce objective-conflict coefficients and derive near-optimal regret bounds for online settings using this measure, thereby quantifying the impact of misalignment on learning efficiency. Then, we design routed ensemble policy learn-ing, an algorithm that maintains a small set of basis policies and dynamically routes trajectories to the policy with the highest potential reward. Finally, we consider trajectory selection as another approach to mitigate objective conflict, and design a kernel surrogate trajectory attribution algorithm that estimates how feedback signals from a set of trajectories influence policy learning on other trajectories. The kernel can capture nonlinear interactions such as synergy and antagonism between trajectories. We evaluate our approach across diverse environments and bench-marks: routed ensembles improve average performance by 4.3% over baselines for online environments from Meta-World and MO-Gymnasium, and kernel surrogate attribution improves correlation with leave-one-out analysis over existing baselines by 50%. When applied to trajectory selection, kernel-based attribution yields a 27% improvement in reward over baseline attribution methods. 

## 1 Introduction 

Given m potentially competing objectives in a reinforcement learning (RL) environment, how can we learn a small set of policies that maximizes expected reward under any specified preference weighting? This problem is fundamental in multi-agent systems [29], and is ubiquitous in modern AI systems. In robotics, agents often need to balance speed and energy efficiency. In reinforcement learning with human feedback, reasoning models, and post-training, models are often evaluated under competing criteria such as helpfulness, safety, and honesty [20, 37]. A core challenge in multi-objective RL (MORL) is that the optimal policy can vary drastically with objective weight combinations. For example, even small changes in preference can lead to qualitatively different optimal policies [34, 55]. Prior work has focused on approximating the Pareto frontier of the optimal reward trade-offs [42, 52, 55], or learning one policy for each objective [44, 53], and it remains unclear whether or when transferring trajectories across objectives is effective. A key yet underexplored question is that the difficulty of learning across preferences is fundamentally tied to the degree of misalignment among objectives. When objective weightings are well aligned (e.g., rewarding both final and intermediate progress), additional objectives can facilitate learning,

and data collected under one preference can benefit policy learning for others. When objectives are misaligned or adversarially-injected (e.g., RLHF with corrupted reward components), naive transfer can become inefficient or even harmful [20, 27]. This problem is especially acute in high-dimensional settings, making it a critical open challenge to identify objective alignment [21]. To address this challenge, we introduce a new theoretical framework based on measuring objective conflict coefficients that quantify the degree of misalignment among objectives through the χ2-divergence. Our analysis shows that this coefficient interacts with the coverability coefficient [46]— which captures how well a single exploration policy can cover the preference space—to determine the sample complexity of online MORL. Our regret bound generalizes single-objective results [1, 19, 46, 7, 9] and is the first to formalize how objective misalignment affects learning efficiency across preferences. Our theoretical framework builds on recent advances in transfer learning under distribution shifts [50]. However, the technical challenge in MORL lies in quantifying the discrepancy in policy learning. Our main result, Theorem 3.6, relies on a low-rank basis assumption, along with a coverability condition that ensures sufficient exploration of the basis. Motivated by our theoretical framework, we propose routed ensemble policy learning , an online algo-rithm for learning a small set of basis policies, which can be applied to a real-world RL environment. This algorithm alternates between two steps: (i) routing each trajectory to the basis policy with the highest expected return under the corresponding preference weight, and (ii) partitioning trajectories into disjoint clusters and updating each basis policy using its assigned trajectories with a standard policy learning algorithm such as proximal policy optimization. Next, we consider an alternative perspective for tackling objective conflicts, based on selecting trajectories. This motivates the problem of quantifying the influence of individual trajectories upon the learned optimal policy, a problem for which we term trajectory attribution . We design a scalable kernel-based trajectory attribution procedure that estimates how trajectories help or hinder policy learning. Our procedure fits a kernel ridge regression surrogate to approximate policy performance across trajectory subsets. This can capture both individual contributions and higher-order interactions between trajectories. These interactions are common in high-dimensional settings and often underlie unexpected transfer behavior [21]. However, they are not captured by first-order influence approximations such as influence functions [36], linear surrogate models [26, 56], or gradient-similarity-based analysis [45, 17]. Our approach goes beyond prior work by leveraging efficient approximations grounded in policy gradients and random projections [55]. The results from more accurate trajectory attribution can guide trajectory selection in downstream policy learning. Lastly, we empirically validate our approach on the Meta-World robotics benchmark and high-dimensional MO-Gymnasium tasks. Our algorithm consistently outperforms multi-objective base-lines, achieving a significant reduction in cumulative regret in environments with structured objective conflicts. Specifically, routed ensemble learning outperforms the strongest baseline by 4.3% on the Meta-World benchmark and achieves an 8% average improvement in hypervolume on MO-Gymnasium tasks. Similar results are also observed in reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) settings. Additionally, kernel-based attribution provides a 50% improvement in terms of the Spearman correlations with ground-truth leave-one-out influence scores. When applied to down-stream task selection, kernel-based selection yields a 27% higher reward than existing attribution baselines. We present detailed ablation studies to validate the theoretical insights from our proposed framework. For instance, after the routed ensemble procedure, the coverability metric drops from 1.3

for a single policy to 0.2 for the ensemble policies, indicating that routing mitigates the exploration bottleneck caused by objective conflicts. We also conduct a detailed analysis on the number of basis policies in the ensemble, as well as validate a low-rank basis assumption in our theoretical setup. In summary, the contributions of this paper are threefold. • First, we introduce a new theoretical framework for MORL based on a χ2 measure that we call the objective conflict coefficient. The theoretical result can be relaxed to a setting with an approximate low-rank basis, and we empirically evaluate this assumption in various real-world environments. • Second, we design two algorithms for tackling objective conflicts: (1) routed ensemble policy learning, and (2) kernel-based trajectory attribution to estimate inter-trajectory influence. • Third, we demonstrate significant gains in sample efficiency and robustness across diverse MORL benchmarks covering language modeling, robotic control, and RLHF settings. 2For the rest of this paper, we begin by stating the formal problem setup in Section 2. ## 3 Our Approach 

We first provide several examples to illustrate how multi-objective misalignment affects policy learning. We then formalize this observation through the notion of objective conflict coefficient and show how it interacts with coverability to derive online regret bounds. The regret bound is shown to be tight with a matching lower bound. These theoretical insights motivate our algorithm, routed ensemble policy learning, which learns a small set of basis policies and routes trajectories to the policies that give the highest reward. Lastly, to interpret objective conflict and routing behavior, we develop a kernel-surrogate trajectory attribution algorithm that quantifies how individual trajectories influence policy learning across preference weights.

Example 3.1 (Learning the Pareto frontier of a MOMDP) . The Pareto frontier consists of policies for which no objective can be improved without worsening at least one other objective. Formally, a policy π dominates π′ (denoted π ≻ π′) if Ji(π) ≥ Ji(π′) for all objectives i ∈ [m], with strict inequality for at least one i, where Ji(π) is the i-th entry of J(π).

Example 3.2 (Online exploration under corrupted objectives) . Consider an MOMDP with m = 2 ,where r1 is the clean task reward and r2 may be corrupted (possibly adversarial). This motivates the need to identify corrupted trajectories and to attribute their alignment with w.

aligned objectives , (ii) conflicting objectives with an (approximately) linear Pareto frontier , and (iii) 

conflicting objectives with a strictly concave frontier . Aligned objectives admit a single policy that is optimal for all preferences. Linear conflict is captured by two corner policies whose mixtures span the frontier. Concave conflict requires additional intermediate policies beyond the corner solutions. When m grows, different objectives can simultaneously fall into different regimes—some aligned, linearly conflicting, and others exhibiting concave trade-offs. These induce discontinuous shifts and make policy optimization harder.

3.3 Learning routed ensemble policies 

In Sections 3.1 and 3.2, the basis policies are assumed latent. Next, we design a scalable routed ensemble policy learning algorithm that: (i) explicitly learn a set of N basis policies, with each trajectory routed to the highest-reward basis, and (ii) learn a preference-conditioned transfer operator fw : Π N → Π that maps these basis policies to a composite policy for any given preference w.5We also clarify that the ensemble size N may or may not be the same as K as stated in Assumption 3.4. ## 4 Experiments 

Our experiments are designed to address the following two aspects: (1) to verify the theoretical insights from the regret analysis, and (2) to evaluate the empirical performance of our algorithms across multi-objective RL settings, including synthetic environments, continuous robotic control, and RLHF settings. We highlight three findings. First, routing mitigates negative transfer and reduces cumulative regret in synthetic environments. Second, routed ensembles outperform baselines by a 4.3% relative margin in multi-task RL, up to 48% in multi-objective RL, and 17% in large language model benchmarks. Third, our kernel surrogates improve correlation with ground-truth marginal gains by 50% for trajectory attribution and improve downstream task-selection reward by 27%. Our code repository can be found at https://github.com/VirtuosoResearch/Multi-objective-reinforcement-learning-code/.

## 5 Related Work 

A reinforcement learning environment is typically formalized by means of a Markov decision process [40, 2]. Existing approaches in the literature on multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) generally fall into two categories. The first reduces MORL to single-objective RL through scalarization, optimizing a fixed or sampled preference vector to learn a single policy [14, 33, 32, 38]. The second, which includes our work, examines the multi-objective nature of the problem by learning multiple policies (e.g., to approximate the Pareto frontier) to address sharp variation in optimal policies across preferences [42, 44, 30]. Recent work [55] proposes a convex relaxation algorithm for partitioning multiple objectives into disjoint, related groups. We build on this line of work by providing a theoretical characterization of objective conflicts in MORL, introducing a routed ensemble algorithm, and developing trajectory-level attribution to interpret model behavior . One interesting future direction is to examine the policy learning dynamics in multi-objective Markov decision processes [22, 24]. Lastly, sequential decision-making problems also occur in experimental design, where one often needs to roll out an experiment through staggered adoption [48, 47]. Recent work studies both offline and online RL with linear function approximation [15, 16]. It would be interesting to understand the techniques therein and examine whether it would be possible to extend to learning optimal policies under multiple objectives under linear Bellman completeness. More broadly, we refer the reader to a recent monograph by Foster and Rakhlin [13] for references. Influence estimation techniques [23, 35] have recently been extended from supervised learning to reinforcement learning to trace model behaviors. In the context of offline RL, [11] proposes a framework to attribute specific policy decisions to past trajectories. A fundamental challenge in the online RL setting is the circular dependency where current policy updates influence future data distribution. Recent work [17] addresses this by employing local data attribution via gradient similarity to identify and filter harmful transitions. 11 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we study how to learn policies that balance competing objectives in multi-objective reinforcement learning. We first analyze the theoretical limits of transfer learning across preferences, highlighting that structural misalignment between objectives is the primary cause of negative transfer. To overcome this challenge, we propose a routed ensemble algorithm guided by a new measure, the objective conflict coefficient. Furthermore, we introduce a kernel-based trajectory-attribution method that captures widespread nonlinear interactions among trajectories and can improve subsequent policy learning. The experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves superior sample efficiency and attribution accuracy compared to state-of-the-art baselines across diverse benchmarks. One limitation of our theoretical framework is that the regret bounds do not directly apply to the routed ensemble algorithm. Further understanding the sample complexity of learning ensemble policies in multi-objective Markov decision processes is an interesting question for future work.

Remark A.1. We describe several natural extensions of our approach. First, our algorithms naturally extend to online multitask RL [55], where the state-action space varies across tasks. We leave further exploration of these benchmarks to future work. Second, kernel surrogates can also be used to measure the influence of an individual trajectory on the overall model behavior. This can be accomplished by sampling subsets of trajectories instead of objectives in the above algorithm. Lastly, while we primarily focus on probabilistic policies, similar ideas can be extended to determin-istic policies. 

Remark A.2. Our problem formulation relates to multi-objective optimization in prior literature [34, 6], and we extend this line of work to the online learning setting. Further exploring the connection between ensemble learning and online boosting [5] is another direction for future work. Our work connects to the literature on influence estimation and mechanistic interpretability, which aims to trace model behavior back to influential training data. Prior work include influence func-tions [23], TracIn [35] and linear surrogate models [26]. In reinforcement learning, recent efforts have adapted these techniques to explain policy decisions, including trajectory-level attribution in offline RL [11] and snapshot influence functions for filtering harmful data in online RL [17]. One of the open questions in this area is to develop better theoretical characterizations of attribution methods. Our kernel-based objective attribution thus offers a principled approach and accurately estimates second-order interactions across preferences. This connection seems particularly relevant in light of recent advances in RL reasoning [4].

The constructed family has K pairwise-disjoint sub-chains, one per objective, each containing one Bernoulli two-armed bandit. Here, a sub-chain means a branch of the environment state space, not a policy: the k-th optimal basis policy is the policy that routes into sub-chain Sk and then plays the better binary action. Under a cyclic preference sequence, regret has two sources: 1) a routing term for episodes whose first action does not match the active preference; 2) K independent bandit terms for wrong binary choices inside correctly routed sub-chains. Case 3 (Conflicting objectives with a concave frontier). We remove the central barrier while maintain-ing the separated targets μ1 = ( −2, 0) and μ2 = (2 , 0) . Given a buffer B(k), PPO performs a number of gradient steps to update θ from θ(k) to θ(k+1) by maximizing a clipped surrogate objective. For a single record z = ( s, a, r, . . . ), let ℓPPO (θ; z) denote the per-record loss corresponding to the standard clipped PPO objective (possibly combined with value and entropy terms).
