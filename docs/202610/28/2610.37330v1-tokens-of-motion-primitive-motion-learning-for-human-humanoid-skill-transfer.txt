## ABSTRACT 

Human videos provide a scalable source of manipulation experience, yet trans-ferring them to robots remains challenging because human demonstrations lack robot-compatible actions and differ substantially from robots in embodiment and kinematics. We propose Motion Primitives for Action Composition and Transfer 

(M-PACT), a framework that addresses this challenge by factorizing primitive mo-tion learning from embodiment-specific motion realization. This separation allows us to learn generalizable and reusable motion primitives from large-scale 3D human hand-pose trajectories, while requiring only limited embodiment-specific robot data to ground these primitives into executable motions. Specifically, we first discover local motion segments from human hand trajectories and learn a compact primitive-motion representation that captures recurring manipulation patterns independently of robot embodiment. Given task and observation context, these primitives are then selected and composed, while a lightweight embodiment-specific decoder maps them to robot motions. Crucially, transferring to a new embodiment preserves the learned primitive representation and requires adapting only the realization model, substantially reducing the dependence on costly robot demonstrations. Experiments across EgoDex, HoloAssist, and HumanoidEveryday demonstrate cross-dataset and cross-embodiment transferability of proposed M-PACT, with lightweight decoder adaptation achieving strong humanoid motion reconstruction using as few as ∼100 robot demonstrations. These results establish factorized primitive learning as a scalable bridge from large-scale human motion data to humanoid manipulation. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Developing robots capable of diverse manipulation in unstructured environments remains a central goal in robotics and artificial intelligence. Most manipulation policies are learned from robot-centric experience, including demonstrations collected through teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching and interactions acquired through reinforcement learning or autonomous exploration. Although such data provide directly executable actions, collecting them at sufficient scale and diversity remains costly and difficult across tasks, environments, and embodiments Brohan et al. (2022); O’Neill et al. (2024). Human videos offer a compelling alternative: they are abundant and capture diverse object interactions, behavioral strategies, and long-horizon activities in natural settings Grauman et al. (2022). They can therefore complement limited robot demonstrations and broaden the task and behavioral coverage of robot learning systems Kareer et al. (2025). However, human demonstrations lack robot-compatible action supervision, while human and robot effectors differ substantially in morphology, kinematics, appearance, and feasible action spaces Kareer et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025). Directly imitating human trajectories can consequently produce ambiguous or kinematically infeasible robot actions. Bridging this embodiment gap requires representations that abstract away embodiment-specific execution while preserving transferable motion structure, for effective and generalizable robot control. Vision-language-action (VLA) models (Pertsch et al., 2025; Black et al., 2024; Intelligence et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Brohan et al., 2023) offer a promising framework for general-purpose manipulation

However, two limitations remain. First, human videos lack robot-executable actions, so converting them into control supervision still requires costly robot demonstrations or computationally intensive human-motion estimation. Second, existing pretraining paradigms often couple human motion with scene appearance and context rather than explicitly isolating transferable motion structure, limiting generalization across scenes, tasks, and embodiments. Existing human-to-robot skill transfer approaches primarily bridge the embodiment gap by geometric alignment of human hand and robot trajectories Kareer et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025); Shi et al. (2025); Haldar & Pinto (2025); Zhou et al. (2026); Li et al. (2024), visual transformation of human demonstrations into robot-centric observations Li et al. (2025); Lepert et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2026); Lai et al. (2026). However, hand-object manipulation is inherently compositional and behaviorally multimodal: a single task may comprise heterogeneous motions such as grasping, lifting, placing, etc ., each admitting multiple valid executions. Representing such a skill as a monolithic, full-trajectory supervision signal requires an imitation-learning policy to model long and highly variable motion sequences while simultaneously resolving human-robot embodiment differences, making learning difficult and limiting the reuse of shared behaviors across tasks. Recent approaches have begun to introduce temporal hierarchy, however, these methods generally transfer human behavior through complete trajectories Singh et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025), coarse semantic subtasks Mu et al. (2026); Shi et al. (2025), fixed-horizon trajectory and action predictions Haldar & Pinto (2025); Zhou et al. (2026). Consequently, recurring local motion patterns can remain coupled to task-level ordering and global scene context, limiting their systematic recomposition and transfer across novel tasks and environments. To this end, we propose to learn Motion Primitives for Action Composition and Transfer (M-PACT), a framework that learns localized primitive motions from human videos by factorizing transferable interaction structure from scene- and embodiment-specific realizations. This factorization allows individual primitives to be transferred across embodiments and compositionally reused across tasks and environments. At its core, a self-supervised motion segmentation module, SkillSeg, partitions 3D human hand trajectories using frame-level kinematic cues, and maps the resulting segments into an embodiment-agnostic vocabulary of primitive-motion tokens, by Embodied Primitive Motion Tokenization (EPMT) module. Learned from web-scale egocentric video corpora, EPMT transforms abundant, behaviorally diverse, and cost-effective human pose trajectories into reusable manipulation primitives. We then condition these primitive representations on contextual features from visual foundation models and perform lightweight fine-tuning using labeled video-motion-action data, enabling M-PACT to select, compose, and realize primitives for diverse manipulation tasks. By sepa-rating reusable motion structure from task-specific context and robot-specific control, our approach substantially reduces the need for costly robot demonstrations while improving generalization across tasks and scenes. In summary, our contributions are threefold: 2• We propose to learn a motion tokenizer, EPMT that discovers localized, embodiment-agnostic primitive motions from human hand trajectories. Trained on low-cost, web-scale hand-pose data extracted from egocentric videos, EPMT learns primitive tokens that can be reused for skill transfer across embodiments. • We introduce M-PACT, a multi-stage transfer framework that integrates EPMT with context-aware visual representations to select and compose primitive tokens and ground them into embodiment-specific, robot-executable actions. This design supports manipulation tasks of varying horizons with lightweight fine-tuning on labeled video-motion-action data. • We evaluate motion reconstruction, cross-dataset representation reuse, humanoid decoder adap-tation, and contextual primitive prediction across three datasets, complemented by qualitative real-world humanoid demonstrations. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Learning Manipulation from Human Demonstrations. Human demonstrations offer a scalable source of manipulation experience, motivating approaches based on shared representations, geometric alignment, and motion retargeting. EgoMimic jointly trains policies from aligned egocentric human and robot data, while Point Policy predicts keypoint trajectories that are converted into robot actions through geometric constraints Kareer et al. (2025); Haldar & Pinto (2025). For dexterous manipu-lation, DexCap transfers captured hand motions through inverse kinematics and imitation learning, whereas DexImit reconstructs hand–object interactions and synthesizes robot demonstrations through subtask decomposition and trajectory generation Wang et al. (2024); Mu et al. (2026). In contrast, we first learn a compact vocabulary of local hand-motion primitives, then separately model contextual selection and embodiment-specific decoding, disentangling reusable motion structure from task context and robot execution. 

Temporal Abstraction and Reusable Skill Discovery. Temporal abstraction organizes demon-strations into reusable behavioral units. CompILE jointly discovers segment boundaries and latent behaviors, while Play-LMP and SPiRL learn latent plans or skill priors from unstructured experience Kipf et al. (2019); Lynch et al. (2020); Pertsch et al. (2021). MimicPlay learns a planner through human hand-trajectory prediction and uses it to guide a robot policy trained from teleoperated demon-strations Wang et al. (2023a). Our approach instead discovers boundaries from hand kinematics and represents each segment through factorized translation, rotation, and aperture effects. These local primitives are not predefined semantic subtasks and can therefore be reused across demonstrated skills before contextual prediction is learned over the fixed vocabulary. 

Discrete Action Representations and Motion Tokenization. Discrete action representations support categorical prediction and sequence modeling. VQ-BeT uses hierarchical vector quantization to model multimodal actions, while FAST tokenizes transformed robot action sequences for efficient autoregressive learning Lee et al. (2024); Pertsch et al. (2025). LAPA extends action tokenization to videos without robot labels by learning latent actions between frames and subsequently adapting with robot data Ye et al. (2025). Our framework instead learns from segmented 3D hand trajectories under a shared kinematic convention. Translation and aperture use independent discrete codebooks, rotation remains continuous, and the decoder reconstructs each segment’s net effect conditioned on its initial state. This structure enables embodiment-specific adaptation while keeping the motion encoders and codebooks fixed.

Coverage measures inclusion of the recorded code rather than feasibility or task success; candidate-set construction is detailed in the supplement. 4.3 REAL WORLD EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate our framework on a real humanoid through four increasingly challenging settings: a) EPMT – Motion Reconstruction. We compress a previously executed robot manipulation episode into discrete primitive tokens and decode them back into robot motion, testing whether real manipulation can be faithfully represented through a compact token space, b) Human2Robot – Cross-Embodiment Transfer. Primitive decisions are extracted from egocentric human demonstrations and executed by the humanoid, with only the decoder adapted, testing whether human-derived motion primitives can transfer across embodiments, c) CC-EPMT (State-Only) – Closed-Loop Token Prediction. Rather than providing the token sequence, the robot predicts the next primitive from its current arm state, executes it, and re-observes, enabling autonomous closed-loop primitive composition without visual or task context, d) CC-EPMT (Full) – Context-Conditioned Manipulation. We further condition primitive prediction on the robot’s camera observation and task instruction, testing whether visual and task context improves the selection and execution of appropriate motion primitives. Together, these four settings progressively validate EPMT from motion reconstruction and cross-embodiment transfer to autonomous closed-loop prediction and context-conditioned manipulation. The results demonstrate how the same primitive token representation can bridge human motion and real-world humanoid execution across different levels of autonomy and context. Qualitative results can be found in https://tinyurl.com/ykxkbu3z and supplementary. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented M-PACT, a framework that bridges human demonstrations and humanoid manipulation through a shared primitive motion representation. By separating reusable motion structure from embodiment-specific realization, EPMT enables primitive reuse across datasets and adaptation to humanoid motion through a lightweight decoder, while CC-EPMT extends these primitives to context-conditioned prediction and closed-loop execution. Experiments across human and humanoid datasets, together with real-world humanoid demonstrations, validate the framework from motion 9reconstruction and cross-embodiment transfer to autonomous manipulation. Overall, our results demonstrate the potential of factorized primitive motion tokens as a reusable interface between scalable human motion data and embodiment-specific robot control.
