# Whole-Body Humanoid Control via Stochastic Motion Tokenization 

Michael Drolet 1, Oleg Arenz 1, and Jan Peters 1,2,3,4

Abstract — While velocity-commanded RL policies are effec-tive for controlling quadrupeds, they are a limited interface for humanoids that need to perform complex whole-body motions. Furthermore, RL tracking policies for whole-body motions typically have limited information about the desired future motion, preventing them from performing controlled deviations to achieve long-term task success; such information is particularly relevant when a controller must prepare for transitions between behaviors. We address these challenges using concise motion representations that can be generated by a high-level controller, given a behavior label or text command. These representations can be fed directly into a tracking policy to provide a detailed motion reference. Our method exhibits a high degree of flexibility in performing real-world robot motions such as dancing, fighting, or walking based on conditional in-formation. At matched capacity, our adopted stochastic motion tokenizer reconstructs motions from the Unitree G1 at a level that exceeds state-of-the-art approaches such as finite scalar quantization (FSQ) and the vector-quantized autoencoder (VQ-VAE). The resulting discrete interface conditions whole-body tracking policies that execute generated dance, fight, and walk motions lasting 16 .8 s each and a 35 s fight-to-dance sequence on a physical Unitree G1. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Whole-body humanoid motion is high-dimensional and, at any instant, admits many plausible continuations: from mid-stride, a human may keep walking, turn, gesture, or transition into an entirely different behavior. Classical hu-manoid control builds on dynamic models and optimiza-tion [1]–[3] or motion primitives [4], while RL-based ap-proaches [5], [6] can exhibit emergent behaviors but require reward specification, which is challenging to define explicitly for tasks such as human-like locomotion. Controllers built around fixed gait generators or single-clip tracking fail to capture this multiplicity, ultimately reproducing only one trajectory or motion style well. At the same time, methods based on maximum likelihood, such as behavioral cloning, require action labels via methods such as teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, which is difficult to scale and infeasible for dynamic locomotion behaviors. Furthermore, maximum likelihood is mode averaging, leading to muddied behaviors on unimodal policies. A natural question is how to resolve the dilemma of choos-ing between a specialist policy that can perform one task very well and a generalist policy that can perform many tasks less effectively. We address this problem by grouping motion into larger chunks and providing them as concise motion representations to a reinforcement learning policy, enabling it

This separation is inspired by motor learn-ing theory [7], which argues that, through practice, motor skills become grouped to abstract away nuances exhibited at each timestep. This principle also appears in robot learning: Diffusion Policy and ACT both predict chunks of future ac-tions rather than isolated commands [8], [9], leading to better performance than traditional imitation-learning methods [10]. We therefore view it as a necessity to understand how to group motions in an unsupervised and efficient way, similar to the problem of tokenizing sentences in natural language processing. Ideally, the abstracted representations should be i) compact for computational efficiency and generative utility, ii) seamlessly composable for long-horizon tasks, and iii) expressive enough to capture a wide range of sequences. Our proposed framework is tailored to humanoid whole-body motion; however, the same key principles can be extended to other embodiments and problem settings. To achieve these desirable characteristics, we use a motion tokenizer in the form of a discrete variational autoencoder (VAE). Discrete VAEs offer high bit-efficiency compared to their continuous counterparts, and their categorical rep-resentations make them amenable to downstream generative tasks through the cross-entropy loss, which naturally leads to multimodal posterior sampling. However, popular discrete to-kenizers, such as FSQ and VQ-VAE, are either deterministic or limited to simplified distributions based on the mean-field assumption, which prevents them from correlating different tokens. Furthermore, unlike their continuous counterparts, which aim at generative modeling, they are motivated from a compression perspective and are unable to control their entropy for optimizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Instead, we adopt DAPS [11] for the first stage of our pipeline, which is a stochastic discrete autoencoder whose encoder admits a categorical distribution that is factorized autoregressively and has controlled entropy. When using DAPS to encode (or tokenize ) motions to discrete latents (or tokens ), we find that reconstruction is consistently better arXiv:2610.36452v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 at matched bottleneck capacity. Building upon this stage, we propose a second-stage model, TrajectoryGPT, to generate a motion sequence autoregressively, and train it using the frozen VAE to encode prediction targets for autoregressive next-token prediction, as is common in related work [12]. TrajectoryGPT enables us to schedule and sequence units of motion, all of which would be plausible under the distribution of trajectories in the training dataset. Because TrajectoryGPT is additionally conditioned on a context that describes the motion, it serves as an interface between the human and the robot. This general interface can be extended beyond labels or text to include goal-conditioned information or vision input. Finally, we realize these units of motion using a low-level tracking policy, as part of our third stage. The tracking policy receives a buffer of latent codes—where tokens in the buffer can be set to special wildcard tokens for transitions—to help inform the policy of the upcoming intended behavior. In this regard, the policy can be seen as a decoder responsible for realizing the physical motion (under domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer). In short, our contributions are:

a) Discrete tokenizers: Discretizing data into tokens has been explored in both computer graphics and robotics. In the case of computer graphics, discrete encodings are a popular way to distill a downstream model conditioned on rich context such as language for text-to-image gener-ation [15]. For robotics, approaches such as BEAST [16] and FAST [17], based on splines and frequency-domain transformations, respectively, are popular ways to repre-sent actions, or regression targets, for transformer-based behavioral cloning. An alternative is to use learning-based approaches, where two of the most common methods for encoding discrete latents include the vector-quantized VAE (VQ-VAE) [14] and finite scalar quantization (FSQ) [13]. However, one drawback of vector-quantized methods is that decoding samples from a uniform latent prior (i.e., approximately sampling from p(x)) often yields low-quality generative samples, as the decoder sees a limited combina-tion of latent codes due to the compression objective [11]. Nevertheless, using discrete latents as tokens for motion synthesis (by reusing the VAE’s decoder to convert tokens to kinematic sequences) is relatively nascent in robotics. PoseGPT [12] quantizes human poses with a VQ-VAE [14] and uses the encoded codebook indices to represent the quantized motion tokens. DAPS [11] presents an alternative approach for training the encoder based on policy search; as a result, its latent space is a properly normalized, stochastic, categorical distribution, whose entropy and training stability can be carefully controlled. Compared to vector-quantized and Gumbel-softmax-based approaches, DAPS has proven beneficial on benchmark-scale datasets such as ImageNet 

256 ×256 , in terms of both reconstruction loss and latent code utilization. In this work, we adopt DAPS as a tokenizer for motion trajectories to take advantage of its benefits. 

b) Physics-based motion imitation: Motion imitation has been commonly achieved using reinforcement learning. Adversarial methods such as AMP and ASE [18], [19], in which a discriminator-derived reward guides the policy toward expert-like behavior, have proven highly effective in simulation. When framed as a standard RL problem with tracking rewards, DeepMimic [20] has gained much popularity. These approaches address a key question for motion imitation: namely, given a desired kinematic se-quence, which sequence of actions can generate the same behavior? DeepMimic addresses this using an explicit reward signal, designed to penalize the policy for deviating from a reference motion (typically represented as joint angles and joint velocities [21]) with a smooth exponential kernel. In simulation, recent work has scaled motion imitation through simulated-avatar controllers [22] and distilled universal latent motion representations [23]. On real humanoid hardware, related advances include real-time whole-body teleopera-tion [24], [25], expressive body control with decoupled tracking [26], shadowing-based imitation [27], and large-scale motion tracking with SONIC [28]. While these systems track observed or teleoperated motion (provided as FSQ encodings for SONIC), our method generates an indefinite sequence of motion from an autoregressive model. Unsurprisingly, the utility of motion-tracking policies de-pends heavily on the reference motions. A growing area of research investigates how to synthesize or retarget motions for trackers [29], [30]. An ongoing research problem, how-ever, is improving the quality of motions when conditioned on task information such as goals or language. 

c) Conditioned motion generation: Non-physics-based, or “kinematics only”, methods—largely popular in the com-puter graphics community—have become increasingly rele-vant for generating general-purpose motion for whole-body control. Two dominant approaches, largely influenced by the adoption of multimodal generative models, are con-tinuous diffusion and next-token prediction. The motion diffusion model [31] proposes a continuous diffusion-based approach for synthesizing motion from contextual informa-tion such as language. CLoSD [32] closes the loop be-tween such a text-to-motion diffusion planner and a physics-based tracking controller. Autoregressive approaches in-clude T2M-GPT [33], MotionGPT [34], and the quadruped-focused T2QRM [35]. TextOp [36] recently demonstrated this generator–tracker separation for interactive, language-conditioned motion on humanoid hardware. Recent large-scale efforts also learn generative models of motion, both kinematic [37] and physics-based [38]. III. M ETHOD 

Our learning framework is divided into three stages: motion tokenization, autoregressive motion generation, and training of a low-level tracking policy. We use LAFAN1 [39] and the BONES-SEED dataset [40] to train the first stage, where the motions are already retargeted to the Unitree G1 29-DoF robot. Motions are first resampled to 30 Hz and windowed into 24 -frame ( t=0 .8 s) segments. The robot state is the configuration q ∈ R36 (root pose and 29 joint angles).

The actor observes proprioception, a phase variable, and a rolling buffer of length L=3 latent chunks. Training uses 2048 parallel GPU-simulated environments along with domain randomization for actuator gains, link masses, friction, and pushes. Penalties on the joint acceler-ations and torques are also introduced to help reduce jitter on the policy and soften the robot’s response for sim-to-real transfer. All episodes start from a randomly chosen standing pose or from reference states. When starting in a standing pose, the robot observes a wildcard latent chunk, indicating that it must learn to freely transition into a state that allows for good tracking performance on the reference motion. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

Our experiments assess each stage of the pipeline, cul-minating in deployment on the real robot. We evaluate on the two datasets of Sec. III: BONES-SEED and LAFAN1, across multiple seeds. Sec. IV-A compares tokenizers at matched bits, Sec. IV-B evaluates conditional generation, and Sec. IV-C explores how to provide latent codes to the low-level policy in simulation and on hardware. 

A. Stage 1: Tokenizer Baseline at Matched Bits 

All three tokenizers are trained at matched architectures and bottleneck: 16 tokens, vocab size 210 , embed dim 128 ,for 250 k steps across five seeds. For FSQ, we use the recommended [8 , 5, 5, 5] levels [13] for an approximately 

210 vocab size. DAPS achieved the lowest validation joint position MSE on both BONES-SEED ( 7.1 ± 0.3, versus 

9.0±0.4 for FSQ and 11 .5±1.7 for VQ-VAE) and LAFAN1 (14 .4 ± 1.0, versus 19 .0 ± 1.5 and 28 .3 ± 3.0, respectively), in units of 10 −3. −994 .7 ± 16 .7), with all values reported as mean ± standard deviation over five seeds. DAPS’s advantage likewise held on the training splits, where it achieved the lowest raw-qpos MSE and highest conditional log-likelihood on both datasets. Fig. 5 demonstrates how DAPS exposes a controllable trade-off between token diversity and reconstruction fidelity. Across all 5.58 million segments in the BONES-SEED validation split, each segment was encoded at several temper-atures and compared with its deterministic T =0 encoding. B. Stage 2: Conditional Generation 

Based on its superior reconstruction performance in Stage 1, we select DAPS as the tokenizer for the remainder of the pipeline. We encode the training datasets with the frozen DAPS encoder and train TrajectoryGPT to predict the next DAPS token chunk from the behavior condition and latent history. At inference, generation begins from either an encoded motion prefix or the learned start token and proceeds one chunk at a time. Each predicted chunk is decoded into 0.8 s of G1 kinematics and returned to the latent context, producing open-ended, context-conditioned references for the Stage 3 tracking policy. While cross-entropy serves as the Stage 2 training objective, a lower loss indicates increasingly accurate token prediction within the context of known continuations, and a value approaching zero can be consistent with memorization. We therefore use Stage 2 to produce long reference motions for Stage 3 and do not treat cross-entropy alone as a diagnostic for motion quality. C. Stage 3: Tracking and Transfer 

We train three latent-conditioned PPO policies with the reward and observations of Sec. III to test the same learned interface with different motion-bank compositions. One pol-icy tracks 25 diverse generated motions, another tracks three generated base motions and all nine transition pairings (including same-style transitions), and a third tracks a smaller bank of five motions. Stage 3 uses a subset of the motions produced by the LAFAN1-trained TrajectoryGPT model; Fig. 2 shows six examples from the 25 -motion policy. Each stitched sequence concatenates two independently generated motions: 21 chunks ( 16 .8 s) from the first behavior, two wildcard chunks ( 1.6 s) with no prescribed kinematic reference, and 21 chunks from the second behavior, for a total duration of 35 .2 s. On the G1, the five-motion policy completed generated dance, fight, and walk motions, each lasting 16 .8 s, at 50 Hz while remaining upright and ad-vancing through the complete latent sequence (Fig. 8). The stitching policy completed the full fight-to-dance sequence, including the wildcard interval (Fig. 7). 

D. Implementation 

All stages run in JAX with XLA acceleration. Each stage trains on a single GPU having 24 –80 GB of VRAM; Stage 3 simulates 2048 MuJoCo environments on one GPU. We generate the reported Stage 3 reference banks offline, so TrajectoryGPT latency is excluded from the tracking mea-surements. Deployment consumes the resulting precomputed latent trajectories. The stages are trained sequentially, and each is frozen before the next begins.

V. D ISCUSSION 

Using the same discrete representation for motion genera-tion and tracking provides a direct interface between Trajec-toryGPT and the low-level policy. TrajectoryGPT specifies the intended motion at the token level, while the track-ing policy realizes the decoded motion under the robot’s dynamics. This separation lets us assess errors introduced by reconstruction and physical execution, while errors in autoregression are more difficult to quantify due to the absence of a “ground truth”. DAPS can assign different token sequences to the same motion, and Fig. 5 shows that many retain similar reconstruction accuracy. By changing T ,TrajectoryGPT can be trained on the most likely encoding of each window or on sampled alternatives, without changing either the tokenizer or the Stage 2 architecture. Beyond compression, the latent interface determines how much future intent the controller can observe. Each 0.8 schunk carries 160 bits, or 200 bits/s, and the three-chunk buffer exposes 2.4 s of motion while keeping raw reference poses out of the actor’s observation space. The present TrajectoryGPT is not conditioned on the low-level robot state, so this look-ahead provides a preplanned rather than a 

re-planned motion. To close the loop between the two lev-els [32], TrajectoryGPT could instead condition online on the robot’s own motion: re-encoding the measured history with the frozen DAPS encoder and using these tokens in place of the previously generated chunks requires no change to the architecture, since both share the same token space. Be-cause TrajectoryGPT is trained on dataset histories, however, the re-encoded robot motion could be out of distribution. Relatedly, three variables remain interesting to explore: the chunk duration ( t=0 .8 s) in Stage 1, the length of the latent history ( N =8 ) in Stage 2, and the length of the latent buffer (L=3 ) in Stage 3. Because chunk duration induces different reconstruction errors for a fixed bottleneck, it directly affects the reconstruction performance. Chunk duration may also impact the smoothness of the generated motion for Stage 2, since longer contexts can shrink the set of plausible future continuations. The same applies to N , where larger values can also lead to overfitting. Wildcard chunks remove the reference-tracking objective for the transition interval. The regularization penalties stay active to encourage the robot to remain stable. This gives the state-conditioned policy freedom to connect the surrounding motions while the future latent chunks remain visible. The fight-to-dance trial shows that such a transition can be executed on hardware. Supplying a prior motion for the transition interval could further shape this behavior. A natural next step is a scaling study that varies the repertoire size and transition difficulty and reports per-motion tracking performance. An additional Stage 3 test could hold out latent buffer sequences during policy training and introduce them only at evaluation. This would distinguish the reuse of motion skills from the memorization of complete latent schedules seen in training. Fig. 8 compares the measured robot state with the source kinematics. The desired joint configuration is not provided to the deployed policy, which receives proprioception, phase, and the rolling latent buffer. Error residuals include both policy-tracking and sim-to-real error. Because the reference banks are generated offline and held fixed, these residuals arise downstream of TrajectoryGPT. Neither TrajectoryGPT nor the DAPS decoder runs in the 50 Hz deployment loop. Because the actor consumes tokens directly, high-level up-dates are needed only when a new 0.8 s chunk is required. With two future chunks already buffered, an online Tra-jectoryGPT could run asynchronously and refresh the plan before the 1.6 s look-ahead is exhausted. Online generation is primarily outside the scope of our algorithmic contribution and was not needed to evaluate the method, but could be added if required. Each 24 -frame source chunk is played back over its orig-inal 0.8 s duration at 50 Hz. The phase observation is local to each chunk and is advanced at a fixed rate. Fig. 8 (right) shows this phase resetting at every chunk boundary while the latent index advances through the sequence. Adapting the phase to contact or tracking progress could relax the imposed timing. Furthermore, because planar root position is not explicitly tracked, the tokens specify motion style without specifying a global path. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a three-stage pipeline in which a discrete autoencoder provides the shared interface between motion generation and whole-body control. At a matched 160 -bit bottleneck, DAPS reconstructs G1 motion more accu-rately than FSQ and VQ-VAE while retaining temperature-controlled alternative encodings. TrajectoryGPT learns se-quences in this compact space, and a latent-conditioned policy tracks their decoded motions without receiving raw reference poses. This division allows the high-level model to organize behavior at the token level while the low-level policy remains reactive to environment dynamics. Our results suggest that the recipe behind GPT-style language models can carry over to humanoid control: a stochastic tokenizer defines a motion vocabulary, a trans-former composes behavior one token at a time, and asingle tracking policy reads the token stream on the robot. On a physical Unitree G1, the policy executed individual generated motions and a full fight-to-dance sequence. The measured-state comparison further shows that the deployed policy follows the intended source motion without the motion being exposed explicitly to the actor. Together, these results suggest how discrete motion tokens can serve as both a compact generative vocabulary and a deployable interface for humanoid control. ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS
