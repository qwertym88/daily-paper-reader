Optimality of delayed reward attainment under uncertainty
Chuwei Wang, Alexander Vladimirsky*, Anastasia Bizyaeva*
Abstract— Decision making under uncertainty requires bal-
ancing the value of additional information against the cost of
delaying the decision until that information becomes available.
This challenge is especially prevalent for embodied decisions
such as those faced by animals moving between food sources
and robots selecting navigation targets, where movement
through space shapes both the quality of information and
the cost of acting. We consider a version of this problem,
where the decision is on choosing among rewards of uncer-
tain value, and those rewards can only be attained through
changing the decision maker’s state sufficiently. Our focus is
on interactions between reward learning and state evolution,
restated as a deterministic optimal control problem with two
terminal alternatives, one with a known reward and the other
learned gradually through noisy observations. We demonstrate
that delayed reward attainment is often optimal in a variety of
systems, especially so when the alternatives are initially difficult
to distinguish and the cost of waiting is relatively low. For a
subset of problems, we also derive sufficient conditions that
allow shortening the planning horizon a priori, guaranteeing
that further observations would not affect the value function.
Finally, when observation quality depends on the state, we show
that it is often optimal for a controller to evolve the state
strategically toward more informative sensing locations before
making a decision, in line with the behavior broadly observed
in biological and engineered systems.
I. INTRODUCTION
Many biological and engineered systems must make ir-
reversible decisions under uncertainty. In such settings, an
agent must balance the benefit of making an early decision,
thereby limiting additional costs of delaying, against the ben-
efit of remaining uncommitted while acquiring information
that may improve the eventual choice. This trade-off arises
in foraging, navigation, target identification, and resource
allocation, where values of competing alternatives may be
only partially known. In this paper, we study different ways
in which delaying a decision can be optimal when state
evolution, information acquisition, and uncertain terminal
rewards are all coupled.
Normative theories of decision making balance the value
of acting on current information against the expected value
of future information [1]. Algorithmically, the optimal timing
for choosing among immediately available uncertain rewards
is also well-studied in the literature on sequential sampling
[2]. However, this cost/benefit analysis becomes more subtle

when selecting an alternative does not immediately yield its
reward, but instead requires subsequent state evolution before
the reward can be attained. For example, when animals or
robots make decisions on alternatives with different physical
target locations, rewards can only be obtained through chang-
ing the agent’s physical position. Actions such as movement
maneuvers taken while the decision remains unresolved can
then affect both the information available for making the
decision and the cumulative cost of claiming the reward once
it is selected.
Resulting trade-offs are well-known in both biological and
engineered systems. Across species, longer decision times
have been observed when competing alternatives are difficult
to distinguish, including alternatives with similar subjective
values in monkeys [3] and perceptual discrimination tasks
in bumblebees and mice [4], [5]. When decisions involve
movement, fruit flies, desert locusts, zebrafish, and mice
can delay spatial choices while integrating information about
competing alternatives [6]–[8]. Similarly, in aircraft collision
avoidance, uncertainty in an approaching aircraft’s state can
make it optimal to delay the advisory until the appropriate
maneuver, such as climbing or descending, becomes clearer
[9], [10]. Interestingly, in some robotics applications, delayed
choice is not just an emergent behavior, but might be
deliberately engineered to preserve reachability to multiple
candidate targets for as long as possible while maintaining
upper bounds on the cost of attaining each of them [11],
[12].
Several prior papers have studied the emergence of delay
as optimal behavior due to stochastic perturbations. Optimal
control formulations can lead to delayed decisions because
of the stochasticity in the dynamics of the controller itself
[13], [14], the dynamics of obstacles/adversaries [9], [10],
or the perceived dynamics of targets [15]. In contrast, we
show that the delay can be optimal even if the dynamics
is fully deterministic but the terminal reward is uncertain.
Specifically, we consider two alternative decision targets,
with one yielding a known reward and the other an uncertain
reward inferred from noisy observations. As shown in Fig. 1,
delayed reward attainment emerges as optimal behavior
when the alternatives are initially difficult to distinguish
and waiting is relatively inexpensive. This observation holds
both for linear bounded-control dynamics and nonlinear
opinion dynamics with controlled attention rate, suggesting
that delayed commitment is a general consequence of cost
trade-offs rather than a feature of a particular model.

ming is described in §II. In §III we focus on a subset
of problems, for which we can derive an a priori bound

A. Delayed Commitment Across Different Dynamics
We first examine whether delayed reward attainment per-
sists across distinct decision dynamics. We consider the
linear (14) and nonlinear (15) models under a constant-
variance observation model, σ2(z) = σ2, while allowing the
remaining model and numerical parameters to be selected
separately for each system. For both models, we consider
a finite sequence of three observations. Figure 2 shows
representative optimal trajectories for both models. At each
of the three observation times, the posterior variance Pj =
σ2
j
is common to all branches, while the labeled posterior
means µj represent different realizations of the belief update
caused by different observation values.
Following the second observation, the trajectories illustrate
how the optimal response depends on both the posterior mean
and the confidence in that estimate. When the posterior mean
strongly favors the upper alternative, as for µ2 = 1.90 in both
dynamics, the controller commits to the upper alternative and
terminates before the third observation. When the posterior
favors the lower alternative, as for µ2 = 0.85, the controller
instead begins moving toward the lower boundary while
postponing decisions until further information is received.
The center branch provides a particularly clear illustration
of the role of confidence: the posterior mean remains un-
changed at µ = 1.10 after the second observation, while the
posterior variance decreases. Despite the unchanged mean
belief, the optimal control changes as confidence in that
belief increases. Thus, the policy depends on the full belief
state (µ, P), rather than on the posterior mean alone. At
the same time, the controller remains uncommitted despite
having sufficient time to attain that reward before the next
observation, showing that this delay is an optimal response to
uncertainty rather than a consequence of limited reachability.
Following the third observation, the problem reduces to the
stationary tail problem, and the controller proceeds toward
the terminal favored by the updated belief. The branching
nevertheless illustrates how delayed reward attainment can
arise from remaining uncommitted, which preserves the
ability to redirect the motion when new information arrives.

The reference times are computed by pooling across sampled

For the linear model, tref is the largest sampled reward-

fraction of trajectories under the two-observation policy that

V. CONCLUSION
Choosing between uncertain options is hard even for
a fully rational decision maker. Motivated by embodied
decision making in biology and robotics, we focused on a
“simple” choice between two rewards, one of which has an
uncertain value that has to be estimated through a sequence
of noisy observations. Unlike in the standard sequential sam-
pling setting (e.g., [2]), our rewards cannot be claimed instan-
taneously and have to be attained by sufficiently changing the
decision maker’s internal state, which can also directly affect
the quality of observations. Balancing the costs of controlled
state evolution, information acquisition, and the risk of se-
lecting a suboptimal reward results in a nuanced optimization
problem exhibiting several types of strategic behavior. Using
dynamic programming, we demonstrated that it is often
optimal to delay the reward attainment, particularly when
the respective values of two rewards are initially hard to
distinguish and the cost of waiting for additional observations
is relatively low. We showed that this is generally true for
very different types of controlled dynamics and observation
models. For a subset of problems in this class, we have
also derived an a priori upper bound on a number of useful
observations. When observation quality is state-dependent,
we showed that it can be optimal to approach an uncertain
reward purely for information-gathering reasons, even if its
value is currently viewed as somewhat inferior. Our results
provide a scientific insight on why delays in decision making
under uncertainty may have evolved as ubiquitous features
of natural behavior. They also suggest an important design
principle for autonomous decision making across domains:
when information is uncertain and stakes are high, it pays
to hedge your bets. Future work will extend the presented
framework to multiple uncertain rewards, higher-dimensional
state spaces, stochastic controlled dynamics, and multiple
(cooperating or competing) decision makers.
