ABSTRACT
Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has proved to be a strong alternative to manual network design, but
applying it to 3D medical image segmentation is limited by two well-known costs, large annotation
budgets and multi-GPU clusters. Thus, this paper introduces Active-DiNTS (Active Differentiable
Network Topology Search), an approach that embeds pool-based Active Learning (AL) into a bi-level
differentiable topology search to perform architecture discovery and label curation jointly. At each
query round, unlabeled MRI volumes are ranked by one of three uncertainty signals (Entropy, Vari-
ance, or Standard Deviation), and only the top-ranked volumes are sent to an oracle for annotation.
The new labels feed two interlocked stages. Network weights are updated in an outer loop, while
the macro/micro topology of a U-Net-style backbone is refined in an inner loop. Three AL regimes
(weights-only, topology-only, joint) expose the speed–accuracy trade-off. Evaluations on the Medi-
cal Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) Task01 BrainTumour benchmark showed that Active-DiNTS
surpasses DiNTS, C2FNAS, and nnU-Net in Dice, with gains of about 10 percentage points on
Edema and 5 points on Non-Enhancing core, on a single GPU and using a fraction of the labeled
volumes. The discovered architectures are denser and more FLOP-heavy than prior baselines, but
remain competitive in trainable parameters and peak memory; the fastest search variant finishes in
under 0.25 GPU-days, over 27× faster than the eight-GPU DiNTS search. Together, these results
indicate that pairing differentiable NAS with active data acquisition is a practical recipe for accurate
3D segmentation under realistic constraints.

Introduction
Volumetric segmentation underpins much of modern radiology software. It is what allows clinicians to outline tumors,
plan radiotherapy on a per-organ basis, and follow lesions through time at voxel resolution [1]. The workhorse for
these tasks has been, for almost a decade, the U-Net family of convolutional encoder–decoder models [2, 3, 4]. Strong

as these models are, building a variant that suits a new dataset still relies on substantial manual tuning and domain
knowledge.
A line of work known as Neural Architecture Search (NAS) tries to remove that handcrafted step by treating the
network topology itself as something to be optimized [5, 6, 7]. Among NAS strategies, differentiable methods have
been particularly impactful. By recasting discrete operator choices as continuous parameters that can be trained with
gradient descent, they have brought search budgets down from thousands of GPU-hours to single-digit GPU-days [8].
In medical imaging, DiNTS [9] pushed this idea further by searching over multi-resolution, multi-path encoder–decoder
topologies, producing segmentation networks that match or beat well-tuned 3D U-Nets; a recent systematic review of
differentiable NAS for medical segmentation [10] confirms that this line of work remains an active research direction in
the area.
Yet a structural problem persists. The two implicit prerequisites of NAS, a fully annotated training corpus and a sizable
GPU pool, rarely line up with the reality of clinical research groups. Pixel-level expert labeling of 3D scans is laborious,
slow, and expensive, and the typical hospital research lab does not have a multi-node cluster on hand. That mismatch
raises a natural question: is it possible to search for a strong architecture while also keeping the number of labeled
volumes the search consumes as small as possible?
Active Learning (AL) is a good fit for that question. AL frameworks query annotations only for the samples that the
model is least confident about, and they have repeatedly demonstrated annotation savings across many supervised
settings [11, 12, 13, 14]. For voxel-level prediction tasks, uncertainty signals such as per-voxel entropy can be
aggregated into volume-level scores that drive the selection process, provided that some care is taken with outliers and
class imbalance [15, 16].
Progress in medical image segmentation over the last two years has unfolded along two complementary tracks. One track
scales model and data. Vision foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [17] and its medical
adaptations [18, 19], together with transformer-based 3D backbones [20, 21, 22], lean on large-scale pretraining and
substantial compute to obtain strong general-purpose representations. The other track scales efficiency and adaptability.
Dataset-driven systems such as nnU-Net [23, 24] configure themselves automatically to a given dataset without external
pretraining. Active-DiNTS lives squarely in the second track and pushes it further by jointly tackling two constraints,
scarce annotations and single-GPU search, that the first track does not, and was not designed to, address.
Our claim in this paper is that AL and NAS are mutually reinforcing rather than competing. AL feeds the search
with high-value supervision; the search, in turn, shapes the model that will produce the next round of uncertainty
estimates. Building on that observation, we propose Active-DiNTS, a differentiable topology search augmented with
uncertainty-driven pool-based AL, and we show empirically that the two components compound. On MSD Task01
BrainTumour, the resulting pipeline reaches state-of-the-art Dice with a single GPU and a small slice of the annotation
budget.
In summary, this study presents the following contributions:
• A one-shot differentiable topology search interleaved with pool-based AL for 3D medical segmentation.
Two coupled optimization loops, one for weights and one for topology, draw labeled samples from independent,
uncertainty-ranked pools, which makes it possible to discover architectures with very few annotations.
• A flexible AL engine that supports three update regimes (weights-only, topology-only, joint) and three
uncertainty heuristics (Entropy, Variance, Standard Deviation), enabling explicit control over the speed–
accuracy trade-off.
• New best Dice scores on MSD/Task01 BrainTumour relative to DiNTS, C2FNAS, nnU-Net, and other strong
baselines, achieved on a single GPU and with substantially less labeling effort.
• A resource-efficiency analysis showing that the discovered networks, despite being denser and heavier in
FLOPs, are competitive in trainable-parameter count and in peak GPU memory, and that the search itself is up
to 27× faster than the DiNTS baseline.

Background and Related Work
2.1
U-Net Architectures for Medical Segmentation
The U-Net design proposed by Ronneberger et al. [2] has effectively defined what a baseline medical segmentation
model looks like. Several extensions have followed: V-Net [3] lifted the idea to volumetric inputs by adding residual
links and a Dice-based objective, and UNet++ [4] reworked the skip connections to better fuse information across scales.
For brain tumor segmentation, Multi-Planar U-Net (also distributed as CerebriuDIKU) [25] sidesteps the memory cost

of full 3D convolutions by combining a single 2D U-Net with multi-planar test-time augmentations. nnU-Net [23] takes
yet another route, dispensing with manual tuning altogether. It automatically configures and ensembles a family of
U-Net variants according to dataset-specific properties (resolution, voxel spacing, class frequencies). These hand-crafted
or semi-automated solutions provide strong baselines but, by virtue of being topologically fixed, leave some accuracy
on the table. A recent comprehensive review by Azad et al. [26] surveys the broader U-Net ecosystem and shows how
influential this family has remained.
On the volumetric side, Çiçek et al. [27] introduced a 3D U-Net that learns from sparsely annotated slices, and the
Attention U-Net [28] showed that lightweight attention gates suffice to focus the decoder on regions of interest without
requiring a separate detector. Among semi-supervised approaches, UMCT (sometimes labeled NVDLMED) [29]
combines multi-view co-training with uncertainty-weighted label fusion, allowing the model to learn from both labeled
and unlabeled examples.
2.2
NAS for 3D Segmentation
NAS applied to 3D medical segmentation is a comparatively young area. SCNAS [30] explores encoder–decoder
structures via a stochastic continuous-relaxation scheme that scales to high-resolution input. C2FNAS [31] proceeds in
two passes, first choosing a macro topology and then refining cell-level operators by one-shot uniform sampling, to
reduce the discrepancy between the architecture found during search and the architecture actually deployed.
DiNTS [9] raised the bar by performing a fully differentiable search over multi-resolution, multi-path topologies.
Through a feature-node conversion trick and a topology-aware loss term, DiNTS reduces the so-called discretization
gap, the inconsistency that arises when continuous architecture probabilities are rounded into a discrete graph. Even so,
DiNTS shares two limitations with its NAS peers, both of which Active-DiNTS targets directly. The entire labeled
training set is assumed to be available, and the search is run on a multi-GPU node (eight V100s in DiNTS’s case).
Despite the recent shift of part of the community toward transformer-based backbones and foundation-model fine-tuning
(Sec. 2.4), differentiable NAS remains a practical option when neither large pretraining corpora nor multi-GPU resources
are available, as the contemporary review in [10] also documents.
2.3
Active Learning for Dense Prediction
Pool-based AL keeps a large unlabeled reservoir and at each round picks the most informative samples to be annotated,
according to an acquisition function [11, 12]. Standard heuristics include uncertainty sampling, query-by-committee,
and expected model change [15]; recent surveys give a thorough account of how these classical ideas extend to the
deep-learning setting [14], and Wang et al. [16] provide a dedicated treatment of deep AL in medical image analysis,
including the specific difficulties that dense prediction raises for acquisition functions designed with classification in
mind. For segmentation, uncertainty is typically computed per voxel, often as entropy of the predicted class distribution,
and then condensed into a single score per volume that drives the ranking.
The integration of AL with model training has been studied extensively, but its combination with architecture search
has received little attention. Active-DiNTS targets that gap: AL is wired directly into the bi-level optimization loop of a
differentiable NAS so that the architecture and the labeled pool evolve together toward higher segmentation quality.
2.4
Transformers and Foundation Models for Medical Segmentation
A second wave of progress in medical segmentation, parallel to NAS, has emerged from large-scale pretraining and
attention-based architectures. UNETR [20] and Swin UNETR [21] replace U-Net’s convolutional path with self-
attention blocks that capture long-range dependencies, and they benefit substantially from self-supervised pretraining
on large unlabeled medical corpora [22]. On the foundation-model side, the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [17] and
its successor SAM 2 [32], together with medical adaptations such as MedSAM [18] and Medical-SAM-Adapter [19],
transfer representations from web- or domain-scale pretraining to segmentation tasks via prompting or lightweight
adapters. A recent re-examination of nnU-Net [24] further argues that well-tuned U-Net variants remain difficult to beat
once experimental protocols are controlled rigorously, and contemporary surveys of efficient medical segmentation
in the era of Mamba and foundation models [33], together with meta-learning frameworks for medical imaging [34],
catalog the broader landscape of work that, like Active-DiNTS, targets data efficiency in the clinical setting. We see
these efforts as complementary to Active-DiNTS rather than competing. They trade larger pretraining or compute
budgets for stronger representations, while Active-DiNTS targets settings in which neither pretraining corpora nor
multi-GPU resources are available and the architecture must be discovered from scratch under a tight annotation
budget. The AL machinery introduced in this paper could, in principle, sit on top of a transformer or foundation-model
backbone; we discuss that combination in Sec. 6.

Methodology
3.1
Search Space and One-Shot Differentiable Topology
Active-DiNTS operates within the cell-based, multi-resolution search space inherited from DiNTS [9] and depicted in
Figure 1. The space is structured at two granularities:
• Macro level (topology). A directed graph with L=12 layers spans D=4 spatial resolutions. Adjacent
resolutions are connected through fully populated edges that carry a fixed catalog of operations, namely 2×
downsample, 2× upsample, 3×3×3 convolution, 1×1×1 convolution, 3×3×3 strided convolution, and direct
pass (skip), which together dictate how features flow from input to output.
• Micro level (cell). Every edge instantiates one of O=5 candidate operators: (0) skip, (1) full 3D 3×3×3
convolution, (2) pseudo-3D P3D 3×3×1, (3) P3D 3×1×3, or (4) P3D 1×3×3 [35]. With the exception of
skip, all cells precede and follow their convolution with ReLU activation and Instance Normalization.
In contrast to cell-repetition spaces such as DARTS [8] or Auto-DeepLab [36], this design lets each cell be searched
independently, so the resulting encoder–decoder configurations form a substantially richer family.
Continuous relaxation and discretization.
As in standard one-shot NAS, each discrete operator choice is relaxed
into a continuous probability vector and trained by gradient descent. When the search ends, the operator with the
highest probability on each edge is kept and the rest are pruned, yielding a deployable network. A known shortcoming
of this protocol is the discretization gap: edges whose probabilities are non-negligible but lie just below the cutoff
are dropped, perturbing the feature flow the search converged to. DiNTS attenuates this issue with a topology loss
that pushes high-probability connections into feasible paths, a mechanism that Active-DiNTS preserves. A RAM-cost
factor σ controls the density of the final graph; larger σ produces denser, more interconnected topologies at higher
GPU-memory cost. All experiments in this paper use σ=0.8, the most resource-heavy but most accurate setting.
3.2
Active Learning with Uncertainty-Driven Querying
The architecture search in Active-DiNTS runs inside a pool-based AL loop. Two distinct unlabeled pools are maintained
in parallel:
• Outer pool UW (weight updates). At every query round the current model produces segmentation masks
for all volumes in UW . An uncertainty heuristic scores each prediction; the NQ volumes with the highest
scores are dispatched to the oracle, a domain expert in deployment, or the ground-truth labels in our simulation.
Once annotated, these volumes migrate from UW into the labeled set LW , which feeds T epochs of weight
optimization.
• Inner pool UT (topology updates). After the weight step, the model predicts on UT . The single most
uncertain volume is labeled and added to LT , from which one mini-batch is drawn to update the topology
parameters under DiNTS’s bi-level optimization. Because UT is drained one volume per round while UW is
drained NQ at a time, UT is refilled from the original data whenever it empties before UW does.

Entropy registers the overall disorder of the predicted distribution and is therefore well suited to flag volumes on which
the model is undecided across classes. Variance and Standard Deviation, in contrast, capture how widely individual
voxel predictions deviate from their mean, which is useful when the model is over-confident on a dominant class but

a reasonable signal for surfacing atypical volumes but also exposes it to degenerate cases such as scans with severe
imaging artifacts, where uniformly high voxel-wise uncertainty carries little information about the tumor itself.
3.3
Bi-level Training with Coupled Pools
Figure 2 gives a high-level picture of the pipeline; Algorithm 1 renders it formally. Each outer iteration corresponds to a
single AL query round. Once UW is depleted, the continuous architecture is discretized into a deployable model. A key
point is that, because the AL loop has been training with steadily more informative data all along, the searched model
needs no separate retraining phase. The architecture delivered at the end of the search is already the final model. This
stands in contrast to DiNTS, which reinitializes and retrains the discretized architecture with double the filter count and
batch size.

The bi-level structure follows the conventions of differentiable NAS [8, 36, 9] in that weights and architecture parameters
are optimized on separate data splits. What is new here is that both splits are not fixed partitions of a fully labeled
dataset, but rather grow incrementally under the control of the AL loop. As the search progresses, the weight-update
step becomes heavier because LW accumulates samples, while the prediction-and-scoring step becomes lighter because
UW shrinks; the per-round cost is therefore approximately constant.
The topology update relies on the composite loss originally proposed in DiNTS: (i) a segmentation term combining Dice
and cross-entropy, (ii) a topology term that penalizes infeasible edges, (iii) an entropy regularizer that drives architecture
probabilities toward binary values, and (iv) a RAM-cost term that caps peak memory. Weights are optimized with SGD
at 0.9 momentum, 4×10−5 weight decay, and a cosine-annealing schedule, whereas topology parameters use Adam.
4
Experimental Setup
4.1
Dataset
Active-DiNTS is evaluated on MSD Task01 BrainTumour [37], the publicly released subset of the Medical Segmenta-
tion Decathlon that derives from the BraTS challenge [38]. Its training partition contains 484 multi-parametric MRI
volumes, each one bringing four modalities, namely Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), T1-weighted (T1w),
post-Gadolinium contrast T1-weighted (T1w-Gd), and T2-weighted (T2w), together with three tumor-subregion labels,
Edema, Enhancing tumor, and Non-Enhancing core. The cases come from patients with glioblastoma or lower-grade
glioma. Following He et al. [9], the 484 volumes are partitioned into 388 for training and 96 for testing under 5-fold
cross-validation. Within each training fold, half of the examples populate the weight-update pool UW and the other
half populate the topology-update pool UT . Task01 BrainTumour is used as the primary benchmark for three reasons:
(i) it is the most studied task in MSD and admits direct, like-for-like comparisons with every NAS baseline considered
here; (ii) its multi-modal MRI input and three-class subregion structure stress the topology search across heterogeneous
feature distributions; and (iii) the published DiNTS results on this task provide an especially strong reference point
against which to measure improvements.
4.2
Baselines
We compare Active-DiNTS against two families of methods. The hand-designed group covers CerebriuDIKU (Multi-
Planar U-Net) [25], nnU-Net [23], 3D U-Net [27], Attention U-Net [28], and NVDLMED (UMCT) [29]. The NAS

group covers SCNAS [30], C2FNAS [31], and DiNTS [9]. For DiNTS specifically we report two variants: DiNTS

4.3
Active-DiNTS Configurations
Each Active-DiNTS training run is parameterized by a query function ∈{Random, Entropy, Variance, Std}, an
Active Learning setup strategy ∈{weights-only, topology-only, joint}, and a per-query budget NQ ∈[2, 100].
The training epochs allocated to each query lie in [1, 1430], of which a warm-up may consume up to 50%. These ranges
were explored by grid search under the same 5-fold cross-validation used for evaluation, with the epoch and warm-up
bounds taken from the DiNTS setup [9]. The four MRI modalities map to the four input channels of the network. When
computing uncertainty, the query axis can be set to operate over modalities (Axis 0) or over slices (Axis 3); this controls
the dimension along which dispersion is measured. The Random query function plays the role of a lower-bound control
throughout the experiments.
4.4
Evaluation Metrics and Protocol
Segmentation accuracy is reported with the Dice–Sørensen Coefficient (DSC), DSC = 2|X ∩Y |/(|X| + |Y |), with
X and Y the predicted and ground-truth voxel sets, respectively. We report the DSC of each tumor subregion, DSC1
for Edema, DSC2 for Enhancing, and DSC3 for Non-Enhancing, together with the unweighted mean of the three.
Computational profiles are summarized by FLOPs (GigaFLOPs), trainable parameters (millions), peak GPU memory
during search (MB), number of GPUs, and wall-clock search time expressed in GPU-days.
4.5
Implementation Details
Active-DiNTS is implemented in Python on top of PyTorch [39], with MONAI [40] providing medical-imaging
preprocessing, data augmentation, and the Dice + cross-entropy loss. SciPy [41] supplies the numerical primitives
used by the uncertainty heuristics. All searches and evaluations are run on a single Linux workstation with an Intel
Xeon Silver 4215 CPU (2.50 GHz, 8 cores), 126 GB of RAM, and one NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU with 32 GB of
VRAM. Unlike DiNTS, which searches on one dataset and then reinitializes and retrains the resulting architecture with
double the capacity, Active-DiNTS deploys the searched model as-is, since the AL-guided search already produces a
model of sufficient quality. Multi-channel preprocessing and augmentations follow the standard BraTS/DiNTS protocol.
Architecture parameters are initialized from a Gaussian distribution; weight-update SGD uses 0.9 momentum and
4×10−5 weight decay with cosine annealing, while topology parameters use Adam. The RAM factor is held fixed at
σ=0.8. The complete implementation is publicly available.2

Results and Discussion
5.1
Learning Curves and Active Learning Dynamics
Figure 3 reports Dice as a function of the number of AL queries for the joint (weights & topology) configuration under
each uncertainty heuristic, with the Random baseline and the DiNTS reference plotted for context.
A few observations are worth highlighting. First, every Active-DiNTS variant surpasses the DiNTS baseline after
roughly 18 queries, a point at which fewer than 20% of the training volumes have been labeled. This indicates that
targeted sample selection reaches competitive accuracy considerably faster than training on a fixed, fully labeled corpus.
Second, uncertainty-driven heuristics outperform Random querying consistently; Entropy and Variance hold a slight
advantage in the early stage (queries 1–20), while Std edges ahead afterward. Third, Dice gains are concentrated in
the first 20 queries, where each newly annotated volume carries the most information relative to the still-small labeled
pool; beyond about 60% data usage, improvements are incremental. The occasional dip at individual queries suggests
that certain volumes briefly shift the training data distribution, and that the short training window between consecutive
queries is sometimes insufficient to absorb that shift, though recovery is fast. We did not run a controlled experiment
isolating which volume properties cause these dips, so this reading of them remains a hypothesis.

factorize the 3D kernel along different spatial axes, complement full 3D convolutions by providing anisotropic feature
extraction at a lower parameter cost. Skip connections appear comparatively less often in Active-DiNTS topologies,
which may reflect the denser connectivity already providing ample gradient pathways.

Computational resources.
Table 3 juxtaposes the resource profiles of all methods.
Active-DiNTS architectures are markedly denser than those of DiNTS and C2FNAS, which translates into noticeably
higher FLOPs (about 8200–9650 GFLOPs against 151–658 GFLOPs). This follows directly from running with
fully connected topologies at σ=0.8 and from the fact that the AL-driven search optimizes for predictive accuracy
rather than computational economy. That said, the trainable-parameter count stays in the same range, 127 to 154 M
for Active-DiNTS versus 152 M for DiNTS. Several intermediate Active-DiNTS configurations are actually lighter
(123–126 M).
Peak GPU memory is higher in absolute terms (14.7 to 17.3 GB on a single 32 GB V100), but it still represents only
46–54% of the available VRAM, whereas DiNTS occupies roughly 13 GB on each of eight 16 GB V100s, i.e. around
81% of each card. The total hardware bill is therefore drastically smaller for Active-DiNTS: one GPU instead of eight.
The most prominent efficiency gain shows up in search time. The fastest Active-DiNTS variant, Variance†, reaches the
accuracy of DiNTS (Pancreas) in just 0.21 GPU-days, more than 27× faster than the 5.8 GPU-days that DiNTS needs.
Even to overtake DiNTS (Brain), the Entropy‡ variant only needs 1.1 GPU-days, roughly a 5× speedup. Two factors
plausibly account for these savings. The AL loop trims the effective training set down to its most informative core, and
the absence of a separate retraining phase removes a major source of latency. We did not ablate these two contributions
separately, so their relative weight is not established here.
For scenarios in which inference-time FLOPs are the decisive cost, for example mobile or edge deployment, post-hoc
compression methods such as structured pruning and quantization could be applied to the dense Active-DiNTS models;
we did not evaluate how much accuracy such compression would cost. In the medical-imaging context, however,
lightweight inference is usually less critical than segmentation accuracy and efficient use of the limited annotation
budget, both of which Active-DiNTS optimizes for directly.

5.5
Ablation and Practical Notes
Uncertainty heuristic choice.
Entropy, Variance, and Std converge to nearly identical final Dice values, with all
pairwise differences under 0.5 points. We did not run significance tests across folds, so these differences should be
treated as indistinguishable rather than as a ranking. That robustness simplifies hyperparameter selection in practice,
since any of the three heuristics is a sensible default. In terms of memory overhead, Entropy was the cheapest of the
three in our runs, which is consistent with it acting directly on class-level probabilities whereas Variance and Std need
per-voxel deviations from the mean.
AL setup.
The joint configuration delivers the fastest convergence overall, but topology-only is a close runner-up and
can be preferable when the compute budget for weight updates is constrained. Weights-only stalls past the mid-process
mark, reinforcing the message that the topology has to be actively refined alongside the weights.
Outlier sensitivity.
Because Standard Deviation in particular responds to extreme values, volumes with severe
imaging artifacts can score as highly uncertain without being informative about tumor structure. In a deployment setting
this argues for screening such volumes before they consume oracle time. We did not run an ablation isolating the effect
of such a filter on the learning curves, so we report this as a design consideration rather than as a measured result.
Positioning versus transformer and foundation-model approaches.
Recent transformer-based 3D backbones [20,
21] and foundation-model adapters [18, 19] operate at a different point in the resource trade-off curve. They rely
on large-scale self-supervised pretraining or on prompting infrastructure at inference time. Active-DiNTS sits in the
orthogonal regime in which neither prerequisite is available. With no pretraining corpus, no oracle prompts, and only a
single GPU, it still has to discover a competitive architecture under a constrained annotation budget. Within that regime,
and among the published results we are aware of on MSD Task01 BrainTumour, these are the strongest figures we have

FLOP budget.
The FLOP gap between Active-DiNTS and NAS baselines deserves a final clarifying note. The
reported values reflect training and search-time FLOPs of the dense, fully connected topologies favored by the AL-
driven search at σ=0.8; they do not represent a hard constraint on deployment, since standard post-search compression
(structured pruning, low-rank factorization, quantization) can in principle recover a leaner inference graph from the
same set of weights. In our target setting, research and clinical workstations with constrained annotation budgets, the
dominant practical cost is annotation, not FLOPs, and it is precisely on that axis that Active-DiNTS delivers its largest
gains.
6
Conclusions and Future Work
We have presented Active-DiNTS, a framework that brings together differentiable network topology search and pool-
based Active Learning for 3D medical image segmentation. By using two interconnected and nested optimization loops,
one for network weights and one for the macro/micro topology, and feeding both from uncertainty-ranked labeled pools,
Active-DiNTS finds competitive U-Net-style architectures while requiring far fewer annotations and a much smaller
GPU footprint than mainstream NAS pipelines.

On MSD Task01 BrainTumour, Active-DiNTS exceeds the average Dice of DiNTS, C2FNAS, nnU-Net, and other
strong baselines, with the most pronounced gains on Edema (about 10 pp) and Non-Enhancing core (about 5 pp). It

prior work, and the removal of a separate retraining stage further compresses the pipeline.
Four caveats accompany our results. First, voxel-wise uncertainty heuristics are sensitive to class imbalance and can
be misled by outliers, which is a known weakness of this family of acquisition functions and one we did not quantify
here. Second, the dense topologies favored by the search translate into high training-time FLOPs that, while acceptable
during search, may need post-hoc compression for latency-sensitive deployment. Third, our evaluation concentrates on
a single 3D MRI benchmark; this choice was deliberate, in order to enable direct and controlled comparisons against
the DiNTS reference and prior NAS works that all report on the same task, but extending the analysis to additional
MSD tasks and to non-MRI modalities is a natural next step. Fourth, baseline numbers are taken from the original
publications rather than reproduced under our own pipeline, so the comparison carries whatever protocol differences
those works embed.
As future work, we will extend Active-DiNTS along four directions: (i) wider benchmarking, including additional MSD
tasks (Pancreas, Heart, Prostate) and 2D natural-image segmentation (COCO, PASCAL VOC) to probe cross-task and
cross-domain transfer; (ii) richer AL strategies, such as stream-based selection, query synthesis, or committee-based
disagreement, which may yield more sample-efficient querying; (iii) deployment-aware compression, via structured
pruning and quantization, to bring the inference cost of dense Active-DiNTS networks into line with resource-constrained
clinical hardware; and (iv) integration with foundation-model backbones [17, 32, 18, 20, 33], layering the bi-level AL
loop on top of pretrained transformer or SAM-style models to combine annotation efficiency with the representational
power of large-scale pretraining.
