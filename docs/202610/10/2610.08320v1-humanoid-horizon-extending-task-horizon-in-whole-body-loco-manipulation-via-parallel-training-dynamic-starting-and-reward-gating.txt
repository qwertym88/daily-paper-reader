Abstract
Cluttered indoor environments, where large and heavy objects are scattered across
diverse surfaces, require humanoid robots to sequentially navigate, grasp, transport,
and accurately place each item at its target location within a single uninterrupted
episode. This long-horizon, whole-body loco-manipulation task remains a signifi-
cant challenge for current methods. Previous approaches often suffer from two main
issues: easy-reward bias, where training overemphasizes early transport stages at
the expense of later ones, and catastrophic forgetting, where focusing on later stages
leads to a decline in earlier-stage performance. In this work, we introduce Hu-
manoid Horizon, a unified policy framework designed to overcome these limitations
through three interrelated mechanisms. The Parallel Training Strategy organizes N
scenes into S concurrent stage streams governed by a shared policy, ensuring all
transport stages receive continuous gradient updates and removing the bottleneck
of sequential optimization. The Dynamic Starting Mechanism updates each envi-
ronment’s initial state with terminal states from upstream rollouts, gradually broad-
ening transition coverage and enhancing robustness at stage boundaries. Reward
Gating sets the reward to zero for the rest of the episode in later-stage streams when
the immediately preceding object is displaced beyond a set threshold, so the shared
policy learns not to disturb a just-placed object and earlier placements are preserved
throughout the episode. Collectively, these strategies achieve per-stage success
rates exceeding 80% on the two-object LHM-Humanoid benchmark (350 training
scenes, 66 held-out scenes). As the number of sequentially transported objects
grows beyond two, success declines with the horizon, but the degradation is grace-
ful relative to the sharp drop seen in all baselines. Additionally, we demonstrate
that the RL teacher policy can be distilled into a Vision-Language-Action student
via DAgger. When provided only with egocentric RGB or depth observations and
natural language instructions, the student successfully replicates the teacher’s long-
horizon, multi-object behaviors, highlighting the potential for perception-driven
deployment on real humanoid robots. Videos and results are available at https:
//haozhuo-zhang.github.io/Humanoid-Horizon-project-page/.

Introduction
Humanoid agents that imitate human motion, perceive cluttered scenes, and manipulate objects are
a central yet difficult research target. Prior work has advanced locomotion and control [1, 2, 3, 4],
scene interaction [5, 6, 7, 8], and manipulation [9, 10, 11, 12]. A key gap remains: long-horizon
whole-body loco-manipulation, i.e., continuously transporting multiple large objects over long task
chains without environment resets.
This gap is fundamental. Compared with arms or quadrupeds, humanoids are less dynamically
stable and must coordinate balance, locomotion, and manipulation throughout long episodes. As
a result, long-horizon pipelines developed for more stable platforms are hard to transfer directly.
Despite progress in interface and perception conditioning [9, 13], most current methods still focus
on short-horizon single-object skills [14, 15, 16], often relying on fixed starts, and thus struggle
with continuous multi-object loco-manipulation. When these methods are extended to longer task
chains, two failure modes become prominent: easy-reward bias (over-optimizing early-stage transport
while under-exploring later-stage behaviors) and catastrophic forgetting. In our context, catastrophic
forgetting specifically means that a policy already capable of single-object transport is further trained
from post-first-object terminal states to transport the next object, yet after this shift it loses the ability to
re-execute first-object transport-and-release, even under substantial robot-initial-state randomization.
LHM-Humanoid [17] provides a concrete instance of this behavior (e.g., teacher model 2), which
then motivates dual-teacher distillation into a student and, in turn, constrains scalability to longer
multi-object horizons.
We present Humanoid Horizon, a framework for long-horizon whole-body humanoid loco-
manipulation that uses one policy to perform continuous long-horizon loco-manipulation across
multiple objects, without resets, auxiliary models, or handcrafted task-specific modules; it has three
coupled components: Parallel Training Strategy, which jointly optimizes all transport stages in one
policy to avoid easy-reward bias in sequential curricula and forgetting risks in decoupled stage-wise
training (including distillation-heavy pipelines such as LHM-Humanoid) [17], enabling scalable
learning as object count grows; Dynamic Starting Mechanism, which writes each stage terminal state
to the corresponding downstream environment buffer and initializes the next stage from that buffer
in the next epoch, so coverage of transition states expands through temporal accumulation across
epochs; and Reward Gating, which zeros the remaining reward in a later stage once the immediately
preceding object is displaced beyond a tolerance, so the policy learns not to disturb a just-placed
object and earlier placements are preserved over the episode.
2


We evaluate on the LHM-Humanoid benchmark [17]: 350 training/evaluation scenes and 66 unseen
scenes for generalization, and we follow LHM-Humanoid to test longer-horizon settings with more
than two sequentially transported objects. Combining these mechanisms yields per-stage success
above 80% in two-object sequential transport. As more objects are carried in one episode, success
declines with the horizon, but the degradation is graceful relative to the sharp drop seen in prior
baselines, and a single policy still completes multi-object rearrangement across diverse scenes. As
an exploratory deployment study, we distill the teacher policy into a vision-language-action (VLA)
student using DAgger [18], and with egocentric RGB or depth inputs plus language instructions, the
student preserves long-horizon multi-object behaviors learned by the teacher.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We present Humanoid Horizon, a single-policy framework for long-horizon whole-body
humanoid loco-manipulation that supports continuous multi-object rearrangement without
resets, auxiliary models, or handcrafted task modules.
• We introduce a Parallel Training Strategy that jointly optimizes all transport stages,
reducing easy-reward bias and catastrophic forgetting, and improving scalability to longer
task horizons.
• We propose a Dynamic Starting Mechanism and Reward Gating that together improve
inter-stage transition robustness and preserve previously completed placements throughout
long-horizon execution.
• We demonstrate strong long-horizon performance on 350 benchmark scenes plus 66 unseen
scenes, and show that DAgger-based VLA distillation transfers the learned behavior to
egocentric RGB/depth inputs.

RELATED WORK
2.1
Physically-Based Motion Synthesis and Human-Scene Interaction
Motion synthesis spans computer graphics, vision, and robotics, and is commonly divided into
kinematics-based methods[19, 20] and physics-based methods[2, 4, 1, 21]. Kinematic approaches
emphasize semantic coherence, while physics-based approaches learn dynamically feasible control
policies in simulation. Foundational systems such as AMP[1] and DeepMimic[21] have enabled
later extensions with adversarial priors, latent skill representations, and language conditioning. In
human-scene interaction, InterScene[8] and InterPhys[6] move beyond earlier inverse-kinematics
or hand-designed controllers toward dynamic object interaction, and RL-based methods show
strong manipulation skills[15, 14]. In parallel, embodied AI studies integrated navigation and
manipulation[22, 23], including commonsense-guided policies[24]. However, most systems still
use simplified embodiments (e.g., mobile bases with parallel grippers) and remain short-horizon
and single-object. HumanVLA[9] advances humanoid manipulation of heavier objects but remains
limited to single-object tasks with fixed initial states. LHM-Humanoid[17] is an important step
toward continuous multi-object vision-language loco-manipulation, yet its sequential training and
task-specific designs limit scalability: as horizons grow, catastrophic forgetting and easy-reward bias
become pronounced. Our method addresses these issues with a unified policy using Parallel Training
Strategy, Dynamic Starting Mechanism, and Reward Gating, enabling robust, scalable long-horizon
multi-object loco-manipulation without environment resets.
2.2
Long-Horizon Loco-Manipulation
Long-horizon execution has been studied in fixed-base arms [25, 26, 27], mobile manipula-
tion [28, 29, 30], and aerial manipulation, but remains difficult because long action chains accumulate
physical constraints [31, 32, 33, 34]. Typical strategies decompose tasks via subgoals [35, 36], world-
state prediction [33], trajectory prediction [32], or historical observations [37], yet often depend on
task-specific priors and generalize poorly. Progress is more limited in humanoids: most teleoperation
systems remain short-horizon and open-loop [38, 39, 40] because reliable real-time bipedal state
estimation is hard, and although legged odometry has advanced [41, 42, 43], transfer to humanoids
is still limited (CLONE [44] only partially mitigates drift with LiDAR odometry). Autonomous
long-horizon whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation is therefore still nascent, with representative
attempts including LessMimic [45] and LHM-Humanoid [17]. As horizons grow, these methods
3


still face the same two bottlenecks discussed above: catastrophic forgetting and easy-reward bias.
Motivated by this gap, Humanoid Horizon targets horizon scalability by maintaining stage-wise com-
petence while preserving previously completed placements as task length increases. Learning those
stage transitions also depends on where each stage begins. Reverse-curriculum learning [46] grows
the set of start states backward from the goal, and so needs the agent to be reset into hand-specified
near-goal configurations. Reset-free autonomous RL [47] avoids manual resets by training a separate
reset or backward policy, with its own reward. Curricula taken from expert demonstrations [48] start
the agent from demonstrated states. Dynamic Starting (Sec. 3.3) is aimed at the handoff between
stages instead: the next stage begins from the terminal state the preceding stage has just produced,
with no reset policy, demonstration source, or extra reward.
3
Method
This section presents the Humanoid Horizon training pipeline with a focus on the two central goals
introduced earlier: extending effective task horizon and mitigating forgetting in long sequential
whole-body manipulation. We begin with the task formulation, then present our Parallel Training
Strategy, which re-structures temporally sequential object transport into a unified optimization
problem that preserves earlier-stage competence while learning later stages. We next describe the
Dynamic Starting Mechanism, which improves stage-to-stage continuity by initializing each stage
from naturally occurring intermediate states rather than fixed resets. We then introduce the Reward
Gating mechanism to ensure that later-stage transport does not disrupt objects placed in earlier
stages. Finally, as an extension, we introduce our distillation pipeline that transfers the reinforcement
learning-trained teacher into a vision-language-action (VLA) student policy operating solely from
egocentric RGB/depth observations and natural language instructions.

3.2
Parallel Training Strategy
Prior long-horizon humanoid rearrangement methods—including end-to-end RL, curriculum RL, and
stage-isolated sequential training—are constrained by serial optimization, which tends to induce easy-
reward bias toward early transport stages or catastrophic forgetting when later stages are added. To
remove this bottleneck, we adopt a Parallel Training Strategy that jointly optimizes all transport stages
within one policy. We first pretrain on single-object transport to establish basic loco-manipulation
competence (navigate, grasp/lift, and carry/place from fixed starts), then switch to parallel training
over N scenes with S sequential objects each (Sec. 3.1). These scenes are organized into S stage
streams that share parameters θ. One pool of parallel environments is divided evenly across the
streams, and the baselines use the same pool (Appendix A.4).
For stream i, training starts from buffer Bi: B1 uses the original initial-state distribution, while
Bi (i > 1) approximates post-stage-(i−1) intermediate states. Initially, each environment buffer
in Bi (i > 1) is set to a simple proxy state (robot near object-(i−1) goal with default pose), and
these coarse starts are later replaced by rollout-derived states via the Dynamic Starting mechanism
(Sec. 3.3). Keeping all S streams active throughout training provides persistent gradients for every

3.3
Dynamic Starting Mechanism
While Parallel Training addresses easy-reward bias and catastrophic forgetting, it does not by itself
solve stage-to-stage transition robustness in long-horizon execution. As described in Sec. 3.2, each
stage-i stream (i > 1) starts from a fixed proxy buffer Bi; this is sufficient for bootstrapping
isolated transport skills, but it creates a distribution mismatch because fixed proxies represent only
a narrow, idealized subset of post-stage-(i−1) states. The mismatch worsens with horizon length:
even successful rollouts end in diverse handoff configurations (orientation, foot placement, residual
momentum, and goal-relative offset), so stage-(i+1) policies trained from fixed starts become brittle
when preceding stages terminate in unfamiliar states.
We therefore introduce Dynamic Starting, which progressively replaces proxy starts with rollout-
derived terminal states. At the end of each epoch, for each scene n, we overwrite the corresponding
downstream stage buffer (which stores one state per environment) with the stage-i terminal state:

3.5
Extension: Vision-Language-Action Policy Distillation
As an exploratory step toward real-world deployment, we distill the RL-trained teacher policy into
a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) student model using DAgger [18], which iteratively collects on-
policy student rollouts and queries the teacher for corrective supervision, mitigating the compounding
distribution shift that would otherwise arise from behavior cloning over long horizons. We train two
student variants: one conditioned on egocentric RGB images and one on egocentric depth maps, both
conditioned on per-stage natural language instructions encoded by a frozen BERT-base-uncased [49]
model and issued interactively by a human user: after each transport stage is completed, a new
instruction specifying the next object and its goal is provided (e.g., “carry the laptop to the desk”,
then after completion, “move the trash bin to the corner”). The robot must execute each instruction
online without access to any privileged state information. Despite operating under realistic perceptual
constraints, both VLA variants retain the long-horizon multi-object loco-manipulation capability
of the teacher, suggesting that the behaviors learned by Humanoid Horizon are transferable to
perception-driven settings and represent a meaningful step toward deployment on physical humanoid
platforms.
4
Experiments
4.1
Dataset and Evaluation Metrics
Our experiments use the dataset from LHM-Humanoid [17], spanning four room categories—
bedroom, living room, kitchen, and warehouse—with 350 diverse cluttered scene layouts and
corresponding long-horizon loco-manipulation episodes. The scenes include 79 object categories,
among which 25 are movable placement targets. In each episode, the agent must finish two consecu-
tive fetch-carry-place cycles within one uninterrupted rollout (i.e., without resets). For generalization
6


evaluation, we also use the 66 unseen tasks from LHM-Humanoid [17], which serve as an out-of-
distribution benchmark with novel layouts, objects, and instructions.
We evaluate performance using Succ1 and Succ2 (success rates for each transport cycle), SuccAll
(episodes where both cycles are completed), and Dist 1/Dist 2 (final object-to-goal distances in
meters). A cycle is considered successful if the object root lies within 0.2 m of its target location.
Better performance corresponds to higher success metrics and smaller distance errors.
4.2
Baseline clarifications
If parallel training is removed, our Humanoid Horizon effectively becomes Curriculum RL; in that
case, dynamic starting and reward gating are no longer well-defined because both mechanisms are
designed on top of the parallel-training framework. Hierarchical RL is implemented with five low-
level controllers and a privileged FSM for oracle stage transitions: walk-to-target, reach-and-grasp,
lift, carry with obstacle avoidance, and place-and-release. For HumanVLA [9], we decompose
the 350 long-horizon tasks into 700 short-horizon subtasks to match its original protocol, since
HumanVLA [9] is built for single-object transport; reward design and training procedures are kept the
same, and a privileged FSM is still used at test time to transition between consecutive transport stages.
InterMimic [15] is run according to its standard pipeline, i.e., AMP-motion imitation followed by
policy rollout evaluation. TokenHSI [16] uses the TokenHSI [16] architecture while being trained on
the same five skills as Hierarchical RL. The LHM-Humanoid [17] baseline is likewise trained strictly
under its original pipeline, and only its end-to-end student model is used for comparison. Except for
the VLA table, all compared methods use identical observation and action interfaces, so differences
primarily come from the learning strategies rather than mismatched I/O settings.

partial egocentric vision, but weaker teacher policies suffer larger drops, while Humanoid Horizon
preserves a substantially larger fraction of its original performance. Our RGB and depth variants
outperform all distilled baselines on most metrics, showing that the long-horizon behaviors learned
through parallel training, dynamic starting, and reward gating transfer effectively to the egocentric
VLA setting.

5
Failure Analysis
Humanoid Horizon exhibits two recurring failure modes. First, the Succ1-to-Succ2 gap is larger
than later per-stage drops. The reason is an inherent task property: the first cycle starts from a fixed
canonical pose on open floor, while later cycles begin from arbitrary post-task body configurations in
clutter where surrounding objects become nearby obstacles, making reorientation and obstacle-aware
navigation more difficult.
Second, frozen-action failures occur when the humanoid reaches the target but fails to complete a
stable grasp. A likely contributor is uniform scene allocation during training: harder clutter layouts
and object configurations require more exploration, so equal per-scene training budget can leave
difficult grasp cases under-optimized. These challenges are not unique to our method and are also
reflected in baseline degradation under longer horizons.
6
Conclusion and Future Work
We presented Humanoid Horizon, a unified framework for stable and scalable long-horizon whole-
body humanoid loco-manipulation built on three tightly coupled mechanisms: Parallel Training,
which maintains simultaneous optimization pressure across stages to mitigate easy-reward bias and
catastrophic forgetting; Dynamic Starting, which trains downstream stages from realistic handoff-state
distributions rather than fixed proxies; and Reward Gating, which preserves previously completed
placements during later-stage transport. Across both in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation,
this design delivers state-of-the-art performance on seen and unseen tasks, with strong gains over prior
baselines on per-stage success and full-episode completion. Beyond the two-object setting, the same
policy extends to five-object sequential transport without any change of architecture. Success declines
9


as the horizon lengthens, but the degradation is graceful relative to the sharp drop of competing
methods.
For future work, we plan to address the remaining failure modes (the Succ1-to-Succ2 navigation gap
and frozen-action grasping failures) through improved path planning and difficulty-aware training, and
to scale beyond five-object horizons toward real-world deployment on physical humanoid platforms.

Teacher (RL) observation.

(BPS) representation b ∈R200×3 encodes the object point cloud from 200 basis points. The BPS is
processed by a dedicated MLP [600 →512 →512 →128] with ReLU activations. All components
of opriv and the BPS features are individually normalized via running mean and standard deviation.

The student is trained by DAgger to reproduce the teacher’s actions

Action space.
All policies output a ∈R28, corresponding to normalized actions for the 28

A.3
Network Architectures
Teacher (AMP) network.
The teacher uses a shared-backbone architecture with separate actor,
critic, and discriminator heads:
• BPS encoder: MLP [600 →512 →512 →128] with ReLU.
13


• Actor: MLP [obs_dim →1024 →1024 →512 →28] with ReLU, where obs_dim =
dim(opriv) + 128.
• Critic: MLP [obs_dim →1024 →1024 →512 →1] with ReLU.
• Discriminator: MLP [1310 →1024 →1024 →512 →1] with ReLU; the final layer has
no bias and uses Uniform(−1, 1) weight initialization.
• Action distribution: Gaussian N(µ, σ2) with σ as a learnable parameter (not conditioned
on observation), initialized to exp(−2.9) ≈0.055.
VLA student network.
The VLA student network comprises:
• Image backbone: EfficientNet-B0 (classifier head removed), producing a 1280-dimensional
feature vector. Images are resized to 256 × 256 and normalized with mean and std of 0.5.
• Image pre-MLP: [1280 →128].
• Text pre-MLP: [768 →512 →128], where 768 is the hidden dimension of the frozen
BERT-base-uncased encoder.
• Actor: [dim(obody) + 128 + 128 + 28 →1024 →512 →28] with residual blocks, from
body proprioception, the image embedding, the language embedding, and the previous
action.
• The student outputs deterministic actions (no stochastic sampling).

All experiments are conducted in NVIDIA IsaacGym with GPU-accelerated PhysX on 2× NVIDIA

Termination Conditions
Episodes terminate under two conditions:
1. Fall detection: A non-ignored body part (excluding hands, lower arms, and feet) has contact
force > 0.1 N and height < 0.15 m simultaneously.
2. Maximum episode length: 300 steps for single-object, 600 steps for two-object episodes.

Two systematic differences exist between the training-time metrics reported here and the evaluation
metrics in Table 1 of the main paper. First, the training-time SuccAll (87.6%) is higher than the
evaluation SuccAll (80.4%). This is because Parallel Training flattens all S transport stages into
S × N independent concurrent streams; the logged SuccAll is therefore the average of per-stage
success rates computed independently, rather than the product of sequential successes within a
single uninterrupted episode. In evaluation, stages are executed sequentially—errors in earlier stages
propagate and compound into later ones, naturally yielding a lower SuccAll. Second, the training-
time Succ1 (92.5%) is lower than the evaluation Succ1 (96.6%). This stems from a deliberately
tighter success threshold used during training: as described in Appendix A.9, the training-time
success criterion requires the object to stay within 0.5 × ϵeval = 0.1 m, whereas evaluation counts
a placement as successful if the object is within ϵeval = 0.2 m. This stricter training threshold
encourages more precise object placement, which in turn improves evaluation robustness, but it also
causes training-time per-stage success to be conservatively lower than the evaluation counterpart.

D
Broader Impact
This work advances autonomous humanoid manipulation in simulation, with potential positive
impacts including assistive robotics (e.g., tidying rooms for elderly or mobility-impaired users) and
warehouse automation. Potential risks include the dual-use concern that humanoid agents capable
of autonomous object manipulation in cluttered environments could be repurposed for unintended
applications. We mitigate this by releasing only the simulation-based framework and trained policies;
real-world deployment would require additional safety mechanisms (e.g., force limits, human-in-the-
loop supervision). The method does not use personal data, human subjects, or crowd-sourcing.
