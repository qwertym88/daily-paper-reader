Abstract— Humanoid loco-manipulation of large, heavy ob-
jects demands forceful interaction across the entire body.
However, such payloads shift a humanoid’s center of mass
and impose sustained loads across the upper body, challeng-
ing balance and tracking. We present HULK, a whole-body
control framework for forceful loco-manipulation. Using model
predictive control (MPC) to guide reinforcement learning with
predictions of the loaded dynamics, we train two teachers:
one tracks arm motions under wrist forces, and the other
locomotes while holding large objects against the body. A
capture-point control barrier function augments the wrist-force
teacher during training to improve balance under load. We
distill both teachers into a single policy. We evaluate our method
on a Unitree G1 in both simulation and the real-world. In
simulation, the teacher with the barrier function achieves the
lowest forward and lateral velocity tracking errors at 10 kg
per arm among evaluated controllers and reduces aggregate
balance metric by 35.7% relative to MPC-guided reinforcement
learning alone. Our wrist-force teacher withstands torso push
disturbances of up to 130 N.

I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots are a compelling form factor for a
world built around the human body. The promise is a single
platform that can perform the same physical labor as people,
in the same unmodified spaces. Realizing this promise,
however, requires forceful whole-body work: lifting a loaded
tote, leaning into a cart, or carrying a heavy object against the
chest while walking. These tasks recur throughout logistics,
manufacturing, and assistive applications. What these tasks
share is a load the robot must sustain rather than reject:
it persists for the task’s duration, shifts where the robot’s
weight sits, and competes for actuation that keeps it balanced.
Current approaches to humanoid loco-manipulation have
converged on a layered control strategy. At the bottom sits a
learned whole-body controller (WBC): a policy that turns a
compact set of task-space commands, such as base velocity,
torso pose, and arm targets, into joint actions that keep the
robot balanced. Above it, planners, teleoperation interfaces,
and agentic pipelines issue those commands without reason-
ing about balance themselves, treating the WBC as a low-
level “System 0” [1], [2]. Massively parallel reinforcement
learning (RL) in simulation has made this bottom layer

Forceful interaction, however, remains comparatively un-
derexplored. Unlike agility and perception, it requires the
WBC to adapt its balance strategy to large and sustained
external wrenches. A heavy payload changes the forces and
moments that must be supported by the robot, with their
effect varying continuously with the arm configuration and
the object’s pose relative to the body. Existing approaches
address parts of this problem. Compliance-based methods
lower the effective stiffness of the robot so that it yields
under unexpected contact [14]; while effective for incidental
interaction, this behavior is poorly suited to supporting a
heavy payload against gravity. The strongest forceful con-
trollers instead learn to resist applied forces, but only at the
wrists [15], or only for horizontal push–pull moments [16],
[17]. Carrying a load further requires this adaptation to
persist: the disturbance is not a transient to be rejected,
but a condition that remains throughout the task. The robot
must therefore maintain balance under a continuously applied
wrench while simultaneously producing the forces required
for manipulation.
We address the displaced center of mass and sustained
balance under load in a single learned policy. Model predic-
tive control (MPC) supplies reward guidance during RL [18],
giving the policy a model-based signal for how a load moves
the centroidal state rather than requiring it to discover that
relationship from reward alone. We then augment the wrist-
force teacher with a capture-point control barrier function
(CBF) [19], [20]. Following CBF-RL [21], the barrier acts
during training to improve learned balance recovery under
load while retaining network-only inference. Our contribu-
tions are:
1) We extend learned whole-body control to forceful in-
teractions between external objects and contacts across
the humanoid upper body.
2) We use MPC reward guidance to train robust locomo-
tion and loco-manipulation under sustained payloads.
3) We augment the wrist-force teacher with training-time
capture-point constraints, improving loaded tracking
and balance recovery while retaining network-only in-
ference.
II. RELATED WORKS
Whole-body Control. Whole-body controllers have lately
been trained using reinforcement learning to follow high-
level commands while actively stabilizing the humanoid.
Several works [3], [4], [5], [6], [8] demonstrate the capabili-
ties of sim-to-real reinforcement learning for motion tracking
or teleoperation of the humanoid for human-like behavior
on hardware. Large-scale models now expose whole-body
control as a general-purpose interface: SONIC [7] supersizes
motion tracking toward natural whole-body control using a
large human motion tracking dataset. These methods address
kinematic tracking rather than forces and persistent loading.
Forceful Whole-body Control. Two efforts define the force-
ful direction. FALCON [15] rejects force perturbations at
the wrists with a wrist-force curriculum and a dual-network
architecture, but struggles to hold commands while doing
so. Thor [16] leans the torso to recruit the robot’s own
weight, using ZMP analysis of sagittal-plane wrist forces,
and so addresses horizontal push–pull rather than downward
payload forces. Neither models whole-body object interac-
tion during training, so the payload-augmented center of
mass degrades locomotion under heavy load. HAFO [17] and

Outside the learned-WBC setting, payload carrying has
been addressed by hierarchical optimization [25] and by
distilling trajectory optimization into skills [26], both in-

Dao et al. [27] show sim-to-real box loco-manipulation with-
out a whole-body command interface. AMO [28] provides
such an interface but assumes both feet remain in contact.
Closest to our approach, Li et al. [18] use model predictive
control for reward guidance and demonstrates cart-pushing
and payload carrying. However, this method treats interac-
tions as forces applied at the wrists rather than incorporating
a payload wrench into reference generation, and it does
not address contact against the torso. We extend it to a
whole-body controller accepting height, torso-tilt and arm
commands, with payloads entering the model through force
perturbations and whole-body grasp force profiles.
Balance Constraints in Learned Control. Control barrier
functions are ordinarily enforced by a runtime quadratic pro-
gram that minimally corrects a nominal controller [19], [20],
[29], which for a learned policy means shipping a reduced-
order model, gradient and solver alongside the network.
CBF-RL [21] instead applies the correction during training
and penalizes the residual in the reward, so the requirement is
internalized and deployment carries no extra machinery. We
adopt that formulation. Because a sustained external wrench
displaces the center of mass for a whole episode, the quantity
to constrain is a balance state under load, for which the
capture point model [30], is the natural choice.
III. METHODOLOGY
Our goal is to learn a single whole-body controller for
payload-driven loco-manipulation: a policy that follows pla-
nar velocity, base height, torso orientation and arm joint
commands while a load acts anywhere on the upper body,
and that transfers to hardware from proprioception alone. We
train two specialist teachers separately, one that tracks arm
poses under forces applied at the wrists and one that loco-
motes while maintaining whole-body grasps across multiple
objects. We distill the two into the single student we deploy
(Sec. III-G, Fig. 2). The teachers both share a reward whose
force-aware terms are supplied by a predictive model of the
loaded robot rather than hand-designed (Sec. III-B), using a
centroidal model as the predictor (Sec. III-C) together with
the references that inform it of the load (Sec. III-D), and
balance imposed by a barrier function during training instead
of a runtime filter (Sec. III-F). The two differ only in the
interaction each is exposed to and how it enters the model,
which we make concrete once per teacher in Sec. III-E.

E. Teacher Training
Wrist-force teacher. We train a controller that braces and
resists against forces applied at the wrists while tracking an
upper-body pose command, using a force curriculum akin
to [15]. The arm command is drawn from the BONES-RP1
dataset [33] retargeted to the 29-DoF robot: we keep the 14
arm joint angles of every retargeted frame, roughly 4.8×104
frames over 252 motions, and sample individual frames as
static pose targets rather than replaying a trajectory, so the
teacher has to hold arbitrary arm configurations under load
instead of one scripted motion. Independent 3D forces are
applied at the two wrists, up to ±40 N horizontally and −50
to +5 N vertically, each axis zeroed with probability 0.25,
held for 3 to 5 s, and ramped by a per-environment magnitude
curriculum. The wrists enter the model (6) as two point
interactions alongside the feet, placed by forward kinematics
along the arm chain evaluated at the commanded arm target
and gated on only while a force is live, so uk ∈R18 collects
two foot wrenches and two wrist forces.
Whole-body-grasp teacher. The teacher learns to loco-
mote and stay stable while maintaining whole-body grasps
across multiple objects. The interactions are the ground
reaction forces and the contact forces of each environment’s
assigned grasp. We generate 130,000 grasps offline across
multiple object geometries using energy-based optimization
from [34] (examples in Fig. 6). Each environment is initial-
ized with an object and a compatible grasp to maintain, so the
contact locations of (6) are read from that grasp and gated on
for as long as the assigned object is held. The grasp contact
forces are decision variables subject to the same friction
constraints as the ground-reaction forces. The forces exerted
by the object in the sampled whole-body grasp provide
uref
k , while the MPC optimizes the corresponding interaction
forces. These forces also contribute to the centroidal angular-
momentum dynamics in Eq. (6). All else in Secs. III-B to III-
D is shared between the two teachers. During training, we
warm-start from the wrist-force teacher so that locomotion
and upper-body command tracking are not relearned from
scratch.

G. Student Distillation
The teachers specialize in different interaction regimes.
We freeze both and train a mode-conditioned student with
DAgger [35]. At each student-visited state, the teacher se-
lected by mt ∈{0, 1} supplies the target action. The student
regresses these actions using the same observation space O
as the teacher actors, keeping supervision on its own rollout
distribution.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate whether HULK can maintain commanded
motion under sustained load, recover from disturbances, and
transfer these capabilities to forceful loco-manipulation tasks.

B. Simulation Results
Sustained load and recovery quality. The CP-CBF
teacher combines lower loaded translational errors with
smaller DCM excursions (Tab. I). Relative to Ours, the
largest tracking improvement is lateral, suggesting that the
benefit depends on the commanded direction. Because the
load acts at the forearms rather than the training wrists, these
results also show transfer across force application points.
AMO retains the lowest mean turn-rate error, so the advan-
tage does not extend uniformly across commands. The two
HULK teachers achieve similar push-survival performance,
but differ substantially in DCM excursion. The smaller DCM
excursions of the CP-CBF teacher suggest improved recovery
quality that push survival alone may hide. The smaller DCM
excursions from our CP-CBF teacher suggest a difference in
recovery quality that push survival may hide. This is consis-
tent with the balance state targeted during training, although
negative margins show that recovery involves leaving the
support envelope.
Arm accuracy and locomotion under wrist forces. Both
HULK teachers perform competitively against FALCON in
arm and composite root tracking at the strongest tested wrist

C. Real-world Results
HULK achieves the highest observed completion rate and
maximum completed load on three of the four hardware
tasks, and ties AMO on payload carry (Tab. III). In cart
push/pull, HULK completes 5/6 trials at the common load
versus 3/6 for the best baseline, FALCON, and pulls up to
300 kg compared with 200 kg in the separate load sweep.
In payload carry, all controllers can physically carry the
load, but only HULK and AMO consistently remain within
the prescribed path-deviation threshold, both achieving 4/4
successful trials at the common load. HULK and AMO also
both reach 12.25 kg in the separate load sweep.
The largest completion-rate gains arise when objects are
supported against the torso. HULK completes 4/4 trials with
both the fridge in front of the torso and the canister at
its side. AMO completes 2/4 fridge trials, whereas Thor
and FALCON complete none; all baselines complete 0/4
canister trials. HULK’s maximum completed loads are 7.0 kg
for the fridge and 6.1 kg for the canister, compared with
baseline maxima of 6.0 and 2.6 kg, respectively. Together,
these results show that the distilled policy combines cart
and payload capabilities with successful whole-body carrying
across the two tested load geometries.
V. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
Load
geometry
helps
explain
why
forceful
loco-
manipulation requires whole-body coordination. A force’s
point of application determines its moment about the CoM,
coupling arm configuration to the support needed to sustain
the load (Eq. (6)). Force-aware MPC exposes this coupling
in the training objective. Transfer from wrist forces to fore-
arm loading suggests that learned compensation can extend
beyond the trained contact location, although it does not
establish generalization to arbitrary contact arrangements.
MPC and CP-CBF provide complementary guidance:
MPC supplies references consistent with loaded dynamics,
while the barrier shapes how the policy approaches a balance
boundary. The observed tradeoff between root tracking and
recovery (Tabs. I and II) suggests that carrying may benefit
from allowing motion errors to preserve balance. The ex-
ported policy is unfiltered, so the barrier provides no runtime
safety guarantee.
The model and training regime limit the behaviors this
guidance can support. The centroidal predictor approximates
articulated dynamics without explicitly resolving joint-level
actuation feasibility, so a balanced centroidal plan need not
be executable by every joint. The wrist-force teacher samples
static arm poses, and the grasp teacher starts from established
grasps generated across multiple objects. Coverage of stable
configurations does not establish competence during transi-
tions between them. Grasp acquisition, regrasping, and rapid
contact changes therefore remain outside the demonstrated
scope. Richer dynamics models and training on contact tran-
sitions are directions for extending to dynamic manipulation.

Initialization and commanded grasp. We initialize from
a locomotion and static-arm checkpoint, not a grasp-force-
trained policy. In the initialization study, only actor and
critic network weights transfer; optimizer and curriculum
state start afresh. The grasp bank spans multiple object
