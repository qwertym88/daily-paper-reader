Arithmetic Actor Heads and Training Stabilization for
Out-of-Distribution Reinforcement Learning
Yifan Zhang∗
Liang Zheng†
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) policies can deteriorate under out-of-distribution (OOD) magni-
tude shifts. Starting from soft actor-critic (SAC) and its Bayesian Amnesic Piecewise-Robust
(BAPR) predecessor, we study the causal-symbolic BAPR (CS-BAPR) family. The practical
method combines six training-stabilization settings with alternative actor heads: a Neural Ad-
dition Unit (NAU) with a Neural Multiplication Unit (NMU)-inspired quadratic correction, a
Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN), or a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with rectified linear unit
(ReLU) or hyperbolic-tangent activations.
For a differentiable deterministic policy map π and an action-valued reference πref, we prove
the conditional bound ∥π(xood) −πref(xood)∥≤δ + ε∥d∥+ Lerr
2 ∥d∥2. Here xood is a test state,
d = xood −x0 is displacement from a training reference state x0, δ and ε bound the value and
derivative errors at x0, and Lerr bounds derivative-error growth along the connecting segment.
An isolated NAU is linear and has zero derivative curvature, whereas scalar ReLU has a derivative
jump. These layer facts do not certify the historical actor, which contains nonlinear features, a
quadratic branch, and action squashing. A revised smooth-feature variant admits an explicit
whole-policy derivative bound. A separate frozen-operator result preserves BAPR’s discounted
Bellman contraction.
On a 12-line transit benchmark calibrated with Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO),
training uses nominal in-distribution (ID) demand. All four stabilized variants improve reported
median returns over the unstabilized BAPR implementation under static, abrupt, and oscillating
demand shifts up to 50× nominal flow. Stabilization accounts for most of the gain; the NAU
variant is best at the static 5× point, while other heads perform better in several remaining
regimes.
Optional sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) and invariant risk
minimization (IRM)-inspired components are described separately. Auxiliary linear-quadratic
regulator (LQR) and Hopper experiments show task-dependent behavior. The evidence supports
a transit-focused training recipe and a conditional policy-output analysis.
Keywords: OOD extrapolation; arithmetic actor heads; training stabilization; transit holding;
robust RL.
1
Introduction
Policies trained under nominal conditions may perform poorly when passenger demand or other
exogenous inputs increase beyond the training range. We call this setting magnitude extrapolation.
SAC provides the underlying continuous-control training framework [1]; BAPR supplies change-
sensitive adaptation [2], and robust ensemble SAC (RE-SAC) supplies the critic ensemble and risk
regularization [3].
Magnitude extrapolation connects two questions: whether training produces a stable controller,
and whether the resulting policy responds appropriately to larger inputs. Arithmetic layers offer a
useful inductive bias for numerical extrapolation [4, 5], but a linear output layer alone does not make
a nonlinear policy linear in the original state. We therefore distinguish the layer-level calculation,
the conditional whole-policy error bound, and the observed control returns.

CS-BAPR combines four base stabilization settings with two recipes ported from BAPR: minimum-

The contributions are:
• A transit-focused comparison of four actor-head choices under the same stabilization recipe,
against an unstabilized and a legacy BAPR implementation.
• A policy-output extrapolation bound with explicit boundary errors and derivative-growth
assumptions (Theorem 5.25), together with layer-level NAU, quadratic-unit, and ReLU calcula-
tions (Section 5.2).
• A frozen Bellman-operator contraction result (Theorem 5.17) and a counterexample showing

• Conditional symbolic-reference results that state the required action-space alignment and

2
Related work
2.1
Generalization and robust RL
Simulation-trained locomotion can generalize to previously unseen terrain [7]. Robust Markov decision
processes (MDPs) instead optimize performance over specified transition uncertainty sets [8, 9]. Recent
robust actor-critic and fitted-value methods extend such analyses to function approximation and
offline or hybrid data [10, 11]. Their guarantees depend on their uncertainty and coverage assumptions;
they are not generally restricted to interpolation in a convex hull of training environments. Epistemic
partially observable MDPs explain why uncertainty about test conditions can require adaptive
behavior even when each individual environment is fully observed [12]. Our bound concerns the
output of a fixed deterministic policy map along a state-space segment.
2.2
Arithmetic networks and equation learning
Neural Arithmetic Logic Unit (NALU) networks introduce gated arithmetic computations for numer-
ical extrapolation [4]. NAUs and NMUs separate addition and multiplication and use constrained
weights with sparsity regularization [5]. The NAU is an addition/subtraction unit; the paper title
Neural Arithmetic Units denotes the broader family. Equation-learning networks recover analytic
relations for extrapolation and control [13]; this is a distinct architecture rather than an application
of NAUs. KANs use learnable spline functions on edges [14]. We compare these architectural ideas
in one training framework without claiming priority for applying arithmetic structure to control.
2.3
Symbolic dynamics and invariance
SINDy identifies sparse governing dynamics from a candidate library [15]; SINDy-RL combines
interpretable dynamics, reward, and policy models with RL [16]. AI Feynman exploits structural
properties of equations for symbolic regression [17]. These works motivate equation identification but
do not imply that a dynamics Jacobian equals an optimal-policy Jacobian.
IRM seeks representations whose optimal predictor is shared across training environments [18].
Causal representation learning and causal RL involve additional assumptions about interventions,
latent variables, and causal structure [19, 20]; structural causal models (SCMs) provide a separate
formal account [21]. Our optional selector is inspired by cross-environment invariance and uses
2


prediction-error variance. Its mathematical results assume the stated derivative accuracy; they do
not establish causal discovery from the selector alone.
2.4
Derivative regularity and ensemble stabilization
Rowan analyzes ReLU failures in physics-informed objectives, including mis-specified automatic-
differentiation gradients of discontinuous fields [22]. That result concerns physics-informed training,
rather than a general inability of ReLU networks to approximate smooth functions or extrapolate.
Here we use the elementary derivative jump at the scalar ReLU kink to delimit a smooth-derivative
certificate. Ensemble pessimism also appears in offline RL [23]; this motivates the minimum-ensemble
comparison but does not transfer an offline performance guarantee to our online controller. Our
Bellman analysis follows the frozen-parameter template of RE-SAC and BAPR [3, 2].
3
Scope and applicability
The primary empirical setting is transit holding under changes in passenger-demand magnitude.
The six stabilization settings and the actor-head choice are evaluated jointly, with symbolic losses
disabled. A demand multiplier changes the distribution of visited states; it is not itself the state-space
displacement appearing in the theorem.
The optional symbolic extension requires an action-valued reference constructed from an identified
model or other control information. A sparse dynamics model alone is insufficient: its state-evolution
output has different physical units and generally a different dimension from the policy action.
Tracking and counteraction tasks can both admit model-based feedback, but each requires an explicit
dynamics-to-control construction. The transit results do not validate such a construction.
Cross-environment filtering additionally requires environments that reveal the invariant relation
of interest. The configurable three-environment default is an implementation choice, not a universal
identifiability threshold from IRM. The empirical and theoretical limits are collected in Section 7.1.

Preliminaries
Notation.
Let E = Rdx and F = Rda be the ambient state and action spaces, where dx and da are
their dimensions. The admissible spaces are S ⊆E and A ⊆F. We use x for a state, a for an action,
t for environment time, and j for a training update. The stochastic actor has density pθ(a | x), where
θ denotes actor parameters. Its pre-squash Gaussian mean is µθ(x); π(x) denotes the deterministic
action map after squashing and scaling. Thus pθ, µθ, and π name different objects.
The training domain is Dtrain; Ω⊆E is the convex analysis domain containing the segment of
interest. πref : Ω→F is an action-valued reference. Write d = xood −x0 for displacement from a
fixed x0 ∈Dtrain to an OOD state xood. D denotes the Fréchet derivative; vector norms are Euclidean
and derivative norms are induced operator norms unless a Frobenius norm ∥· ∥F is specified. ∥Q∥∞
denotes the supremum norm of a state-action value function.
The nonnegative constants δ and ε bound reference-value and derivative error at x0. Lπ and M
bound the derivative Lipschitz constants of π and πref, and Lerr = Lπ + M bounds their derivative-
error growth. A subscript identifies the object whose regularity is bounded; the constant for a generic
head H is LH. The operators E and Var denote expectation and variance.

5.1
The extrapolation comparison
We compare π with πref on the segment joining x0 and xood. In the default analysis, the reference is
the affine tangent
πref(x) = π(x0) + Dπ(x0)(x −x0).
(3)
The tangent reference (3) gives δ = ε = M = 0 at x0. The resulting bound measures deviation from
the policy’s own tangent; closeness to an optimal policy requires a separate reference.
Assumption 5.1 (Reference and boundary accuracy). The policy and reference are continuously dif-
ferentiable on a neighborhood of the segment, with ∥π(x0)−πref(x0)∥≤δ and ∥Dπ(x0)−Dπref(x0)∥≤
ε.
Assumption 5.2 (Reference derivative regularity). For x, y ∈Ω, ∥Dπref(x)−Dπref(y)∥≤M∥x−y∥,
with finite M ≥0. An affine reference has M = 0.
Assumption 5.3 (Whole-policy derivative regularity). For x, y ∈Ω, ∥Dπ(x) −Dπ(y)∥≤Lπ∥x −y∥,
with finite Lπ ≥0. This is a condition on the complete deterministic action map, including its feature
extractor and output transformations.
Definition 5.4 (Jacobian consistency (JC)). For an action-valued symbolic reference πsym : Ω→
F and nonnegative alignment-error bound ε2, π is ε2-Jacobian consistent on Ωwhen ∥Dπ(x) −
Dπsym(x)∥≤ε2 for every x ∈Ω.
Definition 5.5 (Derivative regularity). A differentiable head H has derivative Lipschitz constant LH
when the norm of its derivative difference is at most LH times input distance. We use this property
to quantify curvature; it is shared by many smooth non-arithmetic maps.

NAU.
An NAU [5] computes HNAU(z) = WNAUz, where z ∈Rdz is a feature vector, dz is feature
dimension, and WNAU is a fixed weight matrix at evaluation. Training clamps weights to [−1, 1] and

Theorem 5.6 (NAU derivative constancy). For every fixed real matrix WNAU, DHNAU(z) = WNAU
for all z, so LHNAU = 0.
Proof. Differentiation of a linear map gives its fixed matrix. The derivative difference between any
two feature vectors is zero. Discrete weights are unnecessary for this derivative identity.

5.3
Training stabilization
We use six settings in the BAPR training backbone. Exponential moving average (EMA) normalization
stabilizes reward scale, while temporal-difference (TD) targets and entropy tuning retain the SAC
structure. The stabilized variants also use actor weight decay, with head-specific regularizers where
applicable. These settings are evaluated as a package; the tables do not identify an individual setting’s
causal contribution.

6
Experiments
We evaluate the transit-focused training recipe, with optional symbolic losses disabled. ID operation
uses nominal demand. The questions are whether stabilization improves the BAPR implementation,
whether the actor-head choice changes OOD performance, and how the behavior transfers to different
disturbance axes.
6.1
Transit benchmark and evaluation protocol
Environment.
MultiLineEnv is an event-driven holding simulator for 12 urban bus lines, using
SUMO-calibrated headway and dwell dynamics. The single-agent fleet formulation follows the
bus-control setting of Zhang [24]. At station arrivals, the normalized action a ∈[−1, 1] is converted to
hold duration hhold = 30(a+1) s, with a maximum of 60 s. This keeps the replay action in normalized
coordinates and the physical hold time in seconds. The 15-dimensional state includes headways, load,
boarding rate, speed, delay, previous action, and line/station/time descriptors. Poisson passenger
arrivals use line-specific nominal rates.
Approximate linearity diagnostic.
Headway-based control [25] and optimal linear schedule-
reliability control [26] motivate feedback that is locally linear in timing deviations. The recorded
diagnostic for a strong learned controller uses 47,247 decisions over five ID episodes: a 50/50 train/test
split gives a held-out coefficient of determination R2 = 0.70 for an ordinary-least-squares fit from
normalized state to hold time.
The recorded ID clamp rate is zero.
This diagnostic suggests
approximate local linearity; it does not establish a globally linear policy or prove that optimal holding
scales with demand.
OOD protocols.
The demand multiplier m scales the nominal Poisson arrival rate:
• Static: m ∈{1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50} for the full 18,000 s episode.
• Abrupt: m = 1 before t = 3600 s, followed by one switch to m ∈{5, 10, 20, 50}.
10


• Oscillating: piecewise-constant within-episode schedules. commuter_day uses multipliers
(1, 10, 2, 20, 5, 50) at times (0, 3600, 7200, 10800, 14400, 16200) s. square_wave_20x alternates 1
and 20 every 1800 s, starting at 1, for nine switches. escalating_10x uses (1, 2, 5, 15, 50) at
(0, 3600, 7200, 10800, 14400) s; the name is a configuration identifier.
Configurations.
The six reported transit configurations are bapr-pristine (ReLU MLP, six
settings disabled), bapr (historical legacy preset), csbapr-no-nau (ReLU MLP with stabilization),
csbapr (mixed arithmetic head with stabilization), csbapr-tanh (tanh-hidden MLP with stabiliza-
tion), and csbapr-kan (KAN with stabilization). The BC coefficient follows Section 5.3. Every
transit row uses Nseed = 10 seeds and 500 training episodes per seed, with the selected ID checkpoint
evaluated under the OOD protocols. Selection is based on the best ID score rather than an OOD
score. Tables report medians across seeds except Table 1, which reports mean and standard deviation.
Return-unit suffixes K and M mean 103 and 106; higher return is better. Appendix H documents
defaults and overrides.

7
Discussion and conclusion
The strongest empirical finding is that the stabilization recipe improves the evaluated BAPR
implementation on the transit demand protocols. Changing the actor head provides smaller, regime-
dependent differences: arithmetic is best at static 5× demand, KAN at nominal and near-nominal
demand, stabilized ReLU at several mid-to-high demand and switching conditions, and tanh at the
50× tail.
The theoretical finding is the conditional action-output bound (12), whose constants include both
policy and reference regularity. The layer calculations in Theorems 5.6 to 5.8 explain one source of
curvature control, while the composition analysis identifies the additional assumptions needed for
a complete policy certificate. The frozen Bellman analysis retains the predecessor’s fixed-backup
contraction template. The proof map in Appendix A connects each result to the supplied proof
rather than to an unavailable formal artifact.
7.1
Limitations
Whole-policy certification and performance.
The historical arithmetic actor has LeakyReLU
feature layers, a quadratic correction, and tanh squashing. It has not been certified to satisfy
14


Theorem 5.3 across the OOD segments; its earlier curvature surrogate was not a valid Lπ. The
revised smooth-nau variant uses tanh features and computes a whole deterministic-policy bound,
including the quadratic projection and action squash, as detailed in Appendix F.1. It leaves the
historical actor available and reports no finite certificate for that actor or a hard mean clamp. The
certificate covers normalized action outputs, not sampled-action distributions, episode return, or deep
actor-critic convergence. It can be conservative. The reported tables do not evaluate the new variant.
Symbolic and invariance components.
The historical symbolic implementation omitted actions
and used the projected loss (6). The revised code supplies actions to fitting and prediction, constructs
an LQR feedback reference, and implements (5). These repairs are implementation changes, not
retrospective validation of Table 6; new performance experiments are needed. Other tasks require
their own action-valued control construction. Prediction-error variance can prefer a consistently
inaccurate model; the selector neither proves derivative accuracy nor establishes SCM causality. IRM
filtering is inactive in the reported primary runs.
Experimental scope and accounting.
The 12-line transit simulator is one application setting;
LQR and Hopper have three seeds per principal configuration. The legacy Hopper comparison
has two completed seeds with interrupted runs excluded, and does not match the pristine baseline.
The historical transit bapr row predates later defaults; the current bapr entrypoint inherits those
defaults and does not by itself reproduce that older configuration. Actor weight decay and regularizer
strengths also belong to the compared recipe, so the pristine comparison is not a six-factor causal
ablation. Standard SAC, domain-randomized training, and robust adversarial RL (RARL) are not
included in the primary result tables. The comparisons establish improvement over the evaluated
predecessor configurations, with no universal claim for the stabilization package or head.
Reproducibility and proof artifacts.
The manuscript-level comma-separated values (CSV) file
transcribes aggregate table values. It does not replace original seed-level logs, checkpoint-selection
records, or traces supporting the linear-fit diagnostic; those historical artifacts are absent from the
retained package following storage cleanup. The diagnostics and statistical comparisons cannot be
independently reconstructed from this CSV alone. This repository snapshot supplies mathematical
proofs but no executable Lean project for the CS-BAPR results. Formalization tags in earlier drafts
were not a machine-checking certificate.
The next empirical step is a fresh comparison of the revised smooth actor and action-valued
feedback alignment, retaining per-seed returns and checkpoint-selection records. Boundary value
and operator-norm derivative errors in (12) are measured at the stated boundary point, not inferred
from replay-average losses or sample counts. Larger auxiliary seed sweeps and additional standard
baselines would then assess transfer beyond transit demand scaling.

before concatenating episodes. Its wrapper uses the fitted polynomial library’s exponent matrix,
including state-action cross terms and configured degrees up to four. Both prediction paths require

uniform derivative error. A prediction-quality threshold in the configuration is not a certificate of
Theorem 5.11’s action-reference hypotheses.
