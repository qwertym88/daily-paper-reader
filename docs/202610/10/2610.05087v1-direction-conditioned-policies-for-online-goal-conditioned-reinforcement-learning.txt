ABSTRACT
Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) learns representations that estimate
goal reachability, yet its policy remains conditioned on raw goals and therefore
does not directly exploit the geometry encoded by its critic. We introduce Direction-
Conditioned Policies (DCP), a method built around a small modification to CRL:
DCP selects previously visited states as waypoints during online training and
conditions the policy on their direction and distance in representation space. At
deployment, DCP applies the same interface directly to the final goal, requiring
neither waypoint selection nor planning. Across nine navigation and manipulation
tasks, DCP attains higher final success rates than CRL on seven tasks and spends
more time near the goal on seven. Controlled maze experiments further show
that DCP captures shortest-path geometry more accurately and that the supplied
direction causally influences the actor’s behavior. We identify waypoint coverage
and ranking as limits to exploration, and show that learned candidate generation
improves goal reaching in two controlled mazes.

INTRODUCTION
When learning to achieve goals through online interaction, an agent has to discover useful behavior
before it has much successful experience to draw on. Contrastive reinforcement learning (CRL;
Eysenbach et al., 2022) makes use of the states the agent reaches along the way. Its critic learns to
distinguish achieved future goals from other goals, and the policy learns to choose actions that receive
a higher critic score. This gives the agent a learning signal even when reaching the requested goal is
rare. Recent work has made this approach increasingly effective through stabilization techniques,
accelerated simulation, deeper networks, and action-sequence conditioning (Zheng et al., 2023;
Bortkiewicz et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Korniak et al., 2026).
However, the policy still receives the goal in raw coordinates. Consider a goal on the opposite side
of a maze wall: the coordinate difference points through the wall, even though reaching the goal
requires moving away from it first. The critic’s representation is learned from trajectories and can
capture this route structure. This raises a simple question: can we use that representation to express
the goal to the policy? Doing so would give the policy direct access to a geometric relation that the
critic already learns. The difficulty is that the representation itself is being learned online. Early in
training, a distant goal may lie outside the agent’s experience, making its learned direction unreliable.
We therefore consider two parts of the problem together: how to express a target to the policy, and
which targets to pursue while collecting experience. Previously visited states are natural candidate
waypoints (Eysenbach et al., 2019; Ecoffet et al., 2021). Scoring these candidates against the final
goal yields intermediate targets whose representations have been trained on collected experience.
We propose Direction-Conditioned Policies (DCP), a method built around a small change to the CRL
actor. We encode the current achieved goal and the target using the critic’s goal encoder, then supply
the normalized displacement and its magnitude to the actor, along with the full physical state. During
collection, the target is a scored visited waypoint. During hindsight updates, it is a future achieved

Direction-conditioned policies and embedding differences have been used in skill learning and offline

contributions are:
• An online CRL method that uses the critic’s goal representation to condition the actor on
direction and distance, with deployment requiring only the actor and goal encoder.
• A five-seed comparison across nine tasks, with higher final mean success and time near goal

• Controlled experiments that test route geometry, the actor’s use of direction, and waypoint

address a more disconnected, dynamic loco-manipulation task like AntSoccer.

RELATED WORK
Contrastive representations for control.
CRL derives a goal-reaching objective from contrastive
prediction of future states (Eysenbach et al., 2022). JaxGCRL makes large online comparisons
practical through accelerated simulation (Bortkiewicz et al., 2025). Depth scaling (Wang et al., 2025)
and action-sequence conditioning (Korniak et al., 2026) demonstrate that changes to architecture or
critic inputs can substantially alter the capabilities learned with this objective. Single-goal CRL also
demonstrates directed exploration before the first successful trial, without intermediate subgoals (Liu
et al., 2025). We ask how much of the learned representation can be reused by the policy itself. DCP
supplies relative goal-encoder features to the actor while retaining the baseline network depth and
single-action policy.
Direction-conditioned skills and goal representations.
HILP learns Hilbert representations and
directional policies from offline data (Park et al., 2024a); METRA learns metric-aware representa-
tions and skills through online unsupervised exploration (Park et al., 2024b). Plan arithmetic uses
compositional differences and sums in learned plan representations (Devin et al., 2019). Dual Goal
Representations encode goals through temporal distances from other states and improve offline goal

reaching (Park et al., 2026). Relative targets also appear in FeUdal Networks, whose worker follows
latent directions from a manager (Vezhnevets et al., 2017), HIRO’s state-offset subgoals (Nachum
et al., 2018), LSD’s latent skill directions (Park et al., 2022), and HIQL’s latent subgoal actions
(Park et al., 2023); learned temporal distances (Hartikainen et al., 2020; Myers et al., 2024) and
quasimetrics (Wang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Myers et al., 2025b) supply the underlying geometry.
We bring learned goal relations to an online contrastive actor–critic, retaining its actor objective and
learning the representation from the experience the direction-conditioned policy collects.
Interpolation and waypoint substitution.
Contrastive representations can support inference over
intermediate states under specific modeling assumptions (Eysenbach et al., 2024). Planning invariance
examines when an on-path waypoint and a final goal induce the same action (Myers et al., 2025a).
It arises for DCP, which trains on achieved targets but executes toward the final goal, and for flat
policies bootstrapped from subgoal-conditioned ones (Zhou & Kao, 2025). We test whether on-path
substitution preserves the action and how reliably the scorer selects such waypoints.

DCP replaces the raw-goal actor input with
[s, dθ(s, y), rθ(s, y)]. The direction specifies where the target lies in the learned space, and the

For nonzero displacement, direction and radius jointly recover the displacement up to numerical
stabilization. A waypoint and the final goal can share a direction while differing in distance; the actor
receives both quantities.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
DOES DCP IMPROVE ONLINE GOAL REACHING?
Figure 2 shows all nine tasks. The Ant and Humanoid navigation tasks require moving the robot to a
target position around walls. AntPush additionally requires moving an obstructing block to reach the
target position. AntSoccer requires moving a ball to the target. The three Pusher tasks require a robot
arm to move a cylinder and differ in target placement. Appendix B gives their goal distributions,
success conditions, and training budgets.
We evaluate DCP, CRL, and an ablation over CRL, which we term Self-Subgoal Conditioning (SSGC),
on nine Brax-based navigation and manipulation tasks (Freeman et al., 2021; Bortkiewicz et al.,
2025). SSGC uses the same waypoint selection as DCP and supplies raw waypoint coordinates to the
actor. All methods share task-specific interaction budgets, critic objectives, and principal optimization
settings. DCP’s larger input increases actor parameters by 11–21%, entirely in the first layer. Hidden
widths, depths, action heads, and critic parameter counts are unchanged; the total actor–critic increase
is 4.15–6.40% (Appendix A.3).
Each method is trained with five seeds. Episodes last 1,000 steps. AntPush, PusherHard, and
Humanoid U-Maze use 100M training steps; the other tasks use 50M. We report success rate, the
percentage of evaluation episodes entering the goal region at least once, and time near goal, the mean
number of steps spent inside it. We use both because a policy can reach the goal and then move away
from it. Final values refer to the last checkpoint. Figure 3 gives the learning curves; Appendix B
specifies task settings and Appendix G reports intermediate checkpoints.
DCP improves final mean success and time near goal over CRL on seven tasks each (Table 2). The
largest absolute success improvements occur on PusherEasy (+51.0 percentage points), PusherHard
Far (+34.9), and PusherHard (+18.9). DCP also improves success on all four navigation tasks,

4.3
DOES THE REPRESENTATION CAPTURE OBSTACLE-RESPECTING ROUTES?
Our motivation relies on the representation learning something useful about the routes an agent can
take. We examine this directly in a planar point-mass environment where we can compute shortest
paths around the walls. States include position and velocity, and actions are continuous forces.
Appendix C describes all controlled environments and shows their layouts. A weighted occupancy
graph gives exact shortest paths on a fixed spatial discretization, with diagonal edges allowed only
when collision-free. We evaluate 100,128 directed state pairs and separately inspect 2,336 pairs
where straight-to-goal motion conflicts with a graph-optimal first move. The graph provides a spatial
reference for evaluating the learned representations.
In a five-seed representation study, DCP’s goal-embedding distances correlate more strongly with
U-maze graph distances than CRL’s (Table 4). Figure 4 compares the learned fields with graph
distance for one seed; Appendix F shows the individual-seed results and AntMaze examples. On the
pairs where moving directly toward the goal conflicts with the shortest route, the negative spatial
gradient of the learned distance points toward a graph-optimal first move on 70.9% of pairs for DCP
and 64.7% for CRL, averaged across seeds. DCP’s open-room graph and Euclidean correlations differ
by less than 0.001 in mean, while its U-maze graph correlation exceeds its Euclidean correlation
by 0.065. These measurements show that the representation captures the obstacle’s effect on route
length. Each method learns its representation from its own trajectories. We also find that better
distance ordering alone is insufficient to explain control performance: mean U-maze success over
training is similar for DCP and CRL (0.847 and 0.846), and CRL performs better in the open room.
We therefore next examine the actor’s response to the learned direction.

4.5
WHEN DOES A WAYPOINT PRESERVE THE FINAL-GOAL ACTION?
The policy learns from achieved targets during training and receives the final goal at deployment.
For this change to work, a useful intermediate target should lead to an action consistent with the
final goal. We test this on three trained point-mass DCP policies, evaluating 20,000 start–goal pairs
per policy with two alternative targets: an exact midpoint on a graph-shortest path, and an off-path
control matched in distance from the source. Holding the actor and physical state fixed, we compare
each target’s action with the final-goal action. On-path substitution yields mean action cosine 0.789,
versus −0.072 for the off-path control; positive alignment with a graph-optimal first move occurs on
95.2% versus 41.9% of cases. The on-path target performs better on these measures across all seeds.
Here, the graph provides the midpoint, and substituting it changes both the direction and the distance.
We find that the learned policy largely preserves its action when the waypoint lies on the route to
the goal. The next question is whether our scorer actually finds such waypoints. Appendix C relates
latent triangle slack to direction agreement and includes the radius difference in the actor-output
bound.
4.6
HOW WELL DOES THE SCORER SELECT USEFUL WAYPOINTS?
A useful waypoint has to be present among the candidates and then chosen by the scorer. We examine
both steps using five trained U-maze encoders and 20,000 matched start–goal pairs and candidate sets
per encoder, sampled from the occupancy graph. An exact forward on-path candidate is available in
77.7% of draws. Uniform selection chooses one in 8.3%, while the inner-product scorer selects one
in 12.9–18.8%. The scorer also reduces median normalized path slack from 0.761 to 0.197–0.441 and
changes average normalized progress from negative to positive. We therefore see a clear improvement
over random selection, along with a substantial gap between having a useful candidate and choosing
it. Because candidates are sampled from the graph, this experiment tests the scoring rule separately
from the states collected during training.

4.7
CAN A LEARNED CANDIDATE SOURCE IMPROVE EXPLORATION?
The finite pool limits selection to states the agent has already visited. To address this, we replace
pool samples with candidates from an online four-component mixture model trained on trajectory
midpoints (Jurgenson et al., 2020). We keep the actor, critic, scorer, candidate count, and 10M-step
budget fixed. The generator conditions on the current achieved-goal coordinates and the final goal.
We also test whether restricting generated proposals to observed states helps: a third variant projects
each proposal to its nearest stored state before scoring. The nine-task comparison uses the default
visited-state pool.
The generator increases mean success over training in both tasks (Table 5), exceeding the pool in four
of five paired seeds in the two-route maze and all five in the rare-gateway maze. Projection improves
the mean in the two-route setting but removes the benefit at the rare gateway, where it can move
proposals to the wrong side of the passage. The generator extends candidate coverage beyond the
visited-state pool. Appendix D describes its training and the projection step.
4.8
DOES SEEDING THE POOL WITH SUCCESSFUL STATES HELP?
A more direct way to improve candidate availability is to add states from successful trajectories.
We use this intervention to test whether supplying useful states is enough to improve control, while
keeping replay unchanged. Each task uses three target seeds and matched libraries of successful-path
states and states preceding meaningful object motion. On PusherHard, reserving 128 of the 512
slots for successful-path states increases mean success over training from 32.39% with the matched
pre-motion library to 38.17%, winning all three paired seeds. However, adding 128 states while
preserving all 512 online slots and their expected exposure gives no consistent benefit: successful-
path seeding scores 36.26%, compared with 38.09% for the matched control. On AntSoccer, the
scorer selects successful-path states on 30.91% of refreshes, versus 4.59% for pre-motion states,
and waypoint-to-goal direction agreement improves in every seed. Ball motion and success do not
improve. Analysis of completed training runs also finds sparse ball motion and little change from the
initial representation’s distance ordering (Appendix E). In this case, the scorer recognizes the added
states, but the policy still fails to turn them into useful ball motion.
5
CONCLUSION
We asked whether a CRL policy could make better use of the geometry learned by its critic. DCP
supplies the actor with the direction and distance to a target in representation space, using visited
waypoints during collection and the final goal at deployment. Across nine tasks, DCP improves final
mean success and time near goal over CRL on seven tasks each.
Our analyses show that the representation captures route structure and that the actor uses the supplied
direction in navigation and manipulation. They also clarify where the difficulty lies. Waypoint
selection can slow learning when direct goal conditioning already works, and better candidates are
useful only if the learned policy can act on them. Learned generation improves exploration in two
9


controlled mazes; adding successful states to the pool leaves AntSoccer unresolved. Improving
the connection between candidate selection and the behavior the policy can execute is therefore an
important next step.
Our experiments use simulated state observations. Actor sizes and collected trajectories differ across
methods, motivating parameter-matched and shared-data comparisons. Further work could extend
DCP to visual observations and combine it with deeper or action-chunked CRL.

selects a forward on-path candidate in 12.9–18.8% of draws; cosine ranking achieves 24.7–27.8%,
and minimizing the sum of learned distances achieves 28.6–33.0%. Uniform selection achieves 8.3%.
Both alternatives improve waypoint selection in this test. Whether they improve online learning
remains untested: changing the selection rule would also change the experience collected and the
representation learned.

D
CANDIDATE GENERATION AND DEMONSTRATION SEEDING
D.1
ONLINE CANDIDATE GENERATOR
The generator is a conditional mixture-density network with four diagonal-Gaussian components and
two hidden layers of width 128. Its inputs are the current achieved-goal coordinates and a desired
endpoint. Training samples an ordered triple of indices i < k < j from a replay sequence, with
k = ⌊(i + j)/2⌋, and fits the midpoint coordinates by negative log likelihood. Triples crossing
trajectory identifiers are masked out. The learning rate is 3 × 10−4 and the generator warm-up is 1M
interaction steps. Component scales are constrained between 0.05 and 2, with initial scale and offset
scale 0.5. Candidate count stays at 32.
The unprojected variant uses sampled goal-coordinate proposals as the waypoint targets. The projected
variant maps proposals to the nearest pool state’s goal coordinates, then applies the same scorer.
Projection returns a stored target but can move a proposal across an obstacle. All three variants retain
the same deployment policy input. The default DCP configuration uses the visited-state pool.
D.2
DEMONSTRATION-STATE LIBRARIES
We construct each task’s libraries from nine successful DCP episodes, three per source seed. The
successful-path library contains 128 states chosen around object displacement. For the control, we
take the same number of states from those episodes before meaningful object motion. This lets us test
the value of the states supplied to the pool while keeping replay and contrastive training unchanged.
In the reserved-slot comparison, each library occupies 128 of the 512 slots, leaving 384 online slots.
The augmented comparison instead preserves all 512 online slots and adds 128 library slots. It draws
40 candidates so that the expected number of online draws remains 32, approximately preserving
each online state’s exposure. The reserved-slot experiment tests the value of library content under
constrained capacity. The augmented experiment tests the benefit of adding those states to the full
online pool.

E
ADDITIONAL ANALYSES
E.1
LEARNING EARLY IN TRAINING AND MAINTAINING SUCCESS
The direction input is learned online, so we also examine whether an initially inaccurate representation
delays learning and whether success is maintained once it begins. In a three-seed analysis of the nine
tasks, DCP crosses 2% success earlier than CRL on seven tasks and ties on two. At 5% and 10%,
it is earlier on six, tied on one, and later on two. Its success over the first 25M steps is higher on
seven tasks, tied on AntPush, and lower on AntSoccer. These comparisons concern early learning,
separately from the final five-seed results.
After first crossing each threshold, we measure the fraction of the next ten evaluations that falls below
it. The median fraction at 5% and 10% is 0% for DCP, compared with 10% and 20% for CRL. This
measures persistence after initial success.
On AntPush, mean DCP success decreases from 44.8% at 75M steps to 42.7% at 100M steps in the
five-seed results. The learning curves show the full course of training, including this late decline.
E.2
ANTSOCCER REPRESENTATION AND ACTIVITY
To understand what happens on AntSoccer, we examine how much the representation changes and
how often the agent moves the ball. Across five seeds, we compare distance ordering on fixed
coordinate pairs with its ordering at initialization. On AntSoccer, the mean Spearman-correlation
change is only −0.050 at 25M and −0.021 at the final checkpoint; the final changes are larger in
magnitude on AntPush and the Pusher tasks. Only 3.8% of retained consecutive AntSoccer transitions
move the ball by more than 10−3. AntSoccer combines sparse object movement with little change in
distance ordering.
We measured these signals in completed runs. They identify AntSoccer as unusual among the
evaluated tasks. Tracking fixed coordinate probes and replay activity would also make them available
during training; predicting individual-run outcomes requires further testing.
E.3
FROZEN CRL REPRESENTATIONS
If direction conditioning can use geometry learned by CRL, it should also be possible to train a new
actor on a frozen CRL representation. We test this directly. A single-seed AntMaze Big experiment
starts from a CRL checkpoint trained for 50M steps and trains a new DCP actor for 25M additional
interactions. One variant freezes only the goal encoder and adapts the state–action encoder; another
freezes both encoders. A fresh joint DCP receives 25M steps without pretraining.
