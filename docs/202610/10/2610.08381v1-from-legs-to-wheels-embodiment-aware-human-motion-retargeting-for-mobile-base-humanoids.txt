From Legs to Wheels: Embodiment-Aware Human Motion Retargeting
for Mobile-Base Humanoids
Jiyeon Koo, Eunseom Pyo, Jeonghee Seo, Taehwa Kim, and Andrew Jaeyong Choi∗
Abstract— Human video offers a scalable source of robot
demonstrations,
yet
most
human-to-humanoid
retargeting
methods assume a legged robot with human-like kinematics.
This assumption does not hold for mobile-base humanoids
equipped with a wheeled base, vertical lift, and two arms.
Human walking must be expressed through base motion,
while torso bending may require coordinated lift and arm
motion. We address this mismatch with a task-conditioned
framework that assigns reconstructed human motion to base,
lift, and arm responsibilities before robot-specific realization.
The allocator preserves the human-derived path, stabilizes
heading, separates turn and translation when needed, retimes
commands to satisfy base limits, and repairs lift and arm
trajectories. A deployment adapter then converts the reference
to 50 Hz commands using stationary-base detection, deadband
and slew-rate filtering, time-consistent playback scaling, and
separate linear and angular gains. We evaluate the resulting
references with human-derived task-space comparisons, policy-
free simulation replay, and a qualitative execution on a physical
robot.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid motion imitation has advanced through the
combination of human motion capture, retargeting, and re-
inforcement learning (RL). Recent systems retarget whole-
body motion to legged humanoids and train policies to follow
the resulting references in simulation or on hardware [1], [2],
[3]. These pipelines generally assume that the target is also
a biped. Human walking, torso orientation, and limb motion
can therefore be mapped to robot legs, trunk, and arms with
fairly direct correspondence.
We consider a different embodiment: mobile-base hu-
manoids. These robots retain a human-facing upper body and
two arms, but replace the legs with a wheeled base and may
use a vertical lift in place of a human-like spine. Simple joint
correspondence is no longer sufficient. A person walking
forward with still arms should induce base motion, whereas
bending to reach a low object may require changes in base
position, lift height, and arm configuration. Retargeting must
therefore determine not only how to reproduce a motion, but
also which robot subsystem should express each human cue.

gives an overview of our approach. From a reconstructed
motion sequence and a lightweight task condition, the frame-
work extracts locomotion intent for the base, vertical reach
and body-height intent for the lift, and manipulation intent
for the arms. A robot-specific realization module converts

• A task-conditioned motion allocator with explicit base,
lift, and arm variables. Phase-gated human-motion cues
replace one-to-one joint matching.
• A subsystem-specific temporal repair method that pre-

• A 50 Hz deployment adapter for time-consistent

policy-free simulation replay, and qualitative physical-

B. Human-to-Humanoid Motion Retargeting
Classical whole-body retargeting transfers human link
poses to analogous robot links through geometric task
objectives and inverse kinematics [12]. GMR and related
tracking pipelines show that reference quality directly affects
downstream policy learning and tracking robustness [13].
Infeasible poses and inconsistent contacts should therefore be
corrected before control. Existing methods, however, mainly
target legged humanoids and represent human locomotion
through robot leg motion. Our setting instead has to express
that locomotion with a mobile base.
RL-based imitation has evolved from reference tracking
in DeepMimic [14] to reusable motion priors and skill
embeddings learned from large motion collections [15],
[16]. PHC [17], H2O [18], ExBody [1], HumanPlus [2],
OmniH2O [3], and BeyondMimic [19] demonstrate whole-
body tracking of retargeted references. Recent Humanoids
work also learns poses from internet video [20] and generates
editable motion through postural synergies [21]. Our focus
is earlier in the pipeline: generating feasible references when
the robot’s lower body is not human-like.
C. Mobile Manipulation from Human Demonstrations
Mobile ALOHA learns bimanual mobile manipulation
from whole-body teleoperation data [22]. Related Humanoids
studies cover compact mobile humanoid hardware [23], non-
holonomic whole-body impedance control [24], and cou-
pled versus decoupled embodiment in whole-body teleop-
eration [25]. Human demonstrations have also supported
manipulation-speed adaptation [26] and assistive motion
generation [27]. WARP retargets offline demonstrations to
whole-body mobile manipulation while accounting for em-
bodiment differences [28]. Our work asks a complementary
question: how should reconstructed whole-body cues be
divided among a mobile base, vertical lift, and two arms
before GMR-based realization?
D. Optimization-Based Retargeting and IK
Retargeting is commonly formulated as constrained op-
timization over robot configurations. The objective mini-
mizes task-space error subject to joint, velocity, collision,
and smoothness constraints. Differential inverse kinematics
tools such as Mink solve weighted velocity-level tasks in a
constrained MuJoCo model [29]. Recent Humanoids work
learns differentiable reachability constraints for motion gen-
eration [30] and uses continuous contact optimization for
whole-body manipulation [31]. We add an allocation stage
before GMR realization to decide whether each human cue
should drive the base, lift, arms, or a combination of these
subsystems.

A. Human Motion Representation
Fig. 3 shows the motion representation used in our imple-
mentation. Inputs may come from monocular reconstruction,
RGB-D pose estimation, or an existing 3D motion file. At
minimum, we require the root trajectory, torso orientation,
and wrist trajectories. Object position and hand-object con-
tact timing can further inform allocation in manipulation
demonstrations. We normalize the input to a robot-centric
frame by removing global camera drift, converting coordinate
conventions, and estimating the initial facing direction. Our
experiments use precomputed SMPL-style motion [4], [11],
but the allocator only requires the normalized representation
and is not tied to a specific estimator.
B. Task-Conditioned Motion Allocator
The allocator combines human motion cues with a
lightweight task label, such as walking, reaching, bending, or
picking. It is an interpretable optimization and repair module,
not a learned semantic model. The task label resolves cases
in which similar body motions imply different robot actions.
Phase gating further prevents transient or irrelevant cues from
activating the wrong subsystem. The resulting base, lift, and
arm responsibilities form a robot-independent intermediate
sequence.
Locomotion cue: Horizontal root displacement and yaw
change drive the base. Under a locomotion condition,
walking-like root motion receives a high base prior, while
human arm swing is down-weighted as a manipulation target.
Reach-height cue: Hand height, torso pitch, and shoulder-
to-hand displacement determine whether to move the vertical
lift. A robot without a human-like waist cannot directly
reproduce torso lowering. The allocator instead lowers the
lift when available and replans the arm pose. It also stabilizes
monocular root height so that camera or pose-estimation drift
does not trigger unintended lift motion.
Manipulation cue: Wrist trajectories and hand orienta-
tions drive the arms. For manipulation tasks, the allocator
increases hand-tracking gains and uses the base or lift when
needed to keep the target reachable.

C. GMR-Based Motion Realization
Given allocation weights zt, the realization module solves
for robot states and command trajectories. It first converts the
intermediate states to GMR-compatible frames and applies
GMR to the upper body. The allocated planar base pose and
lift reference are then inserted into the mobile-base humanoid
trajectory. We also propagate lift displacement to the arm-
base target to maintain consistent end-effector motion.

D. Base-Preserving Temporal Repair

between consecutive robot states. Abrupt changes may arise
when a target falls outside the workspace or the IK solution
switches branches. We apply subsystem-specific temporal
repair after GMR to smooth these changes without discarding
the allocated base path. A minimum-jerk blend reconstructs
base heading and suppresses unintended translation during
dominant turns. We redistribute the removed translation
over valid non-turn intervals, preserving net displacement
and cumulative path progress. Motion-timed soft decoupling
follows the turn timing of the human-derived motion rather
than imposing a fixed “turn first” schedule. Speed retiming

For the lift, we separate intentional bending and reach-

E. Command and Deployment Adapter
We resample the repaired position reference to 50 Hz for
simulation and command export. The adapter differentiates
the planar base pose into (vx, vy, ω). When net displacement
and yaw are negligible but the path-to-net ratio is high,
it treats the trajectory as pose-estimation jitter and holds
the base stationary. Otherwise, it applies moving-average
smoothing, linear/angular deadbands, and separate acceler-
ation slew limits. The hold, deadband, and slew checks run
again immediately before command publication.

V. EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments compare embodiment-aware allocation
with subsystem-limited retargeting baselines. We evaluate the
generated references through simulation replay and compo-
nent ablations. These experiments assess whether the refer-
ences can be loaded, replayed, and compared across decom-
position baselines; they do not measure learned-controller
performance.
A. Target Platform
The physical demonstration uses a ROBOTIS AI Worker
(FFW-SG2). This mobile-base humanoid has an omnidirec-
tional wheeled base, two 7-DoF arms, grippers, head joints,
and a vertical lift. Its reference contains 31 joints: wheel
drive and steering joints, both arms, grippers, head joints,
and the lift joint. Realization and command export use the
measured robot model, joint and velocity limits, lift range,
and controller interface.

B. Motion Categories
We selected nine representative scenarios from a larger
collection:
• Locomotion: slow walk, run, side walk, and sideways-
turn walk, expressed mainly through the base while
suppressing task-irrelevant arm swing.
• Bend-and-reach: forward bending at different reach
amplitudes, requiring coordinated lift and arm motion
with little base drift.
• Arm-and-base coordination: a backward step with
arms forward and a wide-arm lateral motion, combining
base relocation with smooth arm timing.
• Low pick-like motion: a low-object pick pose, requir-
ing lift lowering and a coordinated arm approach.
C. Baselines
We compare against the following baselines:
• Arm-only IK: map human wrists to robot end-effectors
without moving the base or lift.
• Base-only root tracking: map human root motion to
base motion and keep arms near a nominal pose.
• No-lift allocation: preserve the generated base and arm
trajectories while freezing the vertical lift. This isolates
the role of lift assignment in bending and low-object
motions.
• Ours: the proposed base-preserving allocation, GMR-
based realization, and temporal repair stack.
D. Metrics
Our metrics separate semantic imitation from reference
executability. Semantic imitation uses human-derived task
targets extracted from GVHMR-reconstructed SMPL motion
rather than our generated robot trajectory. We compare only
task-relevant components: root displacement and speed for
locomotion, wrist displacement and height for reaching, and
torso-lowering cues converted to desired lift-height com-
pensation. Since monocular absolute scale is imperfect, we
normalize component errors within each scenario before
averaging. The resulting costs support relative comparisons:
lower values indicate that the robot reference retains more
of the intended human cue.
We measure executability after converting each trajectory
to a 50 Hz simulation reference. A replay is complete when
it loads and runs without early termination. Body mean error
is the average Cartesian discrepancy between the main robot
body and its replay target. Anchor mean error measures
consistency at the base and end-effector anchor frames.

VI. REFERENCE VALIDATION
Our implementation reads reconstructed 3D human motion
and produces task-conditioned base, lift, and arm alloca-
tions. It realizes the upper body through GMR, repairs
the trajectory while preserving the base path, and renders
kinematic previews. The 50 Hz command adapter exports
the same references for execution. In Fig. 4, Task 06 uses
one reference in both simulation and the physical robot; the
intended lift and bilateral arm coordination is retained in
both. The remaining results cover the larger simulation set.
The evaluation set comprises nine generated trajectories
spanning locomotion, bending and reaching, base-arm coor-
dination, and low-pick-like motion. We examine continuous
error against human-derived task targets, component-wise
ablations, and policy-free simulation playback.
Table II reports normalized task-space costs against the
human-derived target. These costs are not binary success
labels, and the generated motion is not used as ground truth.
Methods that retain base motion are expected to perform
similarly on locomotion. Manipulation and reaching, by
contrast, reveal whether the base, lift, and arms are coor-
dinated. We report grouped means for readability and retain
the full scenario-wise matrix with the experiment artifacts.
The full method matches the base-preserving baselines on
locomotion and achieves the lowest grouped mean across all
nine scenarios.

bend-to-lift compensation. No restricted baseline covers ev-

performance while retaining both wrist behavior and lift
compensation, supporting coordinated allocation across the
base, lift, and arms.

VII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
Our formulation is less anthropomorphic than standard
humanoid retargeting, which suits mobile-base robots but
introduces ambiguity. The same human motion may corre-
spond to several robot command sequences. A far reach, for
example, can be expressed by extending the arm, driving the
base, raising the lift, or combining all three. The allocator
must choose among these alternatives while respecting both
feasibility and natural robot behavior.
The study has four main limitations. First, the experiments
use precomputed 3D human motion; comparing alternative
reconstruction methods remains future work. Second, the


allocator is interpretable and partly scenario-conditioned.
Broader baselines, including direct GMR without allocation,
and held-out evaluation would better characterize general-
ization without per-sequence tuning. Third, direct playback
verifies reference consistency and simulator compatibility but
not disturbance rejection. Finally, the physical-robot result is
qualitative, and broader hardware evaluation should measure
completion and tracking error systematically.
Learning-based tracking is a downstream direction rather
than a claim of this work. A supervised tracker or PPO policy
could assess how well the references withstand dynamics
and perturbations. Our results instead support explicit allo-
cation before retargeting: GMR-based realization yields re-
playable mobile-base references and exposes the limitations
of subsystem-restricted mappings.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We introduced task-conditioned embodiment-aware mo-
tion allocation for human video imitation on mobile-base
humanoids. Rather than matching joints directly, the method
assigns motion to the base, lift, and arms, then repairs
heading, timing, and subsystem trajectories while preserving
base-path geometry. GMR realizes the upper body, and a 50
Hz adapter handles stationary-base suppression, slew lim-
its, and time-consistent linear/angular scaling. Across nine
scenarios, our references were compared with subsystem-
restricted baselines and replayed without early termination.
We also executed one reference on the physical robot as a
qualitative demonstration. Future evaluation will add broader
retargeting baselines, held-out scenarios, and quantitative
physical-robot measurements.
