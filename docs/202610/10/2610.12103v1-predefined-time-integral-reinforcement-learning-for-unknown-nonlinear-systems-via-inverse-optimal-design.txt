Inverse-Optimal Design

Abstract
This paper develops a new predefined-time integral reinforcement learning framework for optimal control of unknown nonlinear
systems. The unknown drift is first approximated by a radial basis function (RBF) neural network, together with a data-driven online
identification law for updating the corresponding neural weights. After the identifier converges to a sufficiently small neighborhood
of the true dynamics, the learned model is incorporated into the integral reinforcement learning (IRL) problem. Unlike conventional
reinforcement-learning-based optimal control, the desired convergence time is introduced directly into the control objective: a
Lyapunov function and its prescribed decay behavior are specified by the designer, and inverse-optimal control is then used to
construct a compatible running cost whose optimal policy inherits the predefined-time stabilization property. The value function is
approximated by a second RBF neural network, and a new critic update law is developed to impose predefined-time convergence
on the critic weights. Finite informative learning data are stored in a replay buffer and reused during the critic update, thereby
avoiding the need for persistent excitation throughout the closed-loop operation. Theoretical analysis proves that the critic-weight
error enters a prescribed residual set within the allocated learning horizon, while the closed-loop state reaches a small neighborhood
of the origin within the overall designer-specified deadline. Numerical simulations on an unknown nonlinear system verify accurate
drift reconstruction, predefined-time critic learning, and closed-loop convergence.
Keywords: Predefined-time control; Adaptive Dynamic Programming; integral reinforcement learning; inverse-optimal control;
concurrent learnings
1. Introduction
Modern cyber–physical and networked control systems increas-
ingly involve unknown nonlinear dynamics, limited model in-
formation, and demanding performance requirements, which
make conventional model-based optimal control difficult to ap-
ply directly (Wan et al., 2021; Lee, 2015). Although optimal
control provides a systematic way to design feedback laws from
a performance objective, nonlinear problems are generally gov-
erned by the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, whose
analytical solution is rarely available. The difficulty becomes
greater when the drift dynamics are unknown, since the corre-
sponding optimal feedback law cannot be constructed directly
(Doya, 2000; Vamvoudakis and Lewis, 2010). Moreover, in
many control applications, it is desirable to prescribe the con-
vergence time in advance and make this bound independent of
the initial condition (Polyakov, 2012; Jiménez-Rodríguez et al.,
2017; Kokolakis et al., 2025).
Reinforcement learning (RL) and adaptive dynamic pro-
gramming (ADP) provide data-driven tools for approximating
solutions of the HJB equation without solving the nonlinear
optimal-control problem explicitly (Doya, 2000; Vrabie et al.,
2009; Vamvoudakis and Lewis, 2010). Integral reinforcement
learning (IRL) further replaces the differential Bellman equa-
tion with an integral relation constructed from measured tra-
jectories, which reduces the direct dependence of the learn-

allel, fixed-time and predefined-time control provide system-
atic ways to guarantee convergence within an initial-condition-
independent time bound (Polyakov, 2012; Jiménez-Rodríguez
et al., 2017). More recently, fixed- and predefined-time critic-
learning laws have been developed using current and recorded
data, showing that sufficiently informative stored samples can
replace the persistent-excitation requirement (Kokolakis et al.,
2023, 2025).
A natural way to connect prescribed-time stabilization with
optimal control is through inverse-optimal design. Instead of
solving the HJB equation directly, one first selects a desired
value function and a prescribed Lyapunov decay, and then con-
structs a running cost for which the resulting feedback law is
optimal. This idea provides a useful bridge between predefined-
time stabilization and data-driven optimal learning (Jiménez-
Rodríguez et al., 2017; Kokolakis et al., 2025). However, for an
unknown nonlinear system, such an inverse-optimal construc-
tion still requires information about the unknown drift, since
the drift appears explicitly in the feedback and running-cost ex-
pressions.
To address this model uncertainty, the unknown drift can
be reconstructed before the IRL stage. In the present frame-
work, the drift is represented by a neural basis expansion
with unknown weights and estimated through integral con-
current learning (ICL). The ICL mechanism combines current
moving-window information with informative stored integral
data and therefore allows parameter learning under finite ex-

citation (Chowdhary and Johnson, 2010; Parikh et al., 2019a).
A finite-data least-squares estimate is used only to obtain an in-
formed initial condition, after which the ICL law continues to
update the drift weights rather than treating the batch solution
as an exact model (Chowdhary and Johnson, 2010; Parikh et al.,
2019a).
The reconstructed drift is then used in the inverse-
optimal construction, while the subsequent critic learning is
formulated directly from integral Bellman data (Modares et al.,
2014; Parikh et al., 2019a).
Existing results address only separate parts of this problem.
Modares et al. combined IRL with experience replay for par-
tially unknown continuous-time systems and used stored data to
relax the persistent-excitation requirement, but no predefined-
time convergence guarantee was established (Modares et al.,
2014).
Kokolakis et al.
developed fixed- and predefined-
time critic learning with current and replayed data and estab-
lished optimal stabilization properties (Kokolakis et al., 2023,
2025). However, the corresponding Bellman residual still con-
tains explicit system-dynamics terms, which limits direct im-
plementation when the nonlinear drift is unknown (Kokolakis
et al., 2025). On the other hand, classical concurrent learn-
ing and integral concurrent learning provide parameter conver-
gence under finite excitation, but they do not by themselves
construct a predefined-time optimal controller or guarantee
predefined-time convergence of an IRL critic (Chowdhary and
Johnson, 2010; Parikh et al., 2019a). Likewise, inverse-optimal
predefined-time methods can construct a running cost and feed-
back law from a prescribed Lyapunov decay, but their direct
implementation still requires model information that is unavail-
able for an unknown nonlinear plant (Jiménez-Rodríguez et al.,
2017).
These observations reveal a gap between unknown-dynamics
reconstruction, integral optimal learning, and designer-assigned
predefined-time convergence (Modares et al., 2014; Parikh
et al., 2019a; Kokolakis et al., 2025). In particular, the inverse-
optimal controller cannot be implemented without an estimate
of the unknown drift, while a predefined-time critic result alone
does not provide a convergence deadline for the coupled state–
critic dynamics (Jiménez-Rodríguez et al., 2017; Kokolakis
et al., 2025).
Motivated by these issues, the main contributions of this pa-
per are threefold. First, beyond establishing several theoretical
results and corollaries, a unified predefined-time IRL frame-
work is developed for unknown nonlinear systems by inte-
grating online drift identification, integral concurrent learning,
inverse-optimal design, and critic-based reinforcement learning
within a single architecture. This enables the prescribed-time
optimal-control construction to be carried out without prior
knowledge of the nonlinear drift. Second, a new critic-only IRL
update law is developed using current integral Bellman data
together with a finite informative replay stack, thereby guar-
anteeing practical predefined-time critic learning without re-
quiring persistent excitation throughout the closed-loop opera-
tion. Third, a unified state–critic analysis provides explicit gain
and horizon conditions that connect the critic-learning horizon
and state-decay horizon to a designer-assigned overall deadline,
yielding practical predefined-time closed-loop convergence.

3. Predefined-Time Reinforcement Learning Optimal Con-
trol
3.1. Predefined-Time Optimal-Control Formulation
The normalized predefined-time comparison result in Theo-
rem 1 provides a direct mechanism for assigning a desired
convergence-time bound in advance. In particular, rather than
selecting the decay gains first and subsequently obtaining an

For the aggressive prescribed deadline Tp = 1 s, Figs. 3 and 4

The learned policy in Fig. 4 closely follows the inverse-

The RMS mismatch over [TW, Tp] is only 3.4128 × 10−6, while

For the relaxed deadline Tp = 100 s, Figs. 7 and 8 ex-

stantially milder control transient. The state trajectories con-

predefined-time property, because Tp acts as an upper bound on

The policy-tracking performance in Fig. 8 is also signifi-
cantly tighter. The RMS mismatch between the learned and
inverse-optimal policies over [TW, Tp] decreases to 3.6532 ×

The learning behavior for the relaxed deadline is shown in
Figs. 9 and 10. At TW = 29.955 s, the critic error is reduced
to 4.0012 × 10−4, which is more than one order of magni-
tude smaller than that obtained for the Tp = 1 s case. The
value-function approximation error similarly becomes negligi-
ble, confirming that the same inverse-optimal solution is recov-
ered under the relaxed timing specification.
Taken together, Figs. 3–10 and Table 2 reveal a clear
performance–effort trade-off. Reducing Tp compresses the state
and critic-learning transients but requires a larger control ac-
tion, whereas increasing Tp relaxes the required dissipation and
learning rates and substantially reduces the transient control ef-
fort. At the same time, the final identifier accuracy and the re-
covered inverse-optimal solution remain essentially unchanged.
The comparison also shows that predefined-time conver-
gence should not be interpreted as convergence occurring ex-
actly at Tp. The Tp = 100 s trajectories settle much earlier
than the assigned deadline, which is fully consistent with a
guaranteed upper bound on the convergence time. Conversely,
the Tp = 1 s case demonstrates the cost of enforcing a highly
aggressive deadline: the transient control demand increases
markedly even though the learned policy ultimately converges
to the same inverse-optimal solution.
From a practical perspective, this trade-off is important in
systems subject to actuator saturation, state constraints, or
safety envelopes. An excessively short prescribed deadline may
demand control amplitudes that are infeasible for the physical
actuator even when the theoretical predefined-time conditions
are satisfied. Therefore, Tp should be selected jointly with the
available control authority and the admissible operating region.
This observation motivates future extensions that combine the
proposed predefined-time IRL mechanism with explicit input
constraints, control-barrier conditions, or invariant-set certifi-
cates.
6. Conclusion
This paper has successfully developed a theoretical framework
for predefined-time stabilization in optimal control of fully un-
known nonlinear systems. The obtained results address limi-
tations remaining in the work of Kokolakis et al. (Kokolakis
et al., 2025) and further extend the existing theory by formulat-
ing the problem in the integral reinforcement learning setting,
which is more suitable for practical implementation and for
systems with unknown dynamics. Although integral reinforce-
ment learning was introduced by Modares et al. (Modares et al.,
2014), the present study advances this direction by establishing
an update law that guarantees prescribed-time convergence for
a fully unknown nonlinear system, which is particularly rele-
vant to cyber–physical systems with strict timing requirements.
The paper also introduces a new proof framework for the cou-
pled learning and closed-loop stability problem, and the theo-
retical analysis establishes predefined-time stability while the
simulations confirm the main properties predicted by the the-
ory. Two prescribed deadlines, Tp = 1 s and Tp = 100 s, are
used to examine the controller under aggressive and relaxed
timing requirements and to provide guidance for practical tun-
ing of the prescribed convergence time. At the same time, the
Tp = 100 s case shows that the actual convergence occurs con-
siderably earlier than the assigned deadline, indicating that the
current convergence-time bound remains conservative. Such
conservatism may be undesirable when the convergence pro-
file must closely match a prescribed time, and therefore moti-
vates future work on less conservative analysis and design con-
ditions that more tightly relate the assigned deadline to the ac-
tual closed-loop convergence time.
