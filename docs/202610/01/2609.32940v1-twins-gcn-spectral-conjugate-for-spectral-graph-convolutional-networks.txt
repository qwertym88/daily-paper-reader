## ABSTRACT 

Graph convolutional networks propagate information by repeated local aggrega-tion through a graph shift operator; i.e., a K-layer network reaches K hops neigh-borhood. On the one hand, such spreading can lead to oversmoothing. On the other hand, long-range dependencies demand the depth. Transporting informa-tion on long distances and without attenuation requires the shift to distinguish a direction of flow, which a symmetric operator cannot perform but a directed one can fulfill. A natural way to extract pure-directionality is to take the skew-symmetric part of the shift operator through the Cartesian split, which, however, generally does not commute with the shift itself, meaning that the filters built on it are not shift-invariant. We instead use the spectral conjugate; i.e., the image of the operator under τ : z 7 → ¯z, which commutes with the shift and splits it into a dissipative and a non-dissipative part. Two filter families follow: a sum filter, whose non-dissipative component transports signal without energy loss, and a ratio filter, ratio in the pair of components rather than polynomial in the shift. Both arise from non-holomorphic kernels, placing them outside the holomorphic class underlying classical spectral convolution. On the directed cycle, the ratio filter becomes an IIR filter with global impulse response, for which we prove a long-range reach gap against every degree-K polynomial filter. Chebyshev repa-rameterization gives stable vertex-domain layers with real coefficients, yielding TWIN S-GCN, which solves graph transfer tasks at reduced depth and is com-petitive with state-of-the-art graph convolutional networks on node classification benchmarks. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph convolutional networks are among the most widely used architectures for learning on rela-tional data, owing to their efficiency, being light weight and to their increased interpretability given the spectral theory that underpins them (Bruna et al., 2014; Defferrard et al., 2016; Kipf & Welling, 2017). However, two limitations are well documented: node representations tending to converge toward a constant as depth increases, known as oversmoothing (Li et al., 2018; Oono & Suzuki, 2020; Cai & Wang, 2020; Rusch et al., 2023) and a K-layer network aggregating over at most K

hops neighborhood, which requires sufficient depth to capture long-range dependencies (Topping et al., 2022; Di Giovanni et al., 2023). Residual connections, normalization, and rewiring (Li et al., 2019; Alon & Yahav, 2021) serve as effective solutions for both obstacles, yet, because our goal is to address their common cause, we do not compare against this line of work. The reasons for these limitations are largely related to the nature of the operator used in the convo-lutional networks. For a symmetric graph operator, high frequencies tend to be attenuated due to its real spectrum, leading to progressively less distinctive features since spectral convolution strength-ens this attenuation when the number of convolution layers (depth) increases (Oono & Suzuki, 2020; Cai & Wang, 2020). Escaping this requires eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, an established cri-terion for stable non-dissipative dynamics (Haber & Ruthotto, 2018; Chang et al., 2019), which A-DGN and its successors obtain by imposing skew-symmetry on the learnable weights (Gravina 1arXiv:2609.32940v1 [cs.LG] 26 Sep 2026 et al., 2023; Heilig et al., 2025; Hariri et al., 2026). For directed graphs, the graph shift already pos-sesses non-zero imaginary part as it is non-symmetric (Singh et al., 2016; Marques et al., 2020a). A natural way to extract skew-symmetry and thus imaginary spectrum is the Cartesian split, which separates the shift operator into its symmetric and skew-symmetric parts. We show, however, that neither part commutes with the shift unless the graph is normal—which directed graphs generally are not (Trefethen, 2005; Asllani et al., 2018)—so the induced filters are not shift-invariant. To overcome this limitation while still obtaining imaginary spectrum, we instead use the spectral con-jugate (Nevanlinna, 2018), which commutes with the shift on any graph, coincides with the transpose when the shift is normal, and remains defined for non-diagonalizable shifts. On this decomposition, we build learnable graph convolutional layers from filters in (Chan et al., 2026a). Composing the re-sulting layers gives the T WIN S-GCN framework. Importantly, since the underlying scalar functions (kernels) are not complex-differentiable, the construction falls outside the holomorphic functional calculus classical spectral filters rely on (Higham, 2008), introducing a new class of spectral graph convolutions. 

Contributions. (1) We propose the spectral conjugate, rather than the transpose, to split the di-rected Laplacian Singh et al. (2016) into dissipative and non-dissipative components while preserv-ing shift invariance. (2) We construct the sum and ratio filters from the resulting components, prove they are both non-holomorphic, and derive their vertex-domain forms via the Cauchy-Pompeiu for-mula. (3) We prove that on the directed cycle the ratio filter is an IIR filter with global impulse response, and establish a reach gap against every degree-K polynomial filter in the shift. (4) We reparameterize both filters in Chebyshev bases, yielding stable vertex-domain layers with real coef-ficients. (5) T WIN S-GCN solves graph transfer tasks at a fraction of the depth conventional poly-nomial filters require, preserves feature distinctiveness with depth, and is competitive with state-of-the-art GCNs on general benchmarks for node classification.

## 4 RELATED WORKS 

Spectral graph convolutional networks. FaberNet (HoloNet) (Koke & Cremers, 2024) performs spectral filtering on directed graphs by approximating holomorphic functions through Faber poly-nomials (Coleman & Smith, 1987; Ellacott, 1983), outperforming MagNet (Zhang et al., 2021), a directed GCN built on the Magnetic Laplacian (Furutani et al., 2020). Both constructs filter directly from a graph operator without separating the mechanisms governing propagation, and in particular without the dissipative and asymmetric split of equation 5. This is reflected in the function class they span: being holomorphic, neither can represent the kernels of Theorem 2. Dir-GNN (Rossi et al., 2024) aggregates separately over in- and out-neighborhoods with distinct weight matrices. Since

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced T WIN S-GCN, a model built upon the spectral conjugate split of the shift. We show that the resulting components and induced filters span a novel class of filters with kernels outside ones reachable by the holomorphic class underlying classical spectral convolutions. We illustrate T WIN S-GCN’s capability to preserve feature distinctiveness with far-reaching receptive fields through examples and the graph transfer task. T WIN S-GCN also shows on real benchmarks competitive accuracy for node classification. 

Outlooks and limitations. Although the transpose is not suitable for a shift-invariant split, is in-expensive, well conditioned, and its Cartesian components have spectra distinct from L, which may add expressivity. Performing our splits on both L and LT could thus improve accuracy at the cost of interpretability. Likewise, the antisymmetric weight parameterizations act on the channel-mixing matrices and are tangent to our operator-level construction. They can nevertheless be combined with our approach. Computationally, the spectral conjugate requires a Schur decomposition, a one-time cost per graph that nonetheless restricts the present implementation to moderate-size graphs. C.1 SPECTRAL CONJUGATE DEFINITION 

Definition C.1 (Spectral conjugate) . Let A ∈ Cn×n and consider its Jordan canonical form A =

P(Λ + N)P−1, where Λ is diagonal and N is nilpotent, i.e., there exists k ∈ N s.t. Nk = 0. Then spectral conjugate A♯ is defined as 

A♯ = PΛP −1, (63) where Λ is the complex conjugate of Λ.Interestingly enough, the spectral conjugate A♯ is different from the Hermitian conjugate AH =

AT , which is defined as the complex conjugate of the transpose of A. The spectral conjugate A♯

preserves the eigenvectors and generalized eigenvectors of A, while only taking the complex conju-gate of its eigenvalues. In contrast, the Hermitian conjugate AH does not preserve the eigenvectors of A in general. 

Satisfying properties. Let τ : C → C be the map τ (z) = z, and functional extension to A ∈

Cn×n as τ (A) = A♯. Then the spectral conjugate satisfies the following properties: • τ (A + B) = τ (A) + τ (B), if A and B commute, • τ (A)B = B τ (A), if A and B commute, • τ (αA) = α τ (A), for any α ∈ C,• τ (PAP −1) = P τ (A) P−1, for any invertible matrix P.

Computation. In practice A♯ is computed from the complex Schur decomposition A = QTQ H ,with T upper triangular. The diagonal is set by conjugating the eigenvalues, Fii = Tii , and the strictly upper-triangular entries are filled in super-diagonal by super-diagonal using the Parlett re-currence, which enforces the commutation FT = TF (Parlett, 1976). For clustered or repeated eigenvalues the scalar recurrence is ill-conditioned and the blocked variant is used instead, with each diagonal block set to μI for the cluster mean μ and the off-diagonal blocks recovered from Sylvester equations (Davies & Higham, 2003; Higham, 2008). Recomposing, A♯ = QFQ H , yields the spectral conjugate without forming the Jordan decomposition explicitly. C.2 DRAZIN INVERSE DEFINITION

(2026a), crucial differences include that our framework applies to non-diagonalizable operators, provide foundations in non-holomorphic calculus, and ad-dress problems of oversmoothing and receptive field reach. In particular, we study the target reconstruction error of the different filters (equation 8) underlying T WIN S-GCN, as their behavior determines the receptive field of the model.
