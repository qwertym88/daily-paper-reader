# RoboMMT: Learning Stable Tracking of Modulated Expressive and Interactive Robot Motion 

Till Hielscher and Kai O. Arras 

Abstract — Expressive robot motion can improve naturalness, effectiveness, and perceived likability in human-robot interac-tion. When deployed on dynamically unstable platforms, how-ever, reactive execution of expressive body motion may bring the robot to its stability limits, especially when expressivity is decoupled from physical control. In this paper, we present RoboMMT (Robot Modulated Motion Tracking), a modular control architecture that couples parameterized expressive mo-tion synthesis, physics-based character control, and reactive adaptation to spatial interaction goals. Our approach trains a reference-conditioned tracking policy across a broad domain of expressivity, enabling it to faithfully realize the commanded motions while maintaining dynamic stability. Goal reactivity is achieved by training lightweight, task-specific interaction policies that modulate the reference generator’s spatial goal-attraction point, with the stabilizing tracking policy kept in the loop. Evaluations on a quadruped and two humanoid robots show that the learned tracker remains stable and accurate across the entire modulation domain, whereas trackers trained without this coverage lose balance, tracking accuracy, or both. We show that layering closed-loop goal adaptation over this foundation enables interactive facing and pointing behaviors, while maintaining stable execution and comparable reference-tracking fidelity. The robot learns to proactively redistribute its weight and take corrective steps. Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is demonstrated through real-robot experiments on a quadruped under heavy disturbances. 

I. INTRODUCTION Robots operating in human environments require both verbal and non-verbal channels for social communication, particularly through body language. Beyond executing their primary tasks, these robots must convey social signals to communicate intent or affect. Depending on the context, they need mechanisms to stylize their body language by modulating timing, extent, or motion dynamics in response to how the interaction unfolds [1], [2], [3]. Here, we address the challenge of jointly executing expressive body language while maintaining stability and physical feasibility under complex platform dynamics. Recent advances in character animation use physics-based control through reinforcement learning to enable robots to reproduce complex, expressive reference motions while ac-counting for modeled system dynamics [4], [5]. This way, behaviors authored for virtual characters can be realized on physical platforms [6], [7]. Other lines of work propose motion representations that make stylization directly and in-terpretably controllable. Rather than specifying expressivity

A. Expressive and Interactive Robot Motion 

Generating expressive robot motion across humanoid and non-humanoid systems typically relies on manual keyfram-ing, classic animation heuristics, or retargeted motion capture [12], [13], [1], [2], [3]. Despite communicating affect and intent effectively, these authoring pipelines remain inherently bottlenecked, where every individual expressive behavior requires dedicated, time-consuming manual specification. Parameterized motion representations capture expressive variation through a reduced set of variables. Laban Effort fea-tures can specify expressive qualities while preserving task motion, and related approaches parameterize kinematic and temporal properties like velocity, spatial extent, and timing [14], [8], [15], [16]. Beyond parameterizing descriptive traits, representations built on mathematically integrated animation principles allow systems to automatically and interactively modulate expressive motion [9]. Learning-based methods offer another avenue by extracting latent style spaces directly from motion datasets [17]. Although these methods vary in their underlying structures, each uses parameterized expres-sivity to produce a variety of styles. Compared to latent representations, explicit parameterization keeps variables in-terpretable so designers and high-level controllers can adjust them directly. Interactive robots must also couple this expressivity to the dynamic state of nearby humans. Social and movement-based platforms typically achieve this by directing attention through gaze or body orientation [18], regulating interpersonal dis-tance and spatial layout [19], and adapting action timing to human behavior [20]. These capabilities highlight how responsiveness and behavioral contingency influence the per-ceived quality of interaction [21], [22]. Recent systems build on these insights by explicitly adapting behavior based on human observations, which includes generating interaction-driven control commands [23] and using context to select and modulate expressive movement parameters [10]. However, these interactive and parameter-driven methods predominantly operate at the kinematic level. Rapidly mod-ulating expressive references online provides no guarantees of physical feasibility on dynamic platforms, often inducing balance loss or actuator saturation. Bridging this gap requires decoupling interactive parameter adjustment from low-level physical tracking. 

B. Physics-Based Character Animation and Robotic Char-acters 

Physics-based character animation translates kinematically authored trajectories into physical execution by learning policies that imitate reference motions under simulated dy-namics. Deep reinforcement learning has advanced these capabilities through explicit tracking rewards [4], adversarial motion priors [24], reusable skill embeddings [25], and masked reference completion [26]. Hierarchical two-layer architectures further decouple high-level reference generation from low-level physical control [11], drawing from large-scale motion capture datasets to supply target behaviors [27]. While dataset-driven approaches thrive on humanoid plat-forms that closely resemble human morphology [28], [29], [5], non-humanoid robotic characters lack comparable mo-tion repositories. Consequently, research on non-humanoid characters emphasizes morphology-independent policy learn-ing, imitation control, and physics-aware retargeting across diverse robot structures [4], [30]. On these platforms, care-fully authored physical traits and reference trajectories define the character’s expressivity and identity [6], [7], leaving the learned controller to realize these predefined behaviors under real-world dynamics. More recently, runtime adaptation has been introduced directly into physics-based controllers. For instance, Hopkins et al. [31] expose continuous style controls for interactive gait design, Alegre et al. [32] condition tracking controllers on shifting multi-objective preferences at inference, and Kobayashi et al. [33] inject handcrafted emotion parameters into locomotion policies. While these developments show that stable physics-based controllers can accommodate behavioral variations beyond a single static motion, current adaptation mechanisms remain bound to uninterpretable latent spaces and scalar preference weights, or narrow locomotion styles. In contrast, our work grounds controller execution in a broad, interpretable DMP parameter space, coupling stable physical tracking with closed-loop goal adaptation for interactive robotic characters. METHOD OVERVIEW Execution of interactive body language requires a control architecture that preserves dynamic balance without com-promising expressive motion style. As discussed in Sec. II, existing kinematic motion generators enable continuous style modulation but lack physical awareness, whereas traditional tracking controllers maintain balance by smoothing out styl-ized nuances. We bridge this gap through a three-stage hierarchical pipeline (Fig. 1). First, a DMP-based reference stream generator to produce continuous motion with inter-pretable expressive variation (Sec. III-A). Second, a single reference-conditioned policy that executes these trajectories under complex platform dynamics, retaining the kinematic characteristics encoded by the modulated reference (Sec. V). Finally, closed-loop goal adaptation modules that dynami-cally update task targets, enabling real-time reactivity with-out destabilizing low-level balance (Sec. VI). Throughout, we assume that expressive motion, including its communicative purpose and its generation, is an input to our method, and not a learning objective. Training proceeds in two phases (Fig. 2). In Phase 1, the tracking policy is explicitly trained over the reference domain induced by the motion library and bounded mod-ulation parameters, including spatial goals pGoal used by downstream interaction policies. This exposes the controller during training to the same classes of reference variation that it will encounter when modulation is applied online (Sec. V). Providing the tracking policy with only the kinematic ref-erence trajectory qref  

> t

rather than the explicit modulation vector mt keeps stylistic representation strictly within the generator layer while conditioning the controller to maintain balance across upstream style shifts. In Phase 2, the tracking policy is frozen, and task-specific modulators are trained to map live interaction states to spatial goal adjustments (Sec. VI). Because these goal modulators act directly on the kinematic reference generator, they require no dynamics model. Consequently, adapting the system to new interaction tasks requires training only a lightweight task modulator rather than retraining the underlying physical tracking policy. V. VII. IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING All training is conducted in the Newton physics engine (v1.2.1) on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090, with PD gains and λ j set per character. Settings for the optimization and training setup are given in Table I. The reference domain 

Dref is synthesized offline for rollout efficiency in the paral-lelized setting. Each stream switches primitive and modula-tion several times within it, drawing pExa , pTime ∈ [0.5, 2.0],

pArc ∈ [−3, 3], pAnt , pFollow ∈ [0, 0.5] and a per-dimension goal shift pGoal ∈ [−0.5, 0.5] rad. The training of interactive goal modulation cannot use a precomputed domain, since ∆gt

makes the reference state-dependent. The DMP is therefore integrated into the environment step and batched on the GPU, and unless a fall occurs, new episodes are started without resetting the environment, leaving the robot and DMP phase uninterrupted. 

A. Sim-to-Real Configuration 

Transfer to a physical platform introduces two sources of mismatch that are absent from the simulated characters: actuator saturation and system latency. Both are modeled inside the training environment rather than compensated for at runtime. Torque is constrained to the platform’s per-joint limits within the physics solver, so the constraint is enforced at every substep. Latency is applied as an integer step delay, 0–2 steps on the proprioceptive observation and 0–1 step on the commanded targets, resampled per environment to obtain robustness over this range. The reference window and the action history are excluded, being computed onboard within the same step. Domain randomization is broadened and extended to include pushes of up to 1 .0 m /s at random intervals. VIII. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS We evaluate the system across three morphologically dis-tinct platforms to address three core questions. First, we investigate whether a single tracking policy per platform reliably executes the specified bounded modulation domain (Sec. VIII-C). Second, we assess whether goal modulators generate reactive behaviors when layered over the frozen tracker (Sec. VIII-D). Finally, we evaluate qualitative per-formance across both simulation and physical hardware (Sec. VIII-E).

D. Interactive Goal Modulation 

Closed-loop goal modulation, evaluated over 256 ran-domly sampled environments with 1000 steps, enables online spatial adaptation while maintaining stable execution across the evaluated facing and pointing tasks (Table II), greatly reducing task errors compared to execution without goal modulation. The same trends are observable when comparing against the ablated tracking policy exposed to the narrow training domain. Here specifically, the complex task formu-lation for the Go2 Pointing stands out (9 .69 vs 42 .10). Task complexity also separates the two trackers. On the humanoid tasks, the narrow-domain tracker reports the lower joint error, as in Sec. VIII-C. These tasks modulate free upper-body chains and remain close to the unmodulated references it was trained on. Go2 Pointing instead shifts joints that carry the robot. Such deviations lie outside its training domain, and it loses balance, producing the only survival below 100 % observed under interaction. With qualitative results in Fig. 4, we demonstrate that the tracking policy enables stable execution during both low and high energy modulation, where the executed plain reference stream fails. Analyzing the movements reveals that stability is generally induced by shifting body mass or adapting ground contact patterns. For interactive behavior, we show how the robot’s pose is adapted to fulfill the spatial interaction task, compared to the plain execution of the reference (Fig. 5). We further deploy both the tracking policy and goal-modulation zero-shot on the physical Go2 using the configuration of Sec. VII-A. Qualitatively, we demonstrate modulated interaction sequences with varying expressivity covering the domain, targeted towards a human interaction partner, with the goal modulator toggled on and off during execution. In a representative long-running hardware execu-tion exceeding 90 s, comprising 20 concatenated primitives and externally induced disturbances, the robot completed the sequence without falling across 5 trials. IX. CONCLUSION We presented RoboMMT , a modular architecture for ex-ecuting expressive body language on dynamically unstable robots. By training a single reference-conditioned tracking policy over the reference domain Dref induced by a param-eterized DMP motion representation, the controller realizes continuously modulated expressive motion while preserving balance. Layering lightweight goal-modulation policies on top of the frozen tracker adds closed-loop spatial reactivity without altering the tracking objective or requiring a dy-namics model. Across a quadruped and two humanoids, the tracker completed at least 99 .3 % of streams sampled from the full modulation domain, whereas an otherwise identical policy trained only on unmodulated references dropped to 50 .0–70 .1 %, with failures concentrated at high-energy mod-ulation. Two general-purpose whole-body trackers driven by the same stream likewise degraded, indicating that explicit coverage of the target modulation domain matters for robust execution. Goal modulators reduced facing and pointing errors substantially at comparable tracking fidelity, and the complete system transferred zero-shot to a physical Go2, sustaining a 90 s sequence of 20 concatenated primitives under external disturbances.
