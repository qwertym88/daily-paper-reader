## Abstract 

Reinforcement learning for stochastic systems typically optimizes expected cumulative re-wards, while reliable and repeatable behavior also depends on how the state distribution evolves over time. Optimizing expected return alone does not guarantee that this distri-bution converges to a desired target or remains stable. This paper develops a theoretical framework for learning policies that stabilize state distributions. We formulate the problem as a Dissimilarity-based Markov Decision Process (DMDP), in which the policy acts on the current state distribution and the objective measures its dissimilarity to a target distribution. We establish sufficient conditions under which the optimal policy makes the target distribu-tion asymptotically stable. To support policy learning, we introduce a finite-dimensional representation of distributions and show how stability guarantees for its parameter dynamics extend to the underlying state distributions. We further develop certificates for the learned policies, including their deployment using estimated distribution parameters. These results quantify how learning and estimation errors affect the guarantees and identify conditions under which learned policies retain asymptotic stability. For practical implementation, we combine distribution estimates and target-dependent rewards with PPO and SAC. A computable example on a linear system illustrates the proposed theoretical certificates. Furthermore, experiments on a HalfCheetah task demonstrate the benefits of distribution feedback and explicit target matching for regulating the velocity distribution.

1arXiv:2610.29024v1 [cs.LG] 21 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning for stochastic systems raises a stability question beyond the optimization of expected return: under what conditions does a learned policy keep the evolving state distribution close to a desired distribution, for example, a distribution concentrated on a high-reward region, and drive it toward that target? This question matters when the desired behavior concerns the variability and concentration of states across repeated executions. For example, a robot’s mean velocity alone does not determine how often it exceeds a prescribed velocity limit. Conventional Markov decision processes (MDPs) and reinforcement learning optimize expected cumulative rewards or costs (Puterman, 1994; Sutton & Barto, 2018), but the resulting expected return can leave important features of individual trajectories unresolved (Baumann et al., 2025). Moreover, even convergence of an expected stage cost need not imply convergence of the state distribution: distinct distributions can have the same expected cost while the closed-loop distribution continues to oscillate. A distributional stability guarantee therefore requires conditions that connect the learning objective and the feedback policy to the evolution of the distribution itself. Several research directions address parts of this problem. Measure-functional MDPs formulate costs and stability on the space of probability measures, while mean-field formulations incorporate distributions into the decision state. Dissipativity and turnpike notions have been extended to random variables and probability measures (Schießl et al., 2025a;b) and yield closed-loop stability and performance guarantees for stochastic economic model predictive control (Schießl et al., 2024; 2025c). Lyapunov-based learning control provides certificates for learned policies, and distribution steering specifies a desired terminal distribution. Distributional reinforcement learning models the distribution of returns, while state-distribution objectives concern distributions of visited states. These perspectives motivate a more specific theoretical objective: to connect feedback from the current state distribution with infinite-horizon distributional stability, and to characterize when this connection survives learning and estimation errors. Table 1 summarizes the relationship to the cited approaches along six criteria. The first three concern a stage cost that depends nonlinearly on the state distribution (distribution cost), a policy that acts on the distribution or its parameterization (distribution feedback), and a distributional stability notion with sufficient conditions (distributional stability). The remaining criteria concern certificates for learned policies under learning, model, and estimation errors (certified learned policies), applicability to nonlinear dynamics, and an infinite-horizon objective or stability property. Under the definitions detailed in Appendix B, the DMDP framework combines all six properties. Its central contribution is the accompanying theory connecting distribution feedback, optimal-policy stability, and certificates for policies obtained from data. We formulate this problem as a Dissimilarity-based MDP (DMDP). The decision state is the current probability measure, the policy maps that measure to an action, and the stage cost can directly quantify its dissimilarity to a target distribution. For a fixed transition kernel, the distribution evolves deterministically once the current distribution and action are specified. This observation makes it possible to analyze distributional stabilization through deterministic dynamics on the space of probability measures. The resulting problem nevertheless presents two difficulties: its state space is infinite-dimensional, and both the distribution and its evolution must generally be inferred from finite data. Our theoretical development addresses these difficulties through finite-dimensional representations and Lyapunov certificates. The state distribution ρk at time k is represented by a member ρωk of a parameterized family with a finite-dimensional parameter ωk. The deterministic dynamics of ωk are exact when the family is closed under the controlled transition and approximate otherwise. A compatibility condition between parameter distance and distributional dissimilarity then transfers stability from the parameter dynamics to the induced distributions. For learned policies, the key issue is whether a certified decrease survives the errors introduced by learning and deployment. Our results express this relationship through inequalities of the form 

W (ωk+1 ) − W (ωk) ≤ − σ(∥ωk − ω⋆∥) + ∆, where W is a Lyapunov function on the parameter space, ω⋆ is the parameter of the target distribution, σ is a class-K decrease margin, and ∆ collects the relevant error bounds. This inequality identifies where the decrease remains certified. Asymptotic distributional stability follows when the residual is zero or when state-dependent errors leave a strictly positive decrease margin away from the target. With nonzero uniform residuals, the guarantee is a Lyapunov decrease outside an explicitly characterized residual set. ## 2 Dissimilarity-Based MDPs 

This section introduces the Dissimilarity-based MDP and a notion of stability defined on the space of probability measures. Section 2.1 defines the distribution dynamics, the dissimilarity measure, the resulting stability notion, and the associated optimal control problem; Section 2.2 gives a sufficient condition under which an optimal policy renders the DMDP D-asymptotically stable; and Section 2.3 explains why Problem OCP is not directly solvable in practice. 

2.1 State-Distribution Dynamics and Dissimilarity Measures

## 4 Stability Certification for Learned Policies 

The results of the preceding section concern policies and value functions for the exact parameter dynamics. Here, we study how approximation and estimation errors affect their Lyapunov certificates. Section 4.1 transfers a decrease condition computed with an approximate dynamics model to the true dynamics. Section 4.2 then accounts for deployment with estimated distribution parameters. Section 4.3 gives a complementary certificate based on an estimated Lyapunov decrease. These results concern the information available for certification and do not prescribe a particular policy-learning algorithm or data-collection schedule. Section 4.4 studies the corresponding limiting behavior, and Section 4.5 describes the practical implementation settings. 4.5 Practical Implementation 

The experiments train PPO and SAC using distribution estimates as policy inputs, with three departures from the idealized problem ( OCP ). ## 6 Conclusion 

This paper introduced Dissimilarity-based Markov Decision Processes, which control the distribution of the state of a stochastic system rather than a single state: the policy acts on the state distribution, and the stage cost is its dissimilarity to a target distribution. A finite-dimensional parameterization turns this problem into the control of deterministic parameter dynamics, for which Lyapunov certificates were derived for the optimal policy, for policies learned offline with a model of the parameter dynamics, for their online deployment with estimated parameters, and for policies whose Lyapunov decrease is estimated from transition data; in the scalar linear example of Appendix D, these certificates hold wherever they are checked. ## B Technical Comparison with Related Approaches 

This appendix details the comparison in Table 1. A stage cost, objective, or constraint counts as a distribution cost only if it depends nonlinearly on the distribution of the state or of state-action pairs, not merely through the expectation of a per-state cost or through the return distribution. A policy uses distribution feedback only if it is evaluated at the current distribution, or at a finite-dimensional parameter of it, rather than at a single state or through a time-indexed law computed offline for a fixed initial distribution. The assumed ergodicity of a fixed Markov chain and the convergence of learning iterates, such as those of distributional reinforcement learning algorithms (Rowland et al., 2018; Antonetti et al., 2026), do not establish distributional stability. Optimality, regret, and sample-complexity guarantees do not certify learned policies.

B.2 Relation to POMDPs and Belief-State MDPs 

A POMDP agent is commonly decomposed into a state estimator, which updates a belief state from the previous belief, the executed action, and the new observation, and a policy, which maps the belief state to an action (Åström, 1965; Kaelbling et al., 1998). The belief state is a probability distribution over hidden states and a sufficient statistic of the past action-observation history. This view underlies belief-dependent rewards (Araya-López et al., 2010), belief-space planning (Platt et al., 2010), and belief-like representations learned in deep reinforcement learning (Igl et al., 2018; Lambrechts et al., 2022; Luis et al., 2025). In the parameterized DMDP of Problem P-OCP , the parameter ωk represents ρk through ρωk , and the policy 

ϕ : Ω → A acts on this representation, much as a POMDP policy acts on a belief state. However, the two distributions differ intrinsically. In a POMDP, the belief state represents epistemic uncertainty about an unobserved physical state, or about unknown model parameters included in the state, and serves as an information state for maximizing the expected return. It remains a pathwise quantity, i.e., it is computed from the observations of a single realization, and the belief-state MDP is stochastic because the belief depends on future random observations. In the DMDP, ρk is the distribution of the state across realizations rather than a pathwise quantity, the action is common to all realizations, and ρk evolves deterministically through the distribution transition operator in (1). This distributional state results from the stage cost, as Appendix B.3 explains, rather than from missing information, and ρk is the controlled object, which the policy stabilizes toward the target distribution ρ⋆ in the sense of D(ρk∥ρ⋆). 31 B.3 Relation to Measure-Functional MDPs 

Measure-functional MDPs (Gros & Zanon, 2022; Kordabad & Gros, 2022) analyze the stability of stochastic MDPs on the space of probability measures rather than solely on the state space. There, a state-feedback law 

ak = π(sk) induces the measure sequence ρk+1 = Tτ,π ρk, stage costs and storage functions are generalized to functionals of probability measures, and stability is characterized through a dissimilarity measure. State feedback keeps these works within the standard MDP setting; it is a modeling choice rather than a restriction of their dissipativity theory, and Table 1 therefore marks distribution feedback with ( ✓). Whether state feedback suffices is decided by the stage cost: for an expected per-state cost, which is linear in the distribution, an optimal state-feedback policy exists under standard conditions (Puterman, 1994; Hernández-Lerma & Lasserre, 1996), whereas a stage cost such as D(ρk∥ρ⋆) generally makes the optimal action depend on the whole distribution, e.g., on its variance, concentration, tail behavior, or multimodality. Distribution feedback thus follows from the DMDP stage cost, not from a design preference, which is why the DMDP takes ρk as the decision state of the policy ak = ψ(ρk), or ak = ϕ(ωk) in the parameterized formulation. Conversely, as formalized in Corollary 1, an admissible DMDP policy that represents the stabilizing dynamics Tτ,π on M (S)induced by a state-feedback policy π reproduces the same trajectory of probability measures. Dissipativity and turnpike notions have also been extended to random variables and probability measures for stochastic nonlinear optimal control (Schießl et al., 2025a) and, with a stationary stochastic process in place of the optimal steady state, for generalized linear-quadratic problems (Schießl et al., 2025b); for stochastic economic MPC, they yield closed-loop stability and performance guarantees (Schießl et al., 2024; 2025c). These works analyze optimal solutions and MPC closed loops; the DMDP uses dissipativity through the cost rotation in Section 3.2, and Section 4 extends the Lyapunov certificates of Section 3.3 to learned distribution-feedback policies. 

B.4 Relation to Distributional Reinforcement Learning 

Distributional reinforcement learning (Bellemare et al., 2017; 2023) usually models the distribution of the random return rather than only its expectation; this richer representation of long-term reward uncertainty can improve value learning or risk-sensitive decision-making (Dabney et al., 2018a). The DMDP instead models and controls the distribution ρk of the system state rather than a representation of the value function or return, with the stability objective D(ρk∥ρ⋆) → 0. This distinction matters when reliability, safety, or personalization must be specified at the distribution level: two policies may have similar expected costs or return distributions while inducing very different state distributions, with different confidence regions, tail risks, or multimodal behaviors, and the DMDP captures these differences directly. 

B.5 Further Related Approaches 

Lyapunov functions of the state have been used to certify the stability or safety of learned policies (Perkins & Barto, 2002; Berkenkamp et al., 2017; Chow et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2019; Han et al., 2020), and Buşoniu et al. (2018) and Krasowski et al. (2023) survey stability and provable safety in reinforcement learning. These certificates concern the realized state rather than its distribution. Mean-field MDPs have the distribution of a large population of agents as their state (Motte & Pham, 2022; Bäuerle, 2023), and reinforcement learning methods for mean-field control take this population distribution as a policy input (Carmona et al., 2023; Pásztor et al., 2023). These methods do not establish its stability. Distribution steering drives the state distribution of a linear stochastic system to a prescribed Gaussian distribution at a finite final time, either as a terminal constraint (Chen et al., 2016) or through a Wasserstein terminal cost (Balci & Bakolas, 2021). Optimal transport has also been used to coordinate large populations of controlled agents (Le Corre et al., 2026). Other objectives are defined on the distribution of the states, or state-action pairs, visited by a policy and aggregated over time, such as the entropy of the state-visitation distribution (Hazan et al., 2019), the matching of a target state marginal distribution (Lee et al., 2019), and convex functions of the stationary state-action distribution (Zahavy et al., 2021). 32 C Distribution Estimation and Model Consistency

The stability distance compares estimates of ρk and ˆ ρ from different halves of the evaluation episodes and is averaged over both assignments of the halves. The stationary reference value is the mean stability distance for k ≥ 500 of 10 surrogates with independent random circular shifts of the segment k ≥ 500 of every episode. The ratio of the stability distance to this value is near 1 for a stationary ρk and well above 1 if ρk drifts or the run is synchronized. A run is synchronized, i.e., its episodes keep a common phase, if the standard deviation over k ≥ 500 of the mean velocity across its episodes exceeds twice the standard error of a mean of 64 independent velocities.
