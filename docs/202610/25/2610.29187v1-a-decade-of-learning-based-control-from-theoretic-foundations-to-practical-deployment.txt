# A Decade of Learning-Based Control: From Theoretic Foundations to Practical Deployment 

Angela P. Schoellig 1, Matthias A. M¨ uller 2, Sebastian Trimpe 3, Melanie N. Zeilinger 4,Mahathi Anand 1, Andrea Carron 4, Oliver Hausd¨ orfer 1,Victor G. Lopez 2, Friedrich Solowjow 3, Haocheng Zhao 1, and Siqi Zhou 5

Abstract — Motivated by ten years of Learning-Based Control Invited Sessions at the IEEE Conference on Decision and Control (CDC), this tutorial paper reviews key advances at the intersection of machine learning and control. We focus on meth-ods that combine the flexibility of machine learning techniques with the structure and theoretical foundations of control to design autonomous systems that are capable, reliable, and safe. We first introduce core notions for establishing stability and safety guarantees, then review approaches that learn dynamics models to improve control under uncertainty, and finally discuss data-driven methods that directly synthesize or learn policies, including reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and Bayesian optimization. We further discuss representative applications and open challenges. The goal is to provide an accessible entry point to the field and to support continued progress toward reliable learning-based control in real-world systems. To support learning and practical exploration, we share implementations and illustrative examples along with our discussion, together with additional resources on our website: https://learning-based-control.github.io/tutorial/. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Over the past decade, the integration of machine learning and control theory has evolved from a growing research in-terest into a practical approach for many real-world systems. Machine learning provides powerful tools for extracting pat-terns from data, while control theory offers the mathematical foundation needed to use such data-driven methods in closed-loop systems. Principled combinations of these perspectives can extend autonomous systems to more challenging oper-ating domains, broader system classes, and more complex tasks, while preserving the closed-loop properties required for reliable deployment.

Why control? The flexibility that makes machine learning powerful also introduces challenges when learning-based methods are deployed in physical systems. Purely data-driven models can be highly expressive, but often struggle to satisfy strict safety and reliability requirements, and may require large amounts of data that are costly or infeasible to collect on real systems. Control provides structural priors and theoretical tools for bridging this gap. By embedding control principles into the learning design, one can guide the learning process toward methods that are more sample efficient, high-performing, and safe for practical applications [11]–[14]. The use of data to model the unknown has long been central to control, for instance, in system identification and adaptive control. However, the scale, expressiveness, and opacity of modern learning methods create new challenges in sample efficiency, verification, and reliable deployment. Where do these fields converge? To foster principled ap-proaches that integrate machine learning and control theory beyond the standard tools of either discipline alone, the Invited Session Series on Learning-Based Control has been held at the IEEE Conference on Decision and Control (CDC) since 2016 1. Over the past decade, this series has hosted 30 dedicated sessions featuring 182 accepted papers, drawing contributions from over 100 distinct institutions. As reflected in Figure 2, the series captures the increasing incorpora-tion of expressive learning paradigms into control, while maintaining a strong emphasis on principled methods with desired closed-loop properties. Recurring themes include (deep) neural networks, (deep) RL, and Gaussian processes (GPs), combined with control tools such as model predictive control (MPC), Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability, control Lyapunov functions (CLFs), and control barrier functions (CBFs) to address performance, stability, and safety. The steady growth in citations over the past decade (Figure 3) further reflects the sustained interest in these topics and the expanding body of work contributed by the community. This ten-year milestone of the CDC Learning-Based Con-trol Invited Sessions does not exist in isolation. The broader effort to bridge control and learning for safe and scalable autonomy is also reflected in dedicated conferences such as the Learning for Dynamics and Control Conference, as well as in the increasing presence of learning-based methods in core control conferences, as shown in Figure 4. In this tutorial paper, we highlight key developments and emerging trends in learning-based control over the past decade, spanning control-theoretic foundations, dynamics learning, direct data-driven control, and learning-oriented policy optimization (see Figure 1). III. S TABILITY AND SAFETY 

We begin by introducing the control-theoretic foundations that provide the desired closed-loop properties of stability and constraint satisfaction. We first review these concepts in the nominal setting, in which the dynamics are known ex-actly, and then extend the discussion to robust and stochastic formulations that explicitly account for uncertainty. The latter settings are particularly relevant in learning-based control, where the dynamics are only approximately known from data and the resulting model error must be incorporated into the analysis either as a bounded disturbance or as a probabilistic uncertainty description. D. Invariance and Safety Filters 

While stability concerns convergence toward equilibria, safety concerns the satisfaction of state and input con-straints (4) over time. Note that constraint satisfaction at a single time instant does not guarantee constraint satisfaction at future times, since there exist states satisfying the con-straints from which a future constraint violation cannot be avoided under the system dynamics. Safety therefore requires identifying sets from which the constraints remain satisfied for all future times. The central notion underlying safety (in terms of constraint satisfaction) is thus invariance. 

Definition 4 (Forward invariance [33]): Consider system (1a) in closed loop with the policy uk = π(xk), and let S ⊆ Rnx . The set S is said to be forward invariant if, for all initial conditions x0 ∈ S , the corresponding trajectory satisfies xk ∈ S for all k ≥ 0.A forward invariant set guarantees that trajectories initial-ized within it remain inside for all future times; safety then follows whenever such a set lies within the constraints. When the input is a decision variable, the relevant notion is control invariance, i.e., for every x ∈ S , the existence of an input u

satisfying (4) and keeping the state in S.Invariant sets can either be integrated directly as con-straints in the control problem, as e.g., common practice in MPC, or can be used to define a safety filter. Safety filters are independent of the control policy and merely verify the safety of a proposed control action, and otherwise implement a safety-ensuring modification. We focus on the safety filter formulation in the following. For a tutorial overview of common safety filter formulations and their data-driven extensions, see also e.g., [20]. Classical approaches to safety characterize invariant sets through reachability analysis. In particular, HJ reachability methods [13] compute the set of states from which trajec-tories can be kept within the constraints under the system dynamics by propagating backward the set of constraint-violating states. Such methods provide a global character-ization of safety and typically yield tight approximations of the safe set under the assumed dynamics. However, their computational complexity scales poorly with the dimension of the state space, which limits their applicability to high-dimensional systems and has motivated various approximate and learning-based extensions [13], [20], [34]. An alternative approach relies on CBFs [14], which en-force safety locally online through tractable conditions on the closed-loop dynamics. Analogously to Lyapunov func-tions for stability, barrier functions certify safety through conditions that guarantee forward invariance of a safe set. Consider a safe set of the form 

S := {x ∈ Rnx : b(x) ≥ 0},

where b : Rnx → R. In discrete time, given a valid CBF, one can impose conditions on the control input such that 

b(f (xk, u k)) − b(xk) ≥ − α(b(xk)) 

for some α ∈ K satisfying α(r) ≤ r for all r ≥ 0.This condition ensures that trajectories cannot leave the safe set S, thereby rendering the set forward invariant. In discrete time, the barrier condition directly provides a one-step-ahead safety filter. A common optimization problem is given by

E. Robust and Stochastic Invariance 

The notion of invariance extends to uncertain systems, where it must hold for all admissible disturbances in robust settings [33], and in probability or in expectation in stochas-tic ones [40], [41]. In the robust case, HJ reachability extends to worst-case analysis by computing invariant sets under adversarial dis-turbances [42], [43], robust CBFs incorporate the uncertainty directly into the barrier conditions [44], [45], and PSFs account for it through model-based constraint tightening mechanisms [36], [37]. In the stochastic case, stochastic reachability methods characterize the probability of remain-ing within the safe set [46], barrier-based approaches admit stochastic variants with prescribed confidence levels [47], and PSFs extend through chance-constrained or risk-based optimization formulations [48].

IV. D YNAMICS LEARNING FOR CONTROL 

Many control methods use a dynamics model to synthesize a controller for the problem formulated in Section II and to analyze the resulting closed-loop properties, as described in Section III. The achievable performance and guarantees depend on the accuracy of the model, and on how the associ-ated uncertainty is accounted for. In practice, dynamics mod-els may be constructed from our prior knowledge (e.g., first-principles physical models) for some applications; for others, such models may be unavailable or may not adequately capture complex dynamics. This has motivated a large body of work in learning-based control that incorporates measured data to learn or refine dynamics models, thereby comple-menting incomplete prior knowledge. Within this body of work, the learned models are leveraged to improve control performance, while control-theoretic analysis provides the foundation for retaining desired closed-loop properties. To facilitate our discussion, we distinguish between direct 

and indirect data-driven control. In indirect data-driven con-trol, a system model is distilled based on data collected from the real system, and this model is then used for controller design [72]. Direct data-driven control approaches, on the other hand, map system observations to control actions based on past data, without explicitly defining a model. In this section, we focus on the former and provide an introduction to common dynamics learning approaches, together with how they have been integrated with control frameworks. Methods for direct data-driven control are described in greater depth in Section V. In the following subsections, we first formulate the dynamics learning problem and then review common methodological threads, ranging from linear dynamics learn-ing to deterministic nonlinear dynamics learning, and to probabilistic nonlinear dynamics learning approaches. 

A. Dynamics Learning Problem 

We consider a discrete-time system of the form (1a), for which the dynamics can be partially known or fully unknown. Our goal is to infer a model of the dynamics from a sequence of measured inputs and states of length N ,as defined in (5a), (5b), and (6). For the discussion below, we consider systems subject to additive process noise and approximate the true dynamics with a model ˆf of the form 

ˆf (x, u ) = ˆfn(x, u ) + ˆfl(x, u ), (8) where ˆfn is a nominal model that captures available prior knowledge, and ˆfl is the component to be learned from data. When the system dynamics are fully unknown, the nominal model ˆfn can be set to the zero function. For convenience in the following discussion, we define an augmented vector z =[x⊤, u ⊤]⊤, which is the input to both model components. Over the years, many works have considered dynamics learning problems of the form (8). Beyond incorporating a prior dynamics model, dynamics learning can also leverage structural knowledge of the system [73]–[77] to improve learning efficiency or model accuracy. We categorize the resulting frameworks according to the formulation of the learned model, distinguishing between linear and nonlinear models as well as deterministic and probabilistic formula-tions. The chosen formulation influences how model uncer-tainty can be characterized and accounted for during closed-loop operation. In the following, we provide an overview of representative methods, with particular emphasis on the learning framework, the controller synthesis methodology, and the guarantees that can be provided under possible dynamics uncertainties. A summary of the different learning methods, along with their advantages, disadvantages, com-patible control methods, and the stability/safety guarantees they enable, is given in Table I. Note that the control methods provided in the table are not exhaustive, but are only intended to serve as a starting point for those new to the field.

C. Learning Deterministic Nonlinear Models 

Having introduced linear dynamics learning, we now turn to nonlinear systems. While local linear approximations can be effective in some applications, their accuracy may be limited for systems with strongly nonlinear dynamics. Learning nonlinear dynamics models from data provides a means to capture more complex system behaviour for further improving closed-loop performance. Learning general nonlinear models of the form (8) without any prior structural parameterization can be inefficient. We can often leverage a priori knowledge of the system (e.g., leveraging differential flatness, control-affine structures, or polynomial dynamics) to facilitate the learning process. Beyond standard feedforward neural networks, nonlinear dynamics learning may also employ frameworks such as neural ODEs [93], physics-informed neural networks [94], [95], and neural autoregressive models [96] to incorporate richer dynamical structure, physical knowledge, or temporal dependencies. Nevertheless, recent advances have attempted to address this challenge by exploiting the inherent properties of learned models. One property commonly leveraged in closed-loop analysis is Lipschitz continuity. For feedforward neural networks with a finite number of layers and globally Lipschitz-continuous activation functions, there exists a con-stant L ≥ 0 such that ∥ ˆfl,θ (z) − ˆfl,θ (z′)∥ ≤ L∥z − z′∥,

A representative training-based approach is spectral normalization [103], in which the weight matrix of each layer is rescaled during training to constrain its spectral norm. If the activation functions are 1-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean norm, as in the case of many common activation functions (e.g., ReLU and tanh), then an upper bound on the network Lipschitz constant is given by Qli=0 ∥W i∥2. Beyond spectral normalization and related weight-projection methods, Lipschitz continuity can also be imposed through network architectures that combine norm-constrained linear mappings with gradient-norm-preserving activation functions [106]. Both spectrally normalized networks and Lipschitz-constrained architectures have been integrated with control-theoretic tools, includ-ing Lyapunov-based nonlinear control and small-gain ar-guments, to establish closed-loop stability guarantees for learning-enhanced systems in practical applications [107], [108]. Besides Lipschitz continuity, neural networks can also be characterized through local sector bounds or quadratic constraints on their activation functions; these descriptions preserve more information about the network structure than a single global Lipschitz bound and can be incorporated into Lyapunov- and robust-control analyses (e.g., [109], [110]) to establish desired closed-loop properties. While the preceding approaches are well-suited to ana-lyzing feedforward neural networks as static input-output mappings, many learning approaches utilize autoregressive models, recurrent neural networks, and neural ODEs, which naturally define dynamical models with internal states or temporal dependencies induced by state-input sequences of the form (5a), (5b), and (6). For these models, it is important to analyze how their internal states and output trajectories evolve over time. This can be done by studying properties such as ISS and δ-ISS and using them in the closed-loop analysis to establish convergence of the system trajectories to the desired behaviour or to a bounded neighbourhood thereof in the presence of model mismatch (see Section III). In this regard, sufficient conditions for ISS and δ-ISS properties have been obtained for neural autoregressive models [111] and recurrent neural networks [112], [113]. Related con-tractivity and dissipativity guarantees have been developed for neural ODEs [114], while stable-by-design recurrent net-works are proposed in [115]. These model properties can be incorporated directly into controller analysis and synthesis. For instance, in [97], δ-ISS properties are leveraged for the derivation of MPC laws ensuring stability and recursive feasibility. This has also been extended to offset-free tracking control in [98]. 

D. Learning Probabilistic Nonlinear Models 

Although nonlinear dynamics learning methods are more flexible than their linear counterparts, this flexibility can come at the cost of conservatism due to the assumed worst-case bounds when handling model uncertainty. While methods such as approximate Bayesian inference [116], [117], approximate Cram´ er–Rao lower bound [118], and deep ensembles [119] exist, accurately and reliably esti-mating predictive uncertainty for highly expressive function approximators, such as neural networks, remains a non-trivial problem. E. Data Collection for Offline Versus Online Learning 

The dynamics learning methods discussed in this section are performed predominantly in an offline manner, i.e., a suitable model is learned based on a fixed, input-output dataset, and the control is designed based on the learned model prior to closed-loop deployment. The performance of such methods is tied to the assumption that the collected data is informative enough for estimation. In the machine learning literature, similar challenges have been addressed through active learning methods, which enable systematic data collection by actively querying the most informative data points [137], and such techniques have been successfully integrated into learning-based control frameworks to improve sample efficiency and model accuracy [138]–[140]. In this context, common information criteria include (i) mutual information, which quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about the unknown dynamics from the observations [141], [142], (ii) entropy, which targets the regions in the state space where the system dynamics are the most uncertain [143] and 

(iii) criteria based on the Fisher information matrix [144], including D-optimality, which minimizes the volume of the parameter-confidence ellipsoid, and A-optimality, which minimizes the average parameter variance. In contrast to offline learning, online learning approaches pair closed-loop control with online model adaptation through information acquisition. Although this is not the focus of our discussion, we note that active-learning methods also extend to online settings. L. Moreover, the dimension of α(t) scales linearly with the number N of collected data points. To address these issues, different DDPC variants have been proposed that modify the data-driven prediction model (24b) to reduce the amount of required offline data and/or the number of optimization variables of problem (24), see, e.g., [211]–[213]. In order to study closed-loop properties of DDPC such as stability, safety, and robustness, one can conceptually use the same tools as in classical model-based MPC, in particular adding suitable terminal constraints or using a long enough prediction horizon L. On the other hand, the details in deriving these closed-loop properties are quite different than in model-based MPC, in particular in case of noisy (offline and online) data where the prediction model (24b) is not exact anymore. The first results on closed-loop stability and robustness have appeared in [203]; a recent review on available methods and systems-theoretic guarantees in DDPC can be found in [214]. One major challenge and currently very active line of research is to develop DDPC approaches for nonlinear systems. Existing works exploit extensions of Willems’ lemma for specific system classes and/or knowledge of a set of suitable basis functions such as [215]–[218], or use online data in the Hankel matrices such that the prediction model (24b) serves as a local affine approximation of the nonlinear system [219]. The data is thus typically composed of state (5b), input (5a), and reward data. Different categories of RL emerge depending on what objects are learned and how the search for an optimal policy is structured. We first consider model-free RL, i.e., methods that do not construct a model of the system dynamics as part of solving the RL problem. The main categories within model-free RL are (i) policy-based, (ii) value-function-based, and (iii) actor-critic-based approaches, which we discuss next. Afterwards, we discuss why learning a transition model alongside the policy can nonetheless be beneficial for RL. Policy-based approaches are the closest to our optimiza-tion objective (26) and yield iterative updates of the type

Notable examples include asynchronous advantage actor-critic (A3C) [222], trust-region policy optimization (TRPO) [223], proximal policy optimization (PPO) [224], and twin-delayed deep deterministic policy gradient (TD3) [225]. Actor-critic methods are among the model-free RL ap-proaches that are most suitable for control problems and require very little prior knowledge. At the same time, they are typically extremely data hungry and come without any safety guarantees. Data efficiency is a main motivation for 

model-based RL , which is discussed next. The above mentioned RL approaches generally assume that the learning agent can actively sample the environment, i.e., query it by applying actions and observing the resulting next state and reward. In this sense, they are sometimes referred to as online RL. The environment may be either a simulator or a physical system, such as a robot. In robotics, a common approach is to learn a policy in simulation during the design phase and subsequently deploy it on physical hardware, which often requires techniques such as domain randomization and sim-to-real adaptation to bridge the gap between simulated and real environments [226]. Some approaches instead learn directly from hardware interaction [227] or even adapt the policy during deployment [228]. In contrast, offline RL considers the setting in which the agent learns solely from a fixed dataset of previously collected interactions, without actively querying the environment. This is particularly relevant when interaction with the environment is undesirable or impractical, as it enables learning from historical data alone. A key challenge in offline RL is distribution shift: the learned policy may encounter state-action pairs that are poorly represented in the fixed dataset [229].

Decision-time planning methods leverage the model to improve action selection through on-line look-ahead search. Conceptually, decision-time planning can be viewed as an instance of MPC [238]. In traditional control, dynamics models are typically grounded in physical principles and used to establish theoretical guar-antees, such as stability and safety. In model-based RL, by contrast, models are primarily learned or employed to im-prove sample efficiency. Despite these distinct motivations, the two paradigms exhibit strong conceptual and method-ological overlap, enabling fruitful cross-pollination and the transfer of advances between the fields [239], [240]. In particular, access to a model and the integration of control-theoretic techniques (see Section III) provide a means to incorporate different types of safety guarantees in RL. Depending on the specific notion of safety considered, rep-resentative approaches include the use of soft constraints [241], [242], Lyapunov functions [243], barrier-function-based methods [244], and safety filters that modify or restrict policy actions to ensure constraint satisfaction [233], [234], [245]. For the above methods, we consider full state observations, i.e., the true state xk is assumed to be measured and provided to the policy. In practice, however, one often feeds all available measurements into the policy without explicit knowledge of whether they constitute the full state, and may additionally include historical observations to capture temporal dependencies. Beyond this, there is work that looks at latent state space models (sometimes also called world models ) considering output measurements (1b). This type of approach is particularly relevant when acting on image data and gave rise to the Dreamer architecture [246] and other influential methods such as temporal-difference MPC (TD-MPC) [247]. Safety remains an important consideration in this setting as well [248]. 

C. Imitation Learning 

The previous approaches focus on discovering optimal behaviour through trial-and-error via the reward signal. In-stead, we can learn a policy by observing and mimicking an expert’s behaviour, which results in a supervised learning problem. This expert could, for example, be a human. Behaviour cloning and inverse RL form two major classes of imitation learning methods [249]. In behaviour cloning, we leverage a dataset of state-action pairs D = {(xk, u k)}, sometimes also with an additional context variable. We cast the problem as a supervised learning problem and approximate the policy π

that has created the control inputs via a neural network πθ .Inverse RL, on the other hand, learns the underlying reward function, and we obtain RθR , which can afterwards be used to find an optimal policy. Current active topics, especially in robotics, are large behaviour models [250], vision-language-action models such as π0 [251], and foundation models in general. A core component of training such models is imitation learning, often in combination with RL. The number of parameters of the underlying transformer models ranges easily in the magnitude of billions. As an alternative to learning from humans, the expert could also be a machine or an expensive-to-evaluate policy, such as a sophisticated modern MPC. Essentially, the MPC is used to create state-input data offline, which can be used as training data for a neural network to approximate the MPC via supervised learning. The imitation learning policy is often much faster to evaluate during deployment than the original MPC and with constant runtime, which are core motivations for this approach in practice. This approach of approximate MPC (see [252] for a recent survey) gives rise to research questions on constraint satisfaction despite approximation errors [253]–[257], jointly optimizing the MPC and neural network policy [258], [259], how to approximate set-valued solution maps with generative models [260]–[263], adapting policies to parameter changes [264], [265], or even learning iterative solvers [266], to name just a few. Despite great theoretical efforts, there are only a few applications in hardware, for example, in power electronics [267], robotics [261], [263], [268], [269], or automotive production-grade electronic control units [255]. 

D. Controller Tuning with Bayesian Optimization 

In many practical applications, established controller struc-tures such as PID controllers, LQR, or MPC with predefined cost formulations are often preferred due to their inter-pretability, robustness, or ease of implementation. However, even when the controller structure is fixed, its parameters must still be selected to achieve good performance on the actual system. This process is commonly referred to as controller tuning. At a high level, controller tuning coin-cides with optimization problem (25), where the goal is to identify parameter values that optimize a given perfor-mance objective. A key distinction, however, is that the parameter space in controller tuning is typically much lower-dimensional than the policy parameter spaces considered in deep RL. In many cases, tuning involves only a few to several tens of parameters. This lower dimensionality enables the use of specialized optimization methods that often require substantially less data and may provide stronger theoretical or practical guarantees compared with general-purpose deep RL approaches. Controller tuning is a long-standing problem and many methods exist, including PID auto-tuners [270], extremum seeking control [271], virtual reference feedback con-trol [272], iterative feedback tuning [273], and evolution-ary search [274], among many others. Here, we focus on BO [275], which has gathered substantial momentum over the last decade and matured into a powerful framework for controller tuning [19], [276]. Its sample efficiency and black-box formulation make it particularly attractive for tuning directly on physical systems, where evaluations can be costly and accurate simulation models may be unavailable. At the heart of BO for controller tuning lies the op-timization problem (25). We assume that the function J

is unknown, can be evaluated through closed-loop experi-ments, and represents a control performance measure that depends on controller parameters θ, which are typically low-dimensional. To guide the search for optimal parameters, BO constructs a probabilistic surrogate model of J, usually in the form of a Gaussian process, J ∼ GP (m(θ), k (θ, θ ′)) ,where m(·) denotes the mean and k(·, ·) the covariance function (cf. Section IV-D). Alternatively, other probabilistic machine learning models can be used as surrogates, such as neural networks [277]. Based on the posterior predictions of the surrogate model, an acquisition function α(θ, D),where D contains the past evaluations, determines which controller parameters are evaluated next. It quantifies the utility of candidate points by balancing exploration (e.g., testing regions with high uncertainty) and exploitation (i.e., refining solutions that are predicted to perform well). Depending on the specific problem properties, many dif-ferent approaches have been developed. Important research directions include controllers with higher-dimensional con-troller parameters [278], [279], safety during controller tun-ing [77], [280]–[283], and settings where changes in the system must be detected and the controller retuned accord-ingly [284], [285]. Examples from the CDC Learning-Based Control Invited Sessions span healthcare, robotics and autonomous systems, energy and power sys-tems, process and industrial control, and networked systems. Representative applications include adaptive medication ta-pering [286], autonomous driving and racing [287]–[290], quadrotor control [75], [90], [91], [139], manipulation [135], [291], [292], multiagent coordination [293]–[297], power electronics [298] and energy management [299], process control [97], and wireless sensor networks [300]. Across these domains, learning-based methods have been used to improve prediction under uncertainty, adapt controllers from data, reduce conservatism, and enhance performance while preserving desired closed-loop properties. Robotics has been a particularly prominent application area. Robotic systems expose many of the central chal-lenges in learning-based control, including nonlinear and uncertain dynamics, complex task specifications, limited data availability, real-time computation, perception and actuation uncertainty, and strict safety requirements. They therefore provide useful testbeds for understanding the strengths and limitations of different methodological paradigms, as illus-trated, for example, by existing benchmarks [11], [301]. Robotic applications also illustrate how learning-oriented and control-oriented approaches can complement one an-other in practice. Learning-oriented methods are flexible in two respects. They can optimize policies for complex sequential decision-making tasks, as in RL for autonomous drone racing [10] and quadruped locomotion [9], and they can model poorly known dynamics from data, as in learn-ing terrain-dependent disturbance models for off-road driv-ing [302]. Their performance on real systems, however, depends strongly on the coverage and quality of the training data. Robustification strategies such as domain randomiza-tion can compensate for part of the mismatch between training and deployment conditions, but the task performance sacrificed for this robustness is difficult to characterize in advance. Control-oriented methods, in contrast, exploit prior knowledge of system dynamics and structure, which can im-prove data efficiency and support the analysis of closed-loop properties. For instance, differential flatness can substantially reduce the learning burden for quadrotor control [75] and im-posing Lipschitz regularities informed by control theory can support formal closed-loop stability analysis [107], [108]. Recent work extends this complementarity along three axes: higher-dimensional systems, more expressive policy classes, and richer safety specifications. For instance, control-guided deep RL has scaled from quadrotors, where safety filtering is incorporated into training [231], to humanoids, where CLFs shape rewards for dynamic locomotion [303]. Learned policies have likewise progressed from feedforward networks that approximate a single-valued MPC law [253], [268] to diffusion models that represent multimodal dis-tributions over MPC solutions [260], [261]. Beyond more complex systems and more expressive policies, safety certifi-cations are also expanding beyond geometric constraints such as collision avoidance to semantically-driven commonsense specifications required for unstructured, everyday scenar-ios [304]–[306]. Together, these developments illustrate a common direction: theory lends structure to learning as systems and policies grow more complex, while learning broadens the classes of systems, the range of tasks, and the types of safety specifications that control can accommodate. VIII. C ONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK 

This tutorial paper reviewed key methodological direc-tions in learning-based control that have emerged over the past decade, motivated by the CDC Learning-Based Con-trol Invited Sessions. Across the surveyed approaches, a common theme is that machine learning expands what can be modeled, optimized, and adapted, while control theory provides structure for ensuring stability, safety, robustness, and reliable closed-loop behaviour. As autonomous systems move toward increasingly complex, uncertain and safety-critical real-world settings, incorporating data-based methods directly into the closed loop will be critical for achieving reliable high-performance behaviour. We highlight several directions that we believe have the potential to drive further practical impact.  

> •

Reliably scaling up learning-based control: In this tutorial, we touched upon families of methods that address how uncertainty can be accounted for in learned models and how policies can be optimized without explicit system identification, including through direct data-driven control, RL, IL, and BO (Table I). There are several important directions we did not dive into details but are equally important for reliably scaling these methods to complex real-world problems. For instance, systems must be able to learn online and con-tinually as their dynamics, environments, and objectives change, while avoiding catastrophic forgetting, satisfy-ing real-time computational and memory constraints, and preserving closed-loop guarantees. It is often also desirable to actively identify and explore poorly under-stood components , while acquiring reliable uncertainty estimates under continual updates. Finally, learning-based control can extend beyond the time-invariant, discrete-time systems considered here to hybrid, time-varying, and distributed dynamics and possibly more tightly couple perception, state estimation, learning, and control within a unified optimal decision-making framework. Addressing these challenges jointly will be essential for developing learning-based control systems that are capable and scalable without sacrificing relia-bility required for real-world deployment.

APPENDIX 

The dataset comprises 182 papers from the Learning-Based Control invited sessions at CDC (2016–2025). The paper list and author affiliations were compiled from the session index maintained by ETH Zurich 1, with abstracts retrieved via the OpenAlex API and citation counts from Google Scholar. For Figure 2, a fixed set of 25 domain-relevant concept phrases was determined by an LLM scan of all papers, and searched across each paper’s title and abstract using case-insensitive matching. Semantically related phrases were then manually checked and consolidated into groups (e.g., reinforcement learning, deep reinforcement learning, and safe reinforcement learning are counted jointly). For Figure 13, for each paper, open-source code availability was determined by scraping GitHub and project-page URLs from its abstract, then querying the GitHub API for repositories referencing the paper’s arXiv ID (obtained via OpenAlex) in their README. A manual check was conducted on the existence of the repositories. Information required for Figure 4 for related venues (i.e., CDC, ACC, and ECC) was obtained from the DBLP API. All required information was obtained in May 2026.
