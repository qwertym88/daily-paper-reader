Abstract: Model-based reinforcement learning has shown superior sample effi-ciency by leveraging the learned latent dynamics either in the form of derivative-free planning/imagined rollout training or dynamics gradient-based policy up-dates. While these approaches achieve comparatively fast learning, they all suffer, to varying degrees, from issues of recurrent compounding errors, high inference-time latency, and instability in backward propagation through time (BPTT). In this work, we propose a model-based RL approach that uses the chunk world model as a differentiable training-time evaluator to optimize the chunk actor without re-current model invocation. Through the collaborative chunk-wise prediction of the world model and policy, we can generate H-step hypothetical rollouts in a sin-gle forward pass and directly optimize value expansion, avoiding the costly and unstable BPTT. We evaluate our method on 19 challenging high-dimensional hu-manoid tasks from HumanoidBench and deploy our method to the real-world for quadruped bipedal walking. Project Website. 

Keywords: Model-based RL, Online RL, Continuous Control 

1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable success in solving complex continuous con-trol problems [1–3], games [4, 5] and large language model post-training [6, 7]. Modern model-free algorithms [8–11] provide general-purpose optimization methods and strong asymptotic perfor-mance, but they often require a large number of environment interactions. Model-based reinforce-ment learning (MBRL) offers a promising alternative by learning a surrogate world model that can provide additional information beyond real environment interactions. Modern MBRL methods have shown strong sample efficiency by leveraging learned latent dynamics in different ways. Dyna-style methods leverage the learned model to generate additional transitions as synthetic training data aug-mentation [5, 12–16], while MPC-style methods [17–21] use the learned model for decision-time planning. Another line of MBRL methods [22–24], termed as model-gradient-based methods, ex-ploit the learned dynamics as the differentiable simulator to backpropagate policy gradients through predicted trajectories, providing more informative updates than derivative-free policy search. Despite the different optimization forms, these approaches share a common structural dependency: they generate hypothetical future trajectories through recurrent model unrolling, often interleaved with repeated policy queries. This recursive invocation introduces several challenges for efficient policy optimization. First, generating an H-step hypothetical trajectory requires O(H) sequential model calls, which increases both training cost and, for planning-based methods, inference-time latency. Second, when policy gradients are propagated through these recurrent rollouts, optimization must rely on backpropagation through time (BPTT), which is costly and unstable over long horizons. Action chunking, which predicts a sequence of future actions instead of a single-step action, has been widely adopted in imitation learning to improve temporal consistency and reduce compound-ing action errors [25–27]. Recently, this idea has also begun to be explored in reinforcement learn-ing. In model-free RL, Q-Chunking [28] and Decoupled Q-Chunking [29] show that operating over

Beyond model-free settings, action chunks have also been incorporated into offline model-based RL. MAC [30] jointly learns a chunk policy and a long-horizon dynamics model to scale offline model-based policy learning over extended hori-zons. These developments suggest that action chunking can serve as a structural tool for improving temporal credit assignment and long-horizon policy optimization. However, existing chunk-based RL methods have primarily focused on reducing bootstrap in value learning, while the potential of chunk-wise modeling for model-gradient-based policy optimization remains underexplored. To answer this question, we propose Chunk-wise Model-based Policy Optimization(CMPO) that introduces a col-laborative chunk-wise modeling design for single-call model-gradient-based policy updates. Instead of learning a conventional one-step latent dynamics model zt+1 =

f (zt, at) and recurrently applying it over the predic-tion horizon, we learn a chunk world model that di-rectly maps the current latent state and a chunk of fu-ture actions to a sequence of future latent states, i.e., 

zt+1:t+H = f (zt, at:t+H−1). Together with a chunk-wise actor πθ (zt) = at:t+H−1, our method generates an 

H-step hypothetical rollout through a single model invo-cation, as shown in Figure 1. The chunk world model serves as a differentiable simulator, enabling the actor to directly optimize a multi-step value expansion objective without recurrent model-policy interactions that cause un-stable BPTT. At inference time, the actor predicts an ac-tion chunk but executes only the first action, preserving closed-loop responsiveness, which outperforms MPC-style methods without test-time planning. We systemati-cally evaluate our framework across 19 challenging, high-dimensional humanoid tasks from HumanoidBench[31]. Furthermore, to demonstrate the transferability of our approach, we apply our method to quadruped bipedal walking in the real world. Our experimental results show that CMPO achieves highly effi-cient policy improvement while maintaining substantially low training and inference budgets, offer-ing an efficient path forward for generalist robot reinforcement learning. Overall, the contributions of our work are summarized as follows: • We propose Collaborative Chunk-wise Modeling , which jointly models chunk-level actions and latent dynamics to generate multi-step hypothetical rollouts through a single model invocation. • We introduce CMPO , a model-gradient-based RL framework that avoids recurrent model rollout and long-horizon BPTT, improving both optimization stability and computational efficiency. • We demonstrate strong performance and sample efficiency on 19 HumanoidBench tasks, along with successful real-world deployment on quadruped bipedal locomotion. 

2 Related Work 

MBRL with Imagination as Augmentation. A classical way of using learned models in MBRL is to generate additional imagined experiences for policy or value learning, which can date back to Dyna [12]. Model rollouts are synthesized from real replay states and mixed with real transitions to incorporate the model-free learner [13, 32, 33]. In offline RL, methods such as MOPO [14], MOReL [34], and COMBO [35] also use model-generated rollouts to improve policy learning, and 2additionally introduce uncertainty penalties, pessimistic MDPs, or conservative value regularization to mitigate out-of-distribution model exploitation. These methods treat the model primarily as a data generator and the imagined transitions or trajectories are used as additional training samples. 

MBRL with Planning. Another important line of MBRL uses the learned model for decision-time planning. Early planning-based MBRL methods learn dynamics models and perform trajectory optimization through random shooting, the cross-entropy method (CEM), or related sampling-based optimizers [20, 36]. More recently, TD-MPC series [17, 18] combines short-horizon latent-space trajectory optimization with a learned terminal value function, allowing local model-based planning while relying on temporal-difference learning for long-term return estimation. Further, Wang et al. [21] bootstraps the policy learning by imitating the MPC expert and Lin et al. [37] introduces policy regularization to reduce the out-of-distribution queries by the planner and the learned policy prior. 

MBRL with Model Gradient. The third category of MBRL exploits the differentiability of the learned model and leverages model gradient to directly optimize the policy. Stochastic Value Gra-dient(SVG) [38] provides a general framework for continuous control by backpropagating through stochastic dynamics, value functions, and policies. Amos et al. [22] combines SVG with Soft Actor Critic [8] to improve the asymptotic performance of model-based methods and Model-Augmented Actor-Critic (MAAC) [23] further derives the monotonic improvement of the pathwise derivative in terms of the gradient error in the model and the value function. Dreamer series [5, 15, 16] also optimizes the actor by propagating gradients of imagined returns through compact latent trajecto-ries. Amigo et al. [24] instead performs trajectory unrolling with parallel simulators and propagates gradients through the learned model to mitigate compounding errors. These categories may overlap in hybrid algorithms and our method primarily falls within the model-gradient category, while remaining orthogonal to test-time planning. In Section 4.3, we further explore the potential of integrating our method with MPC.

3.2 Motivation 

Typical model-based RL methods [5, 15–18] generate hypothetical rollouts through recurrent inter-actions between the world model and the actor or planner. Given a latent state zt, a standard one-step latent dynamics model recursively predicts zt+k+1 = f (zt+k, at+k), at+k = πθ (zt+k), rt+k =

R(zt+k, at+k), for k = 0 , . . . , H − 1. The resulting imagined trajectory (zτ , aτ , rτ )t+Hτ =t is then used either for policy learning, value expansion, or decision-time planning. Although this recurrent rollout design provides useful model-based signals, it also introduces several structural limitations. 3First, generating an H-step hypothetical trajectory requires O(H) sequential model invocations. Since zt+k+1 depends on zt+k, these calls cannot be fully parallelized over the temporal dimension. This increases the training cost for imagination-based policy learning and further induces inference-time latency for MPC-style methods that repeatedly evaluate candidate action sequences. Action chunking[25], as a technique widely applied in imitation learning for learning temporally consistent behaviors, has recently begun to be explored in model-free RL as well, where it has also been found to reduce temporal difference bootstrap error by using unbiased n-step backups[28, 29]. Actors on chunked action space have also been applied alongside long-horizon world models for scaling offline model-based RL in [30]. These works reveal a broader role of action chunking in reinforcement learning: it can reshape the temporal structure of policy optimization, rather than merely generating consistent low-level controls. Existing chunk-based RL methods mainly exploit this structure from the value-learning perspective. However, the same idea is particularly appealing for model-based gradient policy optimization. The key bottlenecks discussed above all stem from a step-wise rollout mechanism, where the model and the actor must be recurrently invoked to construct an H-step hypothetical trajectory. This observation motivates our central question: can we leverage chunk-wise policy and dynamics modeling to preserve the multi-step training signal of model-based gradient optimization, while eliminating recurrent model invocation, the associated compounding error and BPTT issues? 

3.3 Collaborative Chunk-wise Modeling 

The core motivation is that if both the actor and the world model operate directly at the chunk level, the entire hypothetical trajectory can be generated through a single forward pass. Therefore, we 4model both the policy and the world model in a chunk-wise manner. Given a current latent state zt,the chunked actor predicts a sequence of future actions: 

at:t+H−1 = π(zt),

where H denotes the chunk horizon. Instead of using a one-step latent dynamics model zt+1 =

f (zt, at) and recursively applying it H times, we train a chunk world model that directly maps the current latent state and the entire action chunk to a future latent state chunk: 

zt+1:t+H = f (zt, at:t+H−1).

This collaborative chunk-wise design changes the role of the learned world model. Rather than serv-ing as a recurrent surrogate environment that must be rolled out step by step, the chunk world model acts as a differentiable training-time evaluator for the chunked actor. 4 Experiments 

The goal of our empirical evaluation is to assess whether collaborative chunk-wise modeling can benefit efficient model-gradient-based policy optimization. In particular, we investigate whether CMPO can achieve competitive performance and sample efficiency compared with strong model-free and model-based RL baselines, while also evaluating its applicability to real-world robotic sys-tems. Finally, we further analyze several key properties of CMPO, including optimization stability during training, the effects of different horizon settings, and its integration with test-time planning. 

4.1 High-dimensional Humanoid Continuous Control 

We evaluate our method on 19 selected challenging high-dimensional ( A ∈ R61 ) continuous control tasks from HumanoidBench [31]. These tasks require learning coordinated whole-body behaviors under large state and action spaces, which are suitable for evaluating sample efficiency, training stability, and long-horizon policy optimization of RL algorithms. 

Baselines. We compare our method against both model-free and model-based RL algorithms. For model-free baselines, we include PPO [9] and SAC [8], two widely used continuous-control al-gorithms with strong empirical performance. For model-based baselines, we compare with TD-6MPC2 [18], a strong MPC-style model-based RL method that combines latent dynamics learning with decision-time trajectory optimization, and DreamerV3 [15], a representative imagination-based method that trains actors and critics through recurrent latent rollouts and backpropagate the expected returns through the model. We additionally include MAAC [23] as a gradient-based model-based RL baseline. This comparison allows us to evaluate whether our chunk-wise formulation can retain the benefits of model-based methods while avoiding the instable optimization of BPPT. We provide the more experimental setting details in the Appendix C. 

Results. We present the averaged results across all 19 selected tasks in Figure 2 and demonstrate a subset of the results. The complete results are provided in Appendix C.4. The results show that CMPO achieves substantially better average performance and surpasses the baselines in the ma-jority of the evaluated tasks. Additionally, CMPO demonstrates a significant advantage in sample efficiency. Furthermore, we compare the training wall-time of TD-MPC2 and CMPO. We bench-mark the training wall-time of both methods on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 under identical settings and without employing optimization techniques such as Torch Compile. The results is as shown in Table 1. Benefiting from one-step hypothetical rollout generation enabled by chunk-wise mod-eling, as well as eliminating iterative zeroth-order optimization during inference, CMPO requires only around 50% of the training time of TD-MPC2 under the same number of training steps while achieving approximately a 30 × speedup in inference, further demonstrating its practical efficiency.

CMPO without the chunk-wise model. Our key de-sign lies in the collaborative chunk-wise modeling of both the policy and world dynamics model to enable one-step hypothetical rollout generation. A natural question is whether chunk-wise modeling remains effective when ap-plied to only one component. We note that combining a chunk-wise model with a single-step policy is ill-posed, as future latent-state chunks cannot be properly grounded by single-step actions. Therefore, in Table 2, we only evaluate the setting with a chunk-wise policy and a single-step model (w/o CM). The results indicate that while the chunk-wise policy eliminates repeated policy calls, the recursive invocations required by the single-step model still lead to significant per-formance degradation, further highlighting the negative effects of pathological BPTT dynamics in model-gradient-based methods. 

CMPO with test-time model-based planning. To highlight the effectiveness of chunk-wise model-ing itself, we do not adopt MPC-based test-time planning in the main experiments. Since chunk-wise modeling and MPC are orthogonal design choices, we further combine CMPO with the same MPPI planner used in TD-MPC2 and report the results in Table 2. We observe that MPC improves perfor-mance on unsaturated tasks, likely due to the additional exploration induced by MPPI’s sampling-based zeroth-order optimization. Meanwhile, on simpler tasks, the gradient information provided by chunk-wise modeling alone is sufficient to reach saturated performance. 

5 Conclusion 

We propose a collaborative chunk-wise modeling framework for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning, in which both the actor and the world dynamics model generate chunk-level foresight predictions. This chunk-wise design enables the generation of hypothetical future rollouts without recurrent model invocation, thereby reducing the computational overhead introduced by recursive calls. Moreover, in model-gradient-based training, it effectively stabilizes the optimization under long horizons by mitigating gradient oscillations and avoiding the pathological BPTT dynamics as-sociated with single-step models. The simulation results on high-dimensional humanoid continuous control tasks demonstrate that our method, relying solely on model-gradient-based optimization, surpasses both model-based methods that employ test-time planning and strong model-free base-lines. Finally, the real-world demonstrations of bipedal walking on the Unitree Go2 quadruped robot further validate its practical efficiency. 

Limitation 

In this work, we primarily focus on building a stable model-gradient-based method on top of TD-MPC2, and most of the model architecture is inherited from the design of TD-MPC2. Exploring network architectures that are more suitable for chunk-wise modeling in reinforcement learning remains an open question. In addition, to ensure closed-loop responsiveness in dynamic environ-ments, our chunk policy executes only the first action of each predicted chunk. However, for less dynamic environments, this design implies that the remaining actions within the chunk are discarded and therefore underutilized. While some prior work [40–42] in imitation learning has explored dy-namic action horizons, this direction remains relatively underexplored in reinforcement learning. Therefore, an important future direction is how to effectively integrate our collaborative chunk-wise modeling framework with dynamic horizon execution. 9Acknowledgments

B.2 Network Settings 

We retain most of the network architecture and hyperparameter settings from TD-MPC2 to ensure that the performance gains of CMPO arise solely from our collaborative chunk-wise modeling. Our chunk-wise policy and dynamics model are built upon the MLP-based single-step policy/model architecture of TD-MPC2. Concretely, we extend the output size of the single-step policy from the original action dim to action dim × horizon . For the chunk-wise dynamics model, the input dimension is expanded from latent dim + action dim to latent dim + horizon × action dim , and the output dimension is correspondingly extended from latent dim to latent dim × horizon . Such a design introduces only minimal modifications to the original TD-MPC2 architecture, ensuring that the observed performance gains primarily stem from collaborative chunk-wise modeling rather than architectural differences. 13 C Experimental Details

C.3 Real World Experiment Setup 

To evaluate the real-world performance and sim-to-real transferability of our method, we conduct experiments on a bipedal locomotion task using a Unitree Go2 quadruped. We first train the lo-comotion policy in Isaac Gym and subsequently deploy the learned policy on the real robot. The policy controls all 12 actuated joints through residual joint-position commands, which are tracked 14 by a PD controller. The reward function encourages the robot to maintain an upright bipedal pos-ture while tracking commanded linear and angular velocities, together with regularization terms on joint acceleration, power consumption, action variation, collisions, and hip motion. During simula-tion training, the robot learns to stand and locomote using its hind legs, and the resulting policy is transferred to the physical Go2 platform for real-world evaluation.
