# Decision-Sufficient Posterior Approximation 

Sean Plummer October 9, 2026 

Abstract 

We investigate the consequences of requiring a posterior approximation to preserve a specified downstream decision problem. A target posterior P and loss determine a regret geometry on actions, a baseline approximation Q0 determines the forward-Kullback–Leibler information required to induce action changes, and a restricted approximation family Q determines which such changes are available. Contracting KL divergence over Bayes-action fibers gives exact distances to decision adequacy and decision failure together with the least-informative posterior deformations that reach either side of the decision boundary. In regular finite-dimensional problems, the target and baseline constructions have quadratic local limits: a target regret Hessian G and a baseline information metric JI . Their generalized eigenproblem Gv = γJ I v orders local decision directions by regret consequence per unit informa-tion cost and induces a tolerance-dependent effective dimension. For restricted approximation families, the tangent image separates decision coverage from information efficiency: a family may miss consequential decision directions, or it may realize reachable directions only at excess Fisher cost. The resulting framework provides decision-relative criteria for comparing and designing posterior approximation families. 

# 1 Introduction 

We investigate the consequences of requiring a posterior approximation to preserve a specified downstream decision problem. A Bayesian decision problem maps posterior uncertainty into an action through a loss. Once that map is fixed, posterior discrepancies acquire different operational consequences: some move the chosen action through directions with substantial regret, while others have little effect at the decision scale of interest. This suggests studying posterior approximation through the geometry induced by the decision rather than through a single distribution-wide discrepancy. The construction separates three roles. The target posterior P and loss determine decision consequence : which actions are acceptable and how costly nearby action errors are. A baseline approximation Q0 determines information accessibility : the forward-KL information required to move the Bayes action in a prescribed direction. A specified approximation family Q inside an ambient candidate class of admissible measures determines family representability : which of those decision movements are available inside the family and whether they can be realized at the ambient minimum Fisher cost. The target posterior, baseline approximation, ambient candidate class, and restricted family play distinct roles throughout the paper; in particular, the target posterior need not belong to Q.Using classical tools from Bayesian decision theory, I-projection, semiparametric efficiency, information geometry, and approximation theory, we develop a decision-relative theory of posterior approximation. Contracting KL divergence over Bayes-action fibers produces exact distances to decision adequacy and decision failure. In regular finite-dimensional problems, the target regret 1arXiv:2610.11038v1 [math.ST] 8 Oct 2026 and baseline information constructions have quadratic local limits, represented by matrices G

and JI . Their generalized eigenproblem produces decision–information modes, ordered by regret consequence per unit information cost, and a tolerance-dependent effective dimension. For restricted approximation families, the same geometry yields separate diagnostics for decision coverage and information efficiency. These ingredients connect several established lines of work. Loss-calibrated Bayesian approxima-tion evaluates approximate inference through downstream utility or regret [8]. Decision-theoretic divergences and information–risk relations describe how a loss selects task-relevant distinctions be-tween distributions [2, 14]. I-projection characterizes minimum relative-entropy perturbations under distributional constraints [1], while Watson and Holmes study decision stability inside Kullback– Leibler neighborhoods of an approximate model [13]. Estimating-function and semiparametric theory provide the sensitivity, covariance, and tangent-space efficiency structure used in the local construction [5, 6, 12]. Goal-oriented Bayesian inverse problems use a related spectral reduction to identify directions that are both data-informed and relevant to a quantity of interest [11]. The contribution here is to organize these classical constructions around posterior approximation for a specified decision problem and to carry that organization through exact global contractions, local spectral reduction, and restricted-family diagnostics.

Decision adequacy is deliberately indexed by the downstream task. # 8 Discussion 

A specified decision problem induces three coupled objects for posterior approximation. The target posterior and loss determine a regret geometry on actions; the baseline approximation determines the information required to move through that geometry; and a restricted approximation family determines which movements are available and at what Fisher cost. In regular problems, the pair 

(G, J I ) makes the first two objects explicit, while (ηT , κ T ) summarize local family coverage and information efficiency. 

8.1 Implications for variational approximation 

The geometry gives a constructive criterion for comparing and extending variational families. A useful family extension can improve decision support in two ways: it can add an omitted action direction with substantial regret consequence, thereby reducing ηT , or it can add a posterior score direction that lowers the Fisher cost of an already-reachable action displacement, thereby reducing 

κT . These two improvements are distinct even when the candidate families have the same parameter count or tangent-image rank. The oracle effective dimension deff (ϵ; ρ) gives the smallest rank that could support a uniform decision guarantee at a chosen regret and information scale. The coverage defect ηT then measures how well the actual family image is aligned with that oracle geometry. When coverage is complete, 

κT determines whether the family reaches the relevant directions with the same local information efficiency as the ambient posterior class. The examples illustrate why this decision-relative view can change a family comparison. The same centered mean-field family can have a pure coverage defect for one decision and complete 22 coverage with excess Fisher cost for another. In the structured-Gaussian example, the family with the better global reverse-KL approximation is not the family that best preserves every downstream second-moment decision. These are different questions because they weight posterior discrepancies through different operational maps. 

8.2 Tolerance and information scale 

The effective dimension is indexed by the regret tolerance ϵ and information scale ρ. Tightening the regret budget can make previously negligible directions relevant, while increasing the posterior perturbation radius can expose additional consequential directions. The family certificate ρη T ≤ ϵ

has the same dependence. The choice of ρ is therefore part of the application-level specification of the approximation problem. It can be set by a scientifically meaningful KL neighborhood, by the observed scale of a target–baseline mismatch, or by a distribution of plausible perturbations. When a target ensemble is available, the average-case representation in Section 5.3 replaces worst-case structural importance by the frequency with which plausible mismatches occupy each direction. Structural and realized importance should be kept separate. A large generalized eigenvalue means that a direction can generate substantial regret per unit information; it does not imply that a particular target mismatch has a large component in that direction. 

8.3 Scope of the local theory 

The quadratic theory covers regular finite-dimensional decisions with nondegenerate first-order response and enough localization to keep the relevant optimization near the baseline. The global regret and information contractions remain meaningful beyond that regime, but their local approximations can change. Binary classification already gives a simple nonquadratic example because the acceptable action set changes discontinuously. The restricted-family diagnostics are also local. Curved, disconnected, or irregular families can admit finite movements that are invisible from a single linear tangent space, and favorable tangent geometry does not imply that a nonconvex optimizer will find the relevant family member. The exact restricted contraction remains the global benchmark; (ηT , κ T ) describe the first-order geometry around Q0 for regular families. The ambient I-projection is likewise an information-theoretic benchmark rather than a claim that every minimizing distribution can arise from the original prior–likelihood model. Model realizability, family restriction, optimization error, Monte Carlo error, and finite computation impose additional layers of constraint. Finally, the present development treats G, JI , and the response operators as known. Applied use requires estimating these objects, and uncertainty in those estimates can change the apparent effective dimension, mode ordering, or family defects. Quantifying that uncertainty is a statistical problem built on top of the geometry developed here. 

8.4 Directions for extension 

The geometry suggests a direct family-design problem. When ηT is driven by a small number of omitted high-consequence modes, one can seek the smallest tangent-space augmentation that captures them. When ηT = 0 but κT is large, one can instead seek posterior directions that recover the ambient minimum-information lifts of reachable decisions. This turns decision geometry into a guide for structured variational-family design. 23 There is also a dynamic version of the problem. In an iterative approximation algorithm, the current approximation can be viewed as a state, admissible family or algorithm updates as controls, the induced decision displacement as an output, and KL or computational effort as a movement cost. This formulation leads naturally to reachability and minimum-energy questions under algorithmic constraints and provides a concrete point of contact with control theory. Beyond the regular regime, boundary decisions can lead to tangent-cone geometry, while degen-erate or singular decision maps can require higher-order local structure rather than the quadratic pair (G, J I ). The global decision problem remains the same; the appropriate local approximation changes with the order and shape of the response. 

8.5 Conclusion 

For a specified downstream decision problem, posterior approximation can be assessed through three quantities: the regret induced by action error, the information required to induce that error from a baseline approximation, and the ability of a chosen approximation family to realize the corresponding decision directions. This decision-relative organization yields exact global accessibility measures, local decision–information modes, a tolerance-dependent effective dimension, and separate diagnostics for family coverage and information efficiency.

C.7 Why uniformity and localization are separate requirements 

Uniform expansions on bounded h-sets identify the limiting objective and boundary geometry. Localization ensures that optimizing sequences actually remain inside such bounded sets. Neither property implies the other. In particular, the local expansion of IQ0 (a0 + th ) does not exclude a remote action whose exact contracted information is also O(t2). The information-gap condition in (M4) supplies the global separation needed to rule out that branch.
