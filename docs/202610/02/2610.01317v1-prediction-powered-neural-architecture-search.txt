ABSTRACT 

Evaluating candidate architectures in neural architecture search (NAS) faces an inherent trade-off: on the one hand, reliable performance labels are limited because training and evaluating architectures is costly; on the other hand, zero-cost proxies (ZCPs) are cheap to compute at large scale but can be noisy. Yet, how to effectively combine these two sources of supervision remains unclear. In this paper, we propose PP NAS, a novel prediction-powered inference (PPI) approach for NAS. 

> PP

NAS fuses (1) a small set of architectures with observed performance labels and (2) a large set of architectures with ZCP information. To combine these two sources of supervision, PP NAS exploits the ordinal information provided by ZCPs to construct additional pairwise ranking supervision, while PPI debiases systematic discrepancies between ZCP-based and true performance rankings. We evaluate 

> PP

NAS in end-to-end predictor-based NAS, where it achieves state-of-the-art under limited evaluation budgets. To the best of our knowledge, PP NAS is the first prediction-powered approach for label-efficient NAS. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

The goal of neural architecture search (NAS) is to identify high-performing neural network architec-tures. NAS has been successfully applied across a wide range of tasks, including image classification (Zoph & Le, 2016; Real et al., 2019), object detection and semantic segmentation (Poyser & Breckon, 2024), and natural language processing (Elsken et al., 2019; White et al., 2023). However, obtaining reliable performance estimates for candidate architectures typically requires frequent training and evaluation, which is a key bottleneck in NAS, and subject to an inherent trade-off:  

> 1

Ground-truth performance labels , on the one hand, can provide reliable supervision for learning predictors, which can estimate the performance of unseen architectures and guide the search toward promising candidate architectures (White et al., 2020; 2023; Kadlecová et al., 2024). However, training such predictors requires labeled data about architectures with corresponding performance estimates, but obtaining these is costly (Zoph & Le, 2016; Real et al., 2019). As a result, the number of ground-truth labels available for learning accurate performance predictors is limited.  

> 2

Zero-cost proxies (ZCPs) , on the other hand, can provide cheap signals about the performance of candidate architectures without the cost of training (Mellor et al., 2021; Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Krishnakumar et al., 2022). To do so, ZCPs assign heuristic scores to candidate architectures based on properties of the neural architecture (e.g., summed importance of specific neurons) or based on other derived quantities computed using a small amount of data (Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Ning et al., 2021; Tanaka et al., 2020; Mellor et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2018). Prior work suggests that the ordering induced by ZCPs can provide useful ordinal information for ranking different architectures (Dudziak et al., 2020; Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2023). However, ZCP scores can be noisy and may not reliably reflect true architecture performance. 1arXiv:2610.01317v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Together, both ground-truth performance estimates and ZCPs have different strengths and weaknesses for evaluating candidate architectures in terms of quantity, computational cost, and reliability. This motivates our research question: how can we combine supervision signals from 1 reliable but scarce ground-truth performance estimates and 2 scalable but noisy ZCPs? 

To address this challenge, we propose PP NAS , a prediction-powered inference (PPI) approach for label-efficient NAS . PP NAS fuses (i) a small set of ground-truth labeled architectures with (ii) a substantially larger set of ZCP-only samples. We use both sources to construct pairwise ranking supervision, where the ground-truth performance labels provide reliable rankings, while ZCPs provide abundant but noisy rankings at low cost. To combine these two sources of supervision, 

> PP

NAS exploits the ordinal information provided by ZCPs, while PPI (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) debiases systematic discrepancies between the ZCP-based rankings and the ground-truth performance rankings. As a result, this allows PP NAS to learn an architecture scoring function that can rank unseen candidate architectures for NAS using only a small number of expensive evaluations. We evaluate PP NAS as a performance-predictor within end-to-end NAS. Our results show that PP NAS improves the performance prediction of candidate architectures, which leads to large performance gains in end-to-end NAS under limited budgets. Methodologically, we adapt principles from PPI (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; 2024) for NAS. PPI pro-vides a principled way to combine large-scale, potentially noisy predictions with scarce ground-truth labels to correct systematic prediction errors and enable statistically valid inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Zrnic & Candès, 2024). To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to leverage PPI for label-efficient NAS. However, our setting offers three challenges that require careful adaptation. 

Challenge a : Standard PPI settings focus on estimating a target population parameter. In contrast, in NAS we want to learn a feature-dependent scoring function gθ (X) that must generalize to unseen architecture specifications, where the pool of potential architectures is not fixed. This necessitates an adapted prediction-powered objective for ranking, that can then guide architecture search. Challenge  

> b

: PPI requires prediction in the same target space, but ZCPs instead provide proxy-specific scores that can only be interpreted through their relative ordering. We thus need to formulate PPI over pairwise ranking losses rather than pointwise prediction losses. Challenge c : The resulting pair-wise observations are statistically dependent because multiple pairs can share the same architecture. Consequently, standard variance formulas from PPI++ and power-tuning formulas (Angelopoulos et al., 2024) cannot be applied directly at the pair level by treating the pairwise observations as independent; here we instead account for the dependence by computing architecture-level Hájek contributions (Hoeffding, 1948; Serfling, 1980) when controlling the contribution of ZCP supervision and estimation uncertainty. Our main contributions are three-fold: (1) We leverage prediction-powered inference for label-efficient NAS. (2) We derive a tailored PPI framework for this purpose called PP NAS by developing a rectified pairwise-ranking objective that accounts for systematic discrepancies between ZCP-induced and ground-truth rankings while adaptively weighting the ZCP supervision. (3) We demonstrate the practical benefits of PP NAS in end-to-end NAS, where it improves search performance under limited ground-truth evaluation budgets across multiple benchmarks and ZCPs. 

2 RELATED WORK 

We give an overview of three literature streams relevant to our work: (1) performance prediction in NAS, (2) zero-cost proxies, and (3) prediction-powered inference. 

Performance prediction in NAS: Various search strategies have been proposed for NAS, such as reinforcement learning and evolutionary approaches (Zoph & Le, 2016; Real et al., 2019), weight-sharing methods (Bender et al., 2018; Pham et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2018), and predictor-based NAS (White et al., 2020; 2021; Kadlecová et al., 2024). Predictor-based methods, which are the focus of our work, learn a machine learning model to predict architecture performance from the overall architecture specification, and then use these predictions to select promising candidate architectures for subsequent expensive evaluation. Prominent examples of predictor-based methods are BANANAS 

(White et al., 2021), and BOHAMIANN (Springenberg et al., 2016), which directly predict validation accuracy. An alternative is learning the relative architecture performance through ranking (Ji et al., 2025). Examples of the latter are RankNAS (Hu et al., 2021), ProxyBO (Shen et al., 2023), and

PP NAS does not focus on the performance predictor itself but how to leverage the performance predictions for data fusion. 

Zero-cost proxies: ZCPs provide noisy but cheap performance estimates, which do not require conventional architecture training (Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Mellor et al., 2021; Krishnakumar et al., 2022). Common examples are synflow (Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Tanaka et al., 2020) and grasp 

(Wang et al., 2020). These ZCPs are then used either standalone (Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Mellor et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023) or as additional features into supervised performance predictors (White et al., 2021; Krishnakumar et al., 2022; White et al., 2023; Kadlecová et al., 2024). Other approaches, such as ProxyBO (Shen et al., 2023) or ZCPBP Warmup (Abdelfattah et al., 2021), create ZCP-based rankings to augment the training of a predictor. However, existing approaches from this stream primarily use ZCPs as standalone signals, as additional predictor inputs, or directly within the search procedure. In contrast, a principled way to combine the ZCPs with ground-truth architecture evaluations is missing. Our approach is thus different by using ZCP-induced rankings as 

additional supervision for ground-truth rankings. 

Prediction-powered inference: PPI provides a principled framework for rigorous statistical inference by “fusing” a small amount of ground-truth labeled data with a much larger set of potentially noisy predictions to improve statistical efficiency while using the ground-truth labels to correct for errors in the predictions (Angelopoulos et al., 2023); a brief overview is in Section A . Several extensions have been developed over the past years. For example, PPI++ extends this approach by a weighting parameter λ to improve the statistical efficiency of the resulting estimator (Angelopoulos et al., 2024). Even other works have adapted PPI to specific tasks such as LLM leaderboards Chatzi et al. (2024); Boyeau et al. (2025); Frauen et al. (2026). However, existing PPI approaches differ from our setting in several important ways: (1) In terms of task, PPI has been applied to a range of inference problems, but not to label-efficient NAS. (2) In terms of estimand, rather than ranking a fixed set of observed items, we must learn a feature-dependent scoring function gθ (X) that generalizes to previously unseen architectures ( → Challenge a ). (3) In terms of method, this requires adapting PPI to the dependence structure induced by pairwise architecture comparisons ( → Challenge c ). 

Research gap: To the best of our knowledge, a principled framework for combining (a) scarce ground-truth architecture evaluations with (b) large-scale ZCPs is missing. Here, we thus introduce the first PPI-based approach to label-efficient NAS.

Why standard PPI++ cannot be used. Standard PPI++ selects λ to minimize the variance of the estimator (Angelopoulos et al., 2024). However, in our setting, the ranking losses are computed over all architecture pairs, and these pairwise observations are not independent, as two pairs can share the same architecture (e.g., (i, j ) and (j, k ) sharing j; → Challenge c ). Hence, treating the constructed pairs as independent observations would underestimate the variance due to sampling dependence. To address this, we propose to characterize variability at the level of the independently sampled architectures rather than at the level of the constructed pairs.

4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmark datasets. We use NasBench201 (NB201) (Dong & Yang, 2020), a tabular benchmark that contains pre-computed validation accuracies and ZCPs for neural architectures trained on CIFAR10 

(Krizhevsky, 2009), CIFAR100 (Krizhevsky, 2009), and ImageNet16-120 (Chrabaszcz et al., 2017; Dong et al., 2021). To assess whether our findings generalize to other search spaces, we additionally use NASBench101 (NB101) (Ying et al., 2019) and TransNASBench (TNB) (Duan et al., 2021). 

Baselines. We compare against the following baselines: 1 simple baselines: ZCP only (e.g. Kr-ishnakumar et al., 2022; Kadlecová et al., 2024), random search ; 2 non-ranking baselines: BA-NANAS (White et al., 2021), BOHAMIANN (Springenberg et al., 2016), SemiNAS (Luo et al., 2020); 3 ranking baselines: ReNAS (Xu et al., 2021), PWLNAS (Ji et al., 2025), RankNAS (Hu et al., 2021), ProxyBO (Shen et al., 2023), and ZCPBP Warmup (Abdelfattah et al., 2021). 

ZCPs. We focus on the most-widely used ZCPs from the literature: synflow (Abdelfattah et al., 2021; Tanaka et al., 2020), jacov and nwot (Mellor et al., 2021), grad_norm (Abdelfattah et al., 2021), snip (Lee et al., 2018), grasp (Wang et al., 2020). We encode architectures via a one-hot vector, which is widely used (e.g. White et al., 2020; 2021; Kadlecová et al., 2024). 

Eval protocol: We evaluate a label-constrained setting with scarce ground-truth evaluations. Specifi-cally, we use a 30-iteration predictor-guided NAS protocol, starting from n = 10 ground-truth labeled architectures and N = 1000 ZCP-only architectures. At each iteration, we sample 100 candidates and evaluate the top five according to the respective acquisition rule. We repeat each search 30 times and report mean performance ± standard deviation. Search quality is measured by final incumbent accuracy and the normalized area under the best-so-far accuracy curve (AUC), which captures how quickly strong architectures are identified. • Main results. We first benchmark our proposed PP NAS against the baselines under the same limited ground-truth evaluation budget, so that comparison is fair . Results are in Appendix 12, with further search trajectories in Section C. We find: (1) Our PP NAS performs best, reaching a 1.5

median rank versus 3 for the second-best method. (2) PP NAS consistently reaches a higher AUC than baselines, implying that its ZCP-based supervision can help find better architectures early in the search. ⇒ Takeaway : Our PP NAS improves label-efficient NAS search .

What explains the improved performance? PP NAS differs from baselines in several ways. Here, ReNAS and PWLNAS learn from ground-truth ranking using hinge- and logistic-ranking objectives, respectively, but do not exploit the ZCP-only sample. Further, ZCPBP Warmup uses ZCP-induced rankings to warm up a predictor, but not afterwards. In contrast to these ranking-based approaches, 

PP NAS uses the ground-truth and ZCP-induced ranking supervision together throughout the entire fitting . Importantly, we do not treat the proxy rankings as ground truth: their contribution is explicitly rectified using the proxy loss on the gold-labeled sample. The strong performance of PP NAS thus demonstrates that abundant ZCP-induced rankings and (scarce) ground-truth rankings are best combined in a principled way.

• Analysis of hyperparameter λ. In Figure 4, we visualize how λ evolves over the search process; further results in AppendixD, Fig-ure 11. We consistently observe that, as more ground-truth evaluations become available, λ de-creases from an initial high ∼0.8 and stabilizes around 0.2–0.3. This is consistent with the intended role of λ: when ground-truth supervision is scarce, the model places greater weight on the additional ZCP-induced ranking supervision. ⇒ Takeaway :

PP NAS is effective in adapting λ over the search.

We find that PP NAS again achieves the best mean and median ranks on both benchmarks. The findings for TNB mean that PP NAS also improves performance for macro-level search spaces. ⇒ Takeaway : Our results are robust to different search spaces. 

• Stress test: misspecified ZCP direction. Throughout our experiments, we use the common convention that larger ZCP scores indicate better architectures (see Abdelfattah et al. (2021); Krish-nakumar et al. (2022) and Figure 17). In practice, a ZCP with the opposite direction could simply be reverse-coded. Here, however, we deliberately leave the direction unchanged to stress-test whether 

PP NAS can automatically remain robust to such misspecified ZCP supervision. We now use NB101 to stress-test what happens when ZCP direction is misspecified. Here, for snip , grad_norm , and 

jacov , larger ZCP scores are associated with lower validation performance, as indicated by their negative Spearman correlations in Figure 18. Despite the ZCP misspecification, PP NAS remains competitive. In the per-ZCP comparison (Fig-ure 19), PP NAS ranks first with snip and jacov , second with grad_norm , and first for all remaining ZCPs. This stress test illustrates that the learned architecture ranking can remain robust even when the direction of the auxiliary ZCP supervision is incorrectly specified. ⇒ Takeaway :

PP NAS is robust to misspecified ZCP supervision. More broadly, this reflects the role of the prediction-powered formulation, where ZCP-induced preferences provide additional supervision but are not assumed to be perfectly reliable. 

• Runtimes. Runtimes for PP NAS are comparable to the baselines; see Figure 20. 9Conclusion. Our PP NAS is the first prediction-powered approach for label-efficient NAS, by combining scarce ground-truth supervision with large-scale ZCP-induced rankings through a rectified ranking objective. Across our experiments, it achieves consistent performance improvements across datasets, search spaces, and ZCPs. Future work could extend our prediction-powered approach to LLMs and study whether similar ideas can support the study of scaling laws.

NAS search protocol. All methods are evaluated within the same iterative predictor-guided NAS protocol. Each search starts from n = 10 randomly selected architectures with observed ground-truth validation performance. We additionally provide N = 1000 architectures with ZCP information but without ground-truth performance labels. The unlabeled sample size is kept fixed throughout the search. For each search iteration t = 1 , . . . , 30 , the corresponding performance predictor is fitted using the information available at that iteration. We then sample 100 candidate architectures from the remaining search space, score them according to the method-specific acquisition rule, and select the top five for ground-truth evaluation. The newly evaluated architectures are added to the labeled set before the next predictor fit. Thus, in the main setting, the labeled set grows from 10 to 160 architectures over the 30 search iterations. For methods that require ensemble-based uncertainty, we use an ensemble of three independently fitted predictors. PPNAS instead uses the parameter covariance estimated by its ranking model for uncertainty-aware acquisition and therefore requires only a single fitted model. We repeat every search configuration 30 times. At each iteration, we record the validation accuracy of the best architecture observed so far. We summarize each search trajectory using two complementary metrics: final incumbent accuracy at the end of the search and the normalized area under the best-so-far accuracy curve (AUC). We compute AUC over the common 30-iteration search horizon using trapezoidal integration and normalize by the horizon length, such that AUC remains on the same numerical scale as validation accuracy. Final accuracy and AUC are reported as the mean and standard deviation across repeated searches. To summarize performance across experimental conditions, we rank methods by their AUC within each benchmark–dataset–proxy condition and report the mean and median rank across these com-parisons. AUC rewards methods that identify strong architectures early in the search, whereas final accuracy reflects the quality of the incumbent after the complete evaluation budget has been consumed.
