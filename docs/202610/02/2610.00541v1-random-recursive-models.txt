## ABSTRACT 

Recursive models create computational depth through parameter reuse, offering a parameter-efficient alternative to increasing model size. However, most recur-sive models repeatedly apply one learned transformation or a prescribed sequence of transformations, restricting computation to a fixed layer order. We introduce the Random Recursive Model (RRM), which maintains a pool of L learned layers and performs T recursive steps by sampling one layer independently with replace-ment for each example and step. This enables flexible layer reuse while retaining the parameter efficiency of recurrence. We evaluate RRM on challenging reason-ing tasks, where it matches or exceeds the baselines, often with 50–75% fewer parameters. RRM can vary its depth at inference, including beyond that seen dur-ing training, without retraining or adding parameters, improving tasks that benefit from deeper iterative computation. RRM also supports Monte Carlo inference and probabilistic test-time scaling, both of which improve performance without retraining. These insights may open new directions in neural network architecture design. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recursive models have recently obtained strong results on challenging reasoning tasks (Geiping et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). By reusing parameters across depth, they can allocate many computational steps to an example without requiring a distinct learned layer at every step. This makes recursion a direct way to separate model size from computation, including computation allocated through test-time scaling (Snell et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2026). Most recursive models nevertheless use a fixed-order recurrence. They either repeat one learned transformation or repeatedly apply a set of transformations in a prescribed order. Recent works add further structure through fixed update schedules, separate high- and low-level modules, distinct an-swer and reasoning states, or provisional-answer feedback (Wang et al., 2025; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). We study a different and simpler form of recursion, which we call the Random Recursive Model (RRM), in which learned layers form a pool of reusable transformations and any layer can be reused at any point in the computation. An RRM contains L learned layer transformations and randomly reuses them over T applications, or recursive steps. Unlike standard recurrence, which creates depth by applying the same transfor-mation or the same fixed sequence, RRM can revisit the same layer or apply any of the other learned layers at each application. During both training and inference, at each of the T recursive steps, one layer is sampled uniformly and independently with replacement for each example. RRM therefore retains the parameter sharing of recurrence while allowing up to LT possible layer sequences, with-out imposing a fixed recurrent order. The number of learned layers L determines model size, while 

T determines computational depth and can be increased or decreased at test time. Across our experiments, we start from established fixed-order baselines and replace their backbones with RRM while retaining the task formulation and core model components. Across all five tasks, the strongest RRM configuration matches or exceeds the corresponding baseline. In controlled image classification and autoregressive logic puzzles, RRM does so with roughly 50–75% fewer parameters. The same RRM construction also matches or exceeds existing methods on more difficult reasoning tasks without any structured, hierarchical or prescribed structure. We further study how RRM scales with the number of learned layers L and the number of applica-tions T . RRM therefore naturally supports both training-time scaling and test-time scaling, since T

## 2 BACKGROUND 

Most deep networks execute the learned transformations in a fixed order. This is true both for conventional feed-forward networks, which assign different parameters to different depths, and for modern recursive networks, which reuse parameters across depth. The two families differ in param-eter sharing, but both prescribe the sequence of transformations before seeing an example. 2.1 FIXED -ORDER DEPTH 

A feed-forward network of depth L maps an encoded input h0 = E(x) through an ordered sequence of functions, 

hℓ+1 = fℓ+1 (hℓ), ℓ = 0 , . . . , L − 1. (1) The computation is therefore the fixed composition fL ◦ · · · ◦ f1. Every example applies each function once and in the same order. Transformers are a prominent instance of this design. Each layer has its own parameters and occupies a predetermined position in the stack (Vaswani et al., 2023). Increasing depth ordinarily adds both computation and parameters. 2.2 FIXED -RECURSIVE DEPTH 

Recursive models instead create depth by applying a learned transformation repeatedly. In its sim-plest form, 

ht+1 = f (ht, E (x)) , t = 0 , . . . , T − 1, (2) where the encoded input may optionally be injected at each step. Parameter sharing decouples computational depth T from model size. This principle underlies the Universal Transformer (De-hghani et al., 2019), looped Transformers (Yang et al., 2024), and recurrent-depth language models (Geiping et al., 2025). Recent reasoning systems similarly obtain large effective depth from small recurrent modules, including the Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) (Wang et al., 2025) and the Tiny Recursive Model (TRM) (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). The recurrence becomes explicit when we unroll the computation. Define fx(h) = f (h, E (x)) for a fixed input x. After T steps, 

hT = fx ◦ fx ◦ · · · ◦ fx

## 3 RANDOM RECURSIVE MODELS 

We introduce the Random Recursive Model (RRM). RRM retains the input encoder E, all L learned layers of the backbone F = {f1, . . . , f L}, and the task-specific output head H. Unlike fixed-order feed-forward depth and fixed-recursive depth, RRM makes every backbone layer reusable rather than applying all layers once in their prescribed order or repeatedly following a fixed sequence. Given an input x, the encoder initializes the hidden state as h0 = E(x), although h0 may instead be any task-specific starting state. For a fixed computational depth T , at each step t = 0 , . . . , T − 1,RRM samples one of the L layers uniformly at random and applies it to the current state. 

ht+1 = frt (ht, E (x)) , rt ∼ Uniform {1, . . . , L }, (4) where rt is the layer index sampled at step t. Sampling is performed independently for every exam-ple and every recurrent step. The indices are sampled with replacement, so a function may be applied multiple times or not at all in a particular computation. The second argument E(x) denotes optional input reinjection. When the backbone does not inject the encoded input at every step, Equation 4 reduces to ht+1 = frt (ht). After T applications, the task-specific head returns ˆy = H(hT ). RRM does not prescribe a particular layer type. Its layers may be Transformer blocks or other learned state transformations. The complete computation for an example is determined by the random sequence r =(r0, . . . , r T −1) and can be written as 

hT = frT −1 ◦ · · · ◦ fr1 ◦ fr0 (h0; E(x)) . (5) Figure 1 compares RRM with fixed-order depth and fixed-recursive depth. These architectures differ in how their layers are ordered and reused. Setting L = 1 repeats a single learned transformation. Replacing random layer selection with a prescribed repeating sequence gives fixed-recursive depth, whether that sequence contains one layer, a subset, or all L layers. Setting T = L and rt = t + 1 

gives fixed-order depth. Fixed-recursive and fixed-order models can therefore be viewed as special cases of RRM. RRM retains multiple learned layers while composing them recursively. This preserves the parameter-efficient depth and adjustable computation of recurrence while giving each step access to multiple learned state transformations. At recursive depth T , RRM admits LT possible layer sequences instead of committing to one fixed composition, providing diverse computational trajec-tories without an additional learned mechanism. 3.1 TRAINING

Allocating additional computation at inference is a widely studied approach to scaling reasoning (Snell et al., 2024; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). Thus, RRM supports compute scaling during both training and inference by varying Ttrain or Ttest without changing model size. ## 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate RRM in three settings of increasing reasoning difficulty. For each task, we follow the task formulation, data splits, model components, training protocol, and evaluation procedure of established baselines. Our main change is to replace the fixed-order backbone with an RRM back-bone. Any further task-specific changes are stated below. In every experiment, one layer index is sampled independently for each example and each application. Unless noted otherwise, the number of applications is fixed within a run. Where separate validation and test splits are available, we use validation performance for checkpoint selection and evaluate the selected model on the disjoint test set. Unless stated otherwise, RRM results are reported as mean ± sample standard deviation over three evaluation seeds for a fixed checkpoint. 

CIFAR-10. We use CIFAR-10 as a controlled toy experiment (Krizhevsky & Hinton, 2009). The baseline is a custom eight-layer fixed-order Vision Transformer following Dosovitskiy et al. (2021), adapted to CIFAR-10 and trained under the same protocol as RRM. RRM replaces its eight-layer backbone with two reusable Transformer layers ( L = 2 ). For training-time scaling, we train models with Ttrain ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 16 }. For test-time scaling, we fix models trained with Ttrain ∈ { 8, 16 }

and evaluate Ttest ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 16 , 20 , 24 }. We additionally evaluate Monte Carlo inference with 

M = 16 for every combination of Ttrain ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 16 } and Ttest ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 16 , 20 , 24 }. We separately train an L = 1 , Ttrain = 16 model to study single-layer reuse. Finally, we examine how the class-token representations of the Ttrain = 16 model evolve over Ttest ∈ { 8, 12 , 16 , 20 , 24 }

using one sampled sequence per image. We quantify class separation with a k-nearest-neighbor ( k-NN) classifier using k = 20 and visualize the representations with t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008). Performance is measured by top-1 accuracy on the official test set. Architecture and training details are provided in Appendix A.2. 

Sudoku and Zebra. We train on the processed Sudoku and Zebra datasets publicly released by Shah et al. (2024). 2 We use their strongest solver-ordered formulation. The causal Transformer has eight layers and provides the fixed-order baseline. RRM changes only the backbone composition. On Sudoku, we evaluate L ∈ { 2, 4} reusable layers, while Zebra uses L = 2 . Every RRM is trained with Ttrain = 16 applications. All comparisons use the same 320,000-step training budget. We train on the full released training sets and evaluate on task-specific held-out splits using their greedy autoregressive protocol. For Zebra, we additionally evaluate test-time scaling for the trained RRM with L = 2 and Ttrain = 16 , using Ttest ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 16 , 20 }.

Extreme reasoning tasks. For Sudoku-Extreme, Maze-Hard, and ARC-AGI-1 (Chollet, 2019), we follow the experimental setups used by HRM (Wang et al., 2025) and TRM (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025), including the released datasets, layer components, objective, and optimization settings. As in HRM and TRM, the fixed puzzle embedding E(x) is supplied at every recurrent application. Maze-Hard and Sudoku-Extreme use exact-grid accuracy; ARC-AGI-1 uses Pass@2. We use these tasks to test whether useful recursive computation can emerge from simple random layer reuse, without the prescribed recurrent structures of HRM and TRM.

## 6 RELATED WORK 

RRM is a simple form of parameter-sharing recursion, rather than a replacement for existing recur-sive or capacity-scaling methods. By removing a fixed layer order, it provides a direct way to study how flexible layer reuse interacts with model capacity, computational depth, and reasoning. Our experiments provide initial evidence at the tasks and scales considered, while broader architectures, scales, and reuse strategies remain open for further study. Below, we first distinguish methods that increase capacity at roughly fixed computation from methods that increase computation through parameter sharing, where RRM fits most directly. We then review work on recursive reasoning, test-time scaling, layer order, and randomized networks. 6.1 SCALING CAPACITY WITHOUT PROPORTIONAL INCREASE IN COMPUTATION 

Capacity-scaling methods increase the number of available parameters while activating only a subset for each input. Mixtures of local experts introduced learned specialization through a gating network (Jacobs et al., 1991), while later conditional-computation methods generalized this idea to avoid evaluating the entire model (Bengio et al., 2013; 2016). Sparsely gated mixture-of-experts mod-els made this approach practical at scale (Shazeer et al., 2017), and subsequent systems expanded model capacity while limiting the number of experts applied to each token (Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022). Later work improved how tokens and experts are assigned (Zhou et al., 2022; Puigcerver et al., 2024). These methods provide access to a large parameter pool without a propor-tional increase in computation. In contrast, RRM changes the reuse order of a fixed set of learned layers at a given computational depth and remains compatible with MoEs, since each reusable layer could itself be an MoE. 6.2 SCALING COMPUTATION WITH SHARED PARAMETERS 

Parameter-sharing methods instead increase computational depth by applying the same parameters multiple times. Neural GPUs use a recurrent convolutional transition to perform multiple compu-tational steps with shared parameters (Łukasz Kaiser & Sutskever, 2016). Universal Transformers apply the same principle to self-attention by repeatedly applying a shared transition across depth (Dehghani et al., 2019), while ALBERT uses cross-layer parameter sharing to prevent the parameter count from growing with network depth (Lan et al., 2020). More recently, looped and recursive Transformers have been studied for iterative algorithms, length generalization, parameter-efficient language modeling, latent reasoning, and adaptive computational depth (Yang et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2025a; Geiping et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2025b; Zhu et al., 2026; Sapunov, 2026; Jeddi et al., 2026). Although these approaches differ in how depth is trained or allocated, they repeatedly apply one transformation or a fixed sequence of transformations. RRM retains parameter sharing across depth but samples each application from a pool of learned layers. 6.3 RECURSIVE REASONING AND TEST -T IME SCALING 

Recursive reasoning methods refine an internal state over multiple steps using shared parameters. Recurrent Relational Networks showed that repeated relational updates can solve tasks including Sudoku (Palm et al., 2018). Later work found that recurrent networks trained on easier problems can extrapolate to harder instances by using additional recurrences at inference, but also identi-fied overthinking , where performance deteriorates when recurrence extends too far beyond training (Schwarzschild et al., 2021; Bansal et al., 2022). More recently, the Hierarchical Reasoning Model and Tiny Recursive Model have shown that compact recurrent networks can solve difficult Sudoku, maze, and abstraction tasks (Wang et al., 2025; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). RRM instead studies a simpler form of recursive reasoning, where layers are trained under random reuse and may learn computations that other models encode through a prescribed recurrent structure. 9Varying the number of recurrent steps also connects recursive reasoning to test-time scaling. Test-time scaling can allocate additional inference computation through search and output refinement (Snell et al., 2024). Recursive models can instead scale latent computation by increasing recurrent depth (Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). RRM provides this form of scaling through randomly reused layers. In the basic RRM, T is set directly and can be increased during training or at test time without adding learned parameters. RRM also supports probabilistic test-time scaling without retraining (Sghaier et al., 2026). 6.4 LAYER ORDER AND RANDOMIZED NETWORKS 

Many methods alter the computational path through a network. Early-exit and layer-skipping meth-ods shorten a fixed sequence of layers while preserving its relative order (Teerapittayanon et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019). PathNet and Routing Networks select task-specific paths through reusable modules (Fernando et al., 2017; Rosenbaum et al., 2017), while Modular Networks and Distributed Neural Architectures learn input- or token-dependent compositions that may change module order (Kirsch et al., 2018; Cowsik et al., 2025). Other work searches at test time for ways to skip or repeat contiguous blocks of a pretrained language model (Li et al., 2025). These ap-proaches either retain a constrained order, specialize routes at the task level, or learn discrete routing decisions, whose non-differentiability complicates end-to-end training. Randomized-network methods instead introduce stochasticity without learning an explicit routing policy. LayerShuffle randomly permutes all layers once per batch to make a full-size Vision Trans-former robust to reordering (Freiberger et al., 2024), whereas RRM samples with replacement per example and step, enabling T̸ = L, parameter-efficient recursion, and inference-depth scaling. Dropout randomly masks individual activations (Srivastava et al., 2014). At the level of depth, stochastic depth and LayerDrop randomly omit entire layers, while Swapout and FractalNet sample paths through larger fixed architectures (Huang et al., 2016; Fan et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2016; Larsson et al., 2017). These methods vary which fixed components are active while preserving their relative order. At the level of network topology, randomly wired neural networks sample graph con-nectivity before training, after which the sampled graph defines the architecture (Xie et al., 2019). RRM instead samples a layer at each step, allowing arbitrary order and reuse without learning or searching for a route. 

## 7 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We introduced the Random Recursive Model (RRM), which replaces fixed-order recurrence with random reuse of a small set of learned layers. RRM matches or exceeds the corresponding baselines, often with substantial reductions in parameter count. Separating the number of learned layers from the number of applications also allows RRM to scale computation without increasing model size. Additional inference time applications improve performance on Maze-Hard and Sudoku-Extreme, while gains plateau or eventually decline on tasks that require less computation. Monte Carlo infer-ence and probabilistic test-time scaling provide further improvements without retraining. Together, these results suggest that random layer reuse acts as an effective form of regularization, improving generalization while mitigating overparameterization. Future work should study how the sampling distribution and the diversity of sampled sequences affect learning. We also studied learned sampling policies, but did not obtain useful gains (Ap-pendix A.3). Making learned ordering work remains an important direction, since an adaptive pol-icy could reduce computation through earlier exiting and improve parameter efficiency by using a smaller subset of reusable layers. Future work should also study how scaling the number of layers and applications behaves in larger models and tasks. More fundamentally, it remains unclear why random layer reuse is effective, and studying its theoretical foundations is left for future work. More broadly, these findings may guide the design of networks that retain flexible layer reuse while being even more computationally efficient. 10 ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

BROADER IMPACTS AND AI S AFETY 

RRM composes reusable modules by passing a shared hidden state through a sampled order. Al-though our experiments use layers in small task-specific models, these modules could be replaced by larger agents, making the hidden state an implicit channel between them. Random or learned ordering may improve flexible reasoning, but may also produce difficult-to-monitor coordination or unsafe behavior. Future work that extends RRM to agents, learns ordering policies, or searches over network distributions should therefore constrain possible interactions, record routing decisions, and evaluate safety before deployment.

Panels (b) and (c) of Figure 6 show that the RRM trained with Ttrain = 8 remains close to its trained-depth performance at Ttest = 12 , but deteriorates when extended further. The RRM trained with Ttrain = 16 is stable for Ttest ∈ { 16 , 20 , 24 }. The stable test-time range therefore depends on the depth encountered during training. Deeper training supports a wider range of test-time applications, but neither RRM improves consistently simply by running longer. 

Monte Carlo inference. Finally, we repeat the test-time sweep for the RRM trained with Ttrain =8 using M = 16 sampled sequences per image. As shown in Figure 6(d), Monte Carlo inference raises the accuracy at each tested depth, but it does not prevent the degradation far beyond the training depth. Averaging multiple layer sequences therefore improves accuracy, but does not make additional test-time applications consistently beneficial. These observations should be treated as specific to CIFAR-10. Maze-Hard and Sudoku-Extreme instead improve beyond their training depths, while Zebra and ARC-AGI-1 largely plateaus. The useful test-time range therefore depends on the task and training setup, with additional recurrence eventually becoming unhelpful or detrimental in some settings.
