## Abstract 

Architecture attribute predictors reduce the cost of neural architecture search by estimating accuracy or latency from a small set of evaluated candidates. The key difficulty is that neural architectures are operation-labeled DAGs: edge direction defines computational flow, but existing predictors often treat direction as a local structural cue. We propose DTA-GT, a Direction- and Topology-Aware Graph Transformer based on encoder-wide directional consistency , which preserves directed computational semantics across node initialization, global spectral encod-ing, pairwise interaction, and feature update. DTA-GT realizes this principle with direction- and topology-aware node initialization, Magnetic Laplacian spectral encoding, direction-dual structural attention, and a Direction-Sensitive MoE-FFN. Across accuracy and latency prediction on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201, DTA-GT consistently outperforms representative sequence-, GNN-, Transformer-, and hybrid predictors under limited-budget settings. Ablations and Magnetic spec-tral controls support the contribution of the direction-aware stages and orientation-sensitive spectral information, while mechanistic diagnostics characterize module behavior. These results support encoder-wide directional consistency as an effective design principle for architecture DAG representation.

## 1 Introduction 

Deep learning has been widely applied across various fields [1–6]. Neural architecture design is central to task performance and deployment efficiency, and Neural Architecture Search (NAS) automates this process by searching a candidate architecture space for models that meet performance and deployment constraints [7–12]. However, evaluating candidates during search is expensive: accuracy typically requires full network training, while latency requires hardware measurement or profiling. A practical way to reduce this cost is to train predictors under a limited evaluation budget, where only a small subset of candidate architectures is evaluated and the remaining unseen candidates are estimated by the predictor [13–16].

Learning-based predictors therefore rely on continuous representations of candidate architectures. This representation problem is nontrivial be-cause a neural architecture is an operation-labeled directed acyclic graph (DAG), rather than a mere undirected connectivity pattern. A directed edge u → v indicates that the output of operation u feeds into operation v,so the representation should preserve directed information flow. For accu-racy and latency prediction, this requires capturing operator composition along directed paths, branch splitting and merging, and reachability-related dependencies. If direction is discarded, the graph skeleton may still be retained, but upstream/downstream roles, split/merge structures, and forward dependencies can be blurred. Table 1 provides a design-oriented comparison of representative predictor families with respect to explicit topology and direction modeling. Despite the need to preserve directed computational semantics, existing predictors cover these aspects only partially. Sequence-based methods [8, 17, 18] linearize architectures and can obscure explicit DAG topology and edge-direction semantics. GNN-based predictors [13, 19–22] explicitly model architecture graphs and local edge directions, but higher-order topology and long-range directed dependencies rely on repeated local message passing, where global structural and directional patterns can be weakened. Transformer and hybrid methods [14– 16, 23–25] use self-attention to capture long-range interactions and inject topology-aware cues through operation/topology tokenization, graph-aided attention, relation masks, or bidirectional aggregation. However, these designs still treat direction as scattered structural cues rather than a coordinated modeling principle. They provide partial directional signals, but usually leave them local or stage-specific rather than combining topology encoding, local direction cues, a global direction prior, and direction-aware updates in one encoder. Thus, the main gap is not whether graph structure is used, but whether directional semantics are modeled consistently across the encoder. The challenge is therefore to preserve the asymmetry induced by directed computation while still capturing DAG topology, motivating the following question: 

How can a neural architecture encoder explicitly model directed computational semantics while capturing DAG topology? 

Our key insight is that directionality in architecture DAGs should be preserved throughout represen-tation learning, rather than injected once as a structural cue. We define encoder-wide directional consistency as the requirement that directed computational semantics be preserved across node-role encoding, global direction-aware spectral encoding, pairwise interaction, and feature update. Without such consistency, direction-aware cues introduced at one stage may be diluted by later direction-agnostic transformations. This motivates preserving direction across the main encoder stages rather than relying on an isolated structural signal. We propose DTA-GT, a Direction- and Topology-Aware Graph Transformer designed to preserve directed computational semantics throughout the encoder. DTA-GT realizes encoder-wide directional consistency through four coordinated modules: direction- and topology-aware node initialization 2makes directed node roles available from the input stage; Magnetic Laplacian spectral encoding supplies a global edge-orientation-sensitive spectral prior; direction-dual structural attention guides pairwise interaction with separated directional relation channels; and a Direction-Sensitive MoE-FFN preserves predecessor/successor distinctions during feature update. Together, these modules prevent directional information from being reduced to an isolated structural cue. We evaluate DTA-GT on four prediction settings spanning accuracy and latency prediction over NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201. Across multiple limited-budget settings and evaluation metrics, DTA-GT consistently improves over representative predictors. Stage-wise ablations assess the contribution of the coordinated direction-aware stages, Magnetic spectral controls isolate the effect of the edge-orientation-sensitive spectral prior, and mechanistic diagnostics examine whether the internal behaviors align with the intended directed computational semantics. Our contributions are: • We formulate encoder-wide directional consistency as a design principle for architecture DAG representation, shifting direction modeling from isolated structural cues to coordinated preservation of directed computational semantics throughout the encoder. • We introduce DTA-GT, which integrates direction- and topology-aware node initialization, Magnetic Laplacian spectral encoding, direction-dual structural attention, and a Direction-Sensitive MoE-FFN. These modules jointly model directed node roles, a global edge-orientation-sensitive spectral prior, relation-aware interactions, and predecessor/successor-aware updates, thereby strengthening topology-aware architecture representations and pro-viding a stronger inductive bias for limited-budget prediction. • We conduct a unified evaluation on accuracy and latency prediction over standard NAS benchmarks, with stage-wise ablations, Magnetic spectral controls, and mechanistic diag-nostics to analyze the contribution and behavior of the proposed direction-aware stages. 

## 2 Related Work 

Existing architecture predictors mainly follow three representation paradigms: sequence-based encodings, GNN-based graph modeling, and Transformer/hybrid architectures. These paradigms differ in how they encode topology and direction, and we focus on whether they preserve directed computational semantics beyond local structural cues. 

Sequence-based Methods. Peephole [17], NAO [18], and PNAS [8] encode architectures as flattened vectors or operation sequences. This paradigm prioritizes compact architecture descriptions and efficient predictor training under limited evaluation budgets. However, because the DAG is linearized, these methods do not explicitly model topology or preserve edge-direction semantics. As a result, directed computational dependencies among operations are difficult to capture. 

GNN-based Methods. BRP-NAS [13], Neural Predictor [19], NPENAS [22], GATES [20], Semi-NAS [28], DNNPerf [29], and NNLP [21] represent architectures as DAGs or graph-structured objects. Compared with sequence encodings, GNNs explicitly use graph topology and can exploit edge directions within local neighborhoods. However, in message-passing backbones, higher-order topology and long-range dependencies are mainly captured through repeated local propagation, where information can be mixed, over-smoothed, or over-squashed. Meanwhile, directional bias remains mostly local, and an explicit global direction-aware prior is usually absent. 

Transformer-based and Hybrid Methods. Transformer-based and hybrid predictors, including TNASP [24], NAR-Former [14], PINAT [25], FlowerFormer [23], NAR-Former V2 [15], and NN-Former [16], use self-attention to model global interactions among architecture tokens. To compensate for the lack of explicit graph structure in vanilla self-attention, recent designs inject topology-aware signals through operation/topology tokenization, graph-aided attention, relation masks, or bidirectional aggregation. These designs improve topology-aware representation learning, but their directional cues remain mostly local or stage-specific, without jointly coordinating a global direction-aware spectral prior, relation-aware interaction, and direction-specific updates. 31 3 4 5

## 3 Problem Formulation and Model Overview 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

Following established conventions [14–16, 21, 24], let A be the candidate architecture space. Each ar-chitecture a ∈ A is represented as an operation-labeled directed acyclic graph (DAG) G = ( V, E, τ ),where nodes represent computational operations, directed edges encode data-flow dependencies, and 

τ : V → O assigns an operation type to each node. We adopt the adjacency convention that Au,v = 1 

indicates a directed edge u → v.Each architecture has a scalar target attribute y ∈ R, such as accuracy or latency. Given a measured subset D = {(ai, y i)}Ni=1 sampled from A, we learn a predictor fϑ : A → R to estimate attributes for remaining unmeasured candidates in the same architecture space, without fully training or profiling each candidate. We decompose fϑ into an encoder hϕ and a prediction head pψ : zG = hϕ(G) and 

ˆy = pψ (zG), with ϑ = {ϕ, ψ }. The challenge is to preserve operation semantics, graph topology, and directed computational semantics across the encoder, rather than allowing directional information to be lost after a single encoding stage. 

3.2 Overall Architecture 

Fig. 2 illustrates DTA-GT, which maps an architecture DAG G to a graph-level representation 

zG = hϕ(G) under the encoder-wide directional consistency principle. Specifically, the encoder first initializes node states as H(0) = NodeEmbed( G) + DirSpecEnc( G), combining operation/local directed-role features with a global edge-orientation-sensitive spectral prior. It then applies L residual layers [30] alternating direction-dual structural attention and direction-sensitive MoE-FFN updates, using RG and A for relation-aware interaction and predecessor/successor-aware updates. Finally, attention-based readout produces zG = AttnReadout( H(L)), which is passed to pψ for prediction without altering the original architecture DAG. 

## 4 Method 

4.1 Direction- and Topology-Aware Node Initialization 

Before global spectral encoding and attention-based interaction, each node should carry a basic description of its computation and its directed role in the architecture DAG. Operation labels specify the computation at each node, but they do not distinguish nodes with the same operation type that appear in different local structures or input-to-output positions. Topological-order indices can provide positional cues, but they may introduce ordering artifacts because a DAG can admit multiple 4valid topological orders [14, 15, 25]. We therefore initialize nodes with topological-order-invariant attributes that encode computation, local directed roles, and input-to-output positions. 

Role-aware attributes. Each node vi is described by five discrete features: operation label τ (vi),indegree deg −(vi), outdegree deg +(vi), forward depth ℓfwd (vi), and reverse depth ℓrev (vi). The forward depth is the maximum length of a directed path from an input node to vi, and the reverse depth is the maximum length of a directed path from vi to an output node. The operation label captures computational semantics, indegree and outdegree characterize local merging and branching roles, and (ℓfwd , ℓ rev ) locates the node along the directed input-to-output flow. This depth pair distinguishes nodes with the same forward depth but different remaining distances to the output side. These features make local in/out roles and input-to-output positions available before attention-based interaction. We embed, concatenate, and project them:

4.2 Magnetic Laplacian Spectral Encoding 

The node initialization X provides operation semantics and local directed roles, but not a global direction-aware spectral prior over the architecture DAG. Existing predictors usually lack an explicit global direction-aware spectral prior [13–16, 19–21, 23–25], while standard Laplacian encodings capture symmetric connectivity but not the asymmetry between u → v and v → u. We therefore derive P from the Magnetic Laplacian [31, 32], whose complex phase records edge orientation. This supplies the global spectral stage of encoder-wide directional consistency.

It organizes pairwise interactions through six directional relation channels. 4.4 Direction-Sensitive Mixture-of-Experts Feed-Forward Network 

After structural attention, a standard position-wise FFN applies the same node-wise transformation to all nodes and does not distinguish predecessor from successor contexts. Such direction-agnostic updates can weaken the directional distinctions introduced by the preceding attention layer. We therefore replace the standard FFN with a Direction-Sensitive MoE-FFN, which uses three role-specific experts for the node’s own state, predecessor context, and successor context. A node-wise routing gate adaptively weights these experts according to the current node state. This keeps the feature-update stage direction-aware without assigning a fixed expert to every node role, since the useful predecessor/successor balance can vary across architectures and prediction targets.

5.3 Main Results 

Table 3 compares all methods across the four tasks and training budgets. DTA-GT achieves the highest Kendall’s τ and the lowest MAPE across all four tasks and training budgets, showing consistent improvements in both candidate ranking and attribute estimation. These gains support the value of pre-serving directed computational semantics in architecture representations. Operator composition along directed paths, branch splitting and merging, and reachability-related dependencies describe how computations are organized within an architecture. DTA-GT explicitly models upstream/downstream roles and directional relations across its encoder stages, providing an inductive bias for capturing these computational semantics in both accuracy and latency prediction. Its consistent gains in both accuracy and latency prediction support encoder-wide directional modeling across architecture attributes. 

5.4 Magnetic Spectral Encoding Analysis 

Table 4 isolates the spectral-encoding stage by comparing no directional encoding, symmetric Laplacian controls, and Magnetic Laplacian encoding in the directed encoder. Magnetic Laplacian encoding performs best on all reported metrics, whereas symmetric Laplacian encoding gives limited and task-dependent gains. Replacing Magnetic Laplacian with symmetric Laplacian inside the directed encoder also weakens performance, suggesting that the spectral benefit mainly comes from preserving edge-orientation asymmetry rather than adding generic undirected spectral information. ## 6 Discussion 

Limitations. Our architecture-prediction evaluation focuses on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201. Transfer to other search spaces requires aligned operation vocabularies and dependency semantics, since graph nodes may represent computations at different granularities. Dense directed-relation masks and pairwise attention also incur quadratic storage and attention costs as graph size grows. The effect of improved predictor ranking on end-to-end NAS remains to be evaluated. 

Future work. We will investigate a shared operation-level representation for cross-space pretraining and adaptation, and explore sparse or hierarchical attention with compact reachability representations to support larger computational graphs. We also plan to integrate DTA-GT into NAS pipelines and evaluate the selected architectures and total search cost under a fixed end-to-end evaluation budget. Appendix G elaborates on these directions. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented DTA-GT, a Direction- and Topology-Aware Graph Transformer for neural architecture attribute prediction. DTA-GT is built on encoder-wide directional consistency, which preserves directed computational semantics across node initialization, global direction-aware spectral en-coding, pairwise interaction, and feature update. By combining direction- and topology-aware node initialization, Magnetic Laplacian spectral encoding, direction-dual structural attention, and a Direction-Sensitive MoE-FFN, DTA-GT strengthens topology-aware architecture representations while explicitly modeling edge-direction semantics. Experiments on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201 demonstrate consistent gains on accuracy and latency prediction across limited-budget settings. Ablations, Magnetic spectral controls, and mechanistic diagnostics further support the contribution and intended behavior of the proposed direction-aware stages.

A.1 Implementation Platform 

The NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201 attribute-prediction experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.4.0 and conducted on a workstation running Ubuntu 22.04 with an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU, 64 GB RAM, and a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB memory. The large-DAG scalability experiments are conducted separately on an NVIDIA A800-SXM4-80GB GPU, with the corresponding hardware configuration and timing protocol reported in Appendix F. 

A.2 Datasets, Splits, and Training Budgets 

NAS-Bench-101 [34] contains 423,624 unique cell-based architectures with at most 7 nodes and 9 edges per cell. NAS-Bench-201 [35] contains 15,625 candidate cells with 4 nodes and 6 edges per cell. For NAS-Bench-201, we represent edge operations as nodes in the predictor input graph, whose encoding topology is fixed across operation assignments. The NAS-Bench-201 authors trained candidate architectures on CIFAR-10 for 200 epochs and recorded their validation accuracies, alongside latency measurements collected with a batch size of 256. These measurements serve as labels for our accuracy and latency prediction tasks, with accuracy averaged over the available benchmark runs for each architecture. NAS-Bench-101 provides accuracy labels but not latency labels; therefore, we measure NAS-Bench-101 latency on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU using batch size 1 to complete the four-task evaluation. Because latency labels are benchmark- and device-specific, latency magnitudes are compared only within the same benchmark setting. For each task and training budget, each method is evaluated over five runs using a shared set of five random seeds. For each seed, architectures are randomly shuffled to form the training and validation partitions. The partitions vary across seeds, and all methods share the same partitions for a given seed. The @ notation in the result tables denotes the number of architectures used for predictor fitting, and the validation set contains 1,024 architectures. Because the predictor serves the candidate screening stage of NAS, its objective is to score and rank the complete architecture pool and identify promising candidates for costly downstream evaluation. We therefore compute Kendall’s τ and MAPE over the full benchmark architecture set, including the training and validation architectures and all remaining candidates. 

A.3 Fair Comparison and Reproducibility 

Within each seed, all methods use the same training and validation partitions, full-set evaluation target, training budgets, and metrics. When reproducing baselines, we use the official implementations when available and preserve their original model configurations; otherwise, hyperparameters are selected by grid search within the ranges reported by the corresponding papers. DTA-GT uses one fixed default configuration across tasks and budgets. The main comparison and ablation tables report means over five runs; Appendix H also reports the corresponding standard deviations. 

A.4 Descriptions of Baselines 

We compare DTA-GT with representative sequence-, CNN-, GNN-, Transformer-, and hybrid architecture predictors. For methods with public source code, we use the released implementation as the primary reference. For methods for which we did not identify an official public implementation, we reproduce the predictor from the paper description and tune only within the reported or standard ranges under the shared split, budget, and evaluation protocol described above. 

Sequence- and CNN-based predictors. NAO [18] encodes architectures as operation sequences with an encoder–predictor–decoder framework and performs prediction in a continuous architecture embedding space. ReNAS [36] represents architectures with handcrafted tensor features and trains a relativistic predictor that focuses on ranking architectures rather than only regressing absolute performance. These baselines test whether explicit graph modeling is necessary beyond sequence or tensor encodings.

## C Complexity Analysis 

Let n and m denote the numbers of nodes and directed edges, respectively. Let d denote the hidden dimension, k the number of retained spectral modes, and L the number of encoder layers. We treat the number of attention heads and the six directional relation channels as constants. 

C.1 Time Complexity 

Degrees, directed depths, and a sparse Magnetic Laplacian can be constructed in O(n + m) time from a sparse DAG representation. A dense construction of reachability and common-neighbor relations has O(n3) worst-case time and O(n2) storage, and a full dense Hermitian eigendecomposition has the same worst-case time and storage orders. These graph-dependent quantities are computed once and reused during subsequent encoder evaluations. Aggregating k spectral modes per node costs O(nkd 2 + nk 2d), which becomes O(nd 2) for fixed k.Each encoder layer uses O(nd 2) work for feature projections and O(n2d) work for dense masked attention. When adjacency is represented densely, the directional context updates remain within the same O(n2d + nd 2) per-layer bound. The resulting encoder-evaluation cost is 

Teval = O nkd 2 + nk 2d + L(n2d + nd 2) . (12) The first-use cost adds graph-dependent construction to encoder evaluation: 

Tfirst = Trelations + Tspectral + Tfeatures + Teval . (13) With fixed k and dense preprocessing, the conservative first-use bound is O(n3 + L(n2d + nd 2)) .

C.2 Space Complexity 

Dense relation masks require O(n2) storage, retained eigenvectors require O(nk ), node states require 

O(nd ), and encoder parameters require O(Ld 2). For inference at batch size one, these terms give 

Minference = O(n2 + nk + nd + Ld 2). (14) A conventional backward pass that retains per-layer attention and node activations adds O(L(n2+nd )) 

storage. The measured GPU memory in Appendix F.2 complements these asymptotic bounds for the profiled implementation. 

C.3 Design Choices for Practical Scalability 

The analysis above identifies two scale-dependent costs: graph-dependent construction at first use and dense pairwise storage and computation during encoder evaluation. DTA-GT addresses these costs through three complementary execution choices that preserve the same direction-aware representation. 

Sparse retained-mode spectral computation. The large-graph spectral path represents the Mag-netic Laplacian sparsely and uses thick-restart Lanczos to compute only the retained eigenpairs through sparse matrix-vector products in a compact Krylov subspace. It therefore constructs the spectral modes consumed by the encoder without forming a complete spectral basis, with runtime determined by graph sparsity, solver tolerance, and the iterations required for convergence. 19 Reusable graph-dependent preprocessing. The retained eigenpairs and directional relation masks depend only on the architecture graph, so they are constructed once and reused across encoder layers, training epochs, and repeated predictions of the same architecture. This design separates graph-input construction from model evaluation and amortizes structural and spectral construction whenever an architecture is revisited. 

Topology- and support-aware relation handling. Deployment-oriented architecture design bal-ances predictive quality against computational cost, making sparse-to-moderately-dense connectivity a practically important regime for support-aware execution. The practical motivation for support-aware relation handling is that computational graphs exhibit structured, architecture-dependent connectivity rather than a single density regime. ResNet-style architectures introduce selected shortcut edges, whereas DenseNet-style blocks create a denser support pattern by connecting each layer to all preceding layers within a block. Deriving relation supports from the observed topology preserves this variation and provides the structural basis for sparse execution whenever the graph admits it. The six directional relations expose their supported node pairs explicitly, allowing relation construc-tion to exploit known graph structure. Let S denote the total number of supported pairs across the six relations. A support-indexed attention implementation evaluates only stored supported pairs, yielding an O(Sd ) attention term, while sparse predecessor and successor aggregation costs O(md ).The explicit relation supports therefore provide the execution interface for support-proportional computation as graph scale increases. Appendix D first reports model-level resource costs on NAS-Bench-201, while Appendix F profiles predictive accuracy, spectral and relation construction, model forward, and GPU memory on real and generated DAGs. The staged large-DAG profiles separately report graph-input construction costs, model-forward latency, and peak forward GPU memory.

## E Design Rationale and Parameter Sensitivity 

DTA-GT uses three structurally defined update paths for the node’s own state, predecessor context, and successor context. The number of experts follows this decomposition of directed computational context. A node-dependent router learns adaptive mixing weights over the three contexts from the current node representation. This allows each node to combine its local state with upstream and downstream information according to the prediction task. The four directional stages provide complementary information throughout the encoder. Node initial-ization represents local directed roles, and Magnetic spectral encoding supplies a global orientation-sensitive prior. Structural attention organizes interactions by directed node-pair relations, while the MoE-FFN adapts each node update to its self, predecessor, and successor contexts. Together, these stages preserve computational direction from the input representation through pairwise interaction and feature updates. Source and branching nodes distribute information along downstream paths, whereas converging and sink nodes summarize upstream computation. These roles motivate separate predecessor and successor contexts, with the learned gate adapting their relative contributions at each node. The routing diagnostics in Appendix B examine how these weights vary across node roles and prediction targets. Baseline sources and reproduction strategies are described in Appendix A. DTA-GT is optimized with AdamW using a batch size of 128, a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, and weight decay of 0.01 for at most 3000 epochs. For both accuracy and latency prediction, early stopping monitors validation Kendall’s τ

21 with a patience of 100, and evaluation uses the checkpoint with the highest validation τ . The learning rate is linearly warmed up over the first 10% of training steps and then follows a cosine decay schedule. The default DTA-GT model uses embedding dimension d = 180 , backbone depth L = 10 , and loss weights λmse = 1 , λrank = 0 .2, and λaux = 0 .001 . This default configuration was set a priori by following the model scale and optimizer choices commonly used in closely related Transformer-based predictors, including NAR-Former [14], NAR-Former V2 [15], and NN-Former [16]. We use the same configuration across all tasks and budgets without task- or budget-specific tuning. The shared configuration is (q, k, L, d ) = (0 .25 , 4, 10 , 180) for both benchmarks, both prediction targets, and all reported NAS training budgets. The q sensitivity experiment is reported alongside the Magnetic mechanism analysis in Appendix B.1 and Table 6. That experiment and the k, L,and d experiments below assess sensitivity around the configuration fixed a priori; we retain the same defaults for the main comparisons without task- or budget-specific parameter selection. ## G Extended Discussion 

Cross-space representation and adaptation. A node in a cell-based CNN space may denote a primitive convolution or pooling operation, whereas a node in another space may denote an entire Transformer layer or Mamba block. These different computational granularities motivate a canonical operation-level representation for cross-space adaptation. Composite blocks could be decomposed into shared semantic units or mapped to a common operation vocabulary with aligned edge definitions and target protocols. For spaces with compatible operation definitions and dependency semantics, future work can investigate reusing DTA-GT’s directional encoder and graph preprocessing, with new vocabulary entries and embeddings for additional operation types. This would enable studies of cross-space pretraining followed by adaptation on a small evaluated subset from the target space. 

Task adaptation and NAS evaluation. For a new DAG-level task with compatible atomic units and directed-dependency semantics, adapting the prediction head and fine-tuning the encoder on a small labeled subset provides a natural extension. This would allow the same direction-aware representation principle to be studied under different graph-level targets. A complementary direction is to integrate the predictor into a NAS search loop and evaluate how candidate ranking affects architecture selection under a fixed end-to-end evaluation budget. 

Relation-channel design and extension. The three paired relation families in Section 4.3 are heuristic choices motivated by DAG semantics, providing a concrete instantiation of direction-dual structural attention. They capture local dependencies, multi-hop reachability, and shared predeces-sor/successor structure. Additional directional information, such as hop-specific dependencies or directed motif patterns, can be represented by further ordered or role-specific relation supports, with a corresponding extension of the relation-to-head assignment. The relation set can therefore be tailored to the directional semantics available in a target graph domain. Our experiments evaluate the six-channel instantiation; the predictive value and computational cost of expanded relation sets remain to be studied. 

Computational scaling. Directed reachability can grow quadratically even when the original adjacency is sparse. For example, a directed chain has n − 1 edges but n(n − 1) /2 reachable ordered pairs. Dense relation masks therefore require quadratic storage, and dense masked attention incurs quadratic pairwise computation for a fixed hidden dimension. The A800 profiles in Appendix F.2 demonstrate execution on generated DAGs with up to 32,768 nodes. Extending Graph Transformer predictors to substantially larger computational graphs calls for sparse or hierarchical attention together with compact representations of directed reachability. Combining these mechanisms with reusable spectral preprocessing could extend the encoder to larger computational graphs while retaining its direction-aware representation. Future evaluations should measure prediction quality, first-use graph-input construction, model-forward latency, and GPU memory under matched graph and hardware settings.

The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be. • The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs.
