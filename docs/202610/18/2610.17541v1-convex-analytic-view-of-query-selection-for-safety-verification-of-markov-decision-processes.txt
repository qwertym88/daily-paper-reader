Abstract 

Safety verification of a Markov decision process (MDP) asks whether the probabilities of reaching several failure states jointly lie in a convex safe region under every scheduler. Watanabe and Unno (2026) prove safety by refining an outer approximation of the failure probabilities, where each query bounds a nonnegative weighted sum of the probabilities. Their rule selects weights by the largest Euclidean violation. We present a unified framework to measure violation in an explicitly chosen error unit: directions are scored by the support-function gap between the outer approximation and the safe region, normalized by the support function of the unit. We show that the maximal score, when positive, equals the smallest enlargement of the safe region in multiples of that unit that contains the outer approximation. The Euclidean unit recovers the earlier rule; two new rules are generated by the unit cube and the safe region itself. We experimentally evaluate how the choice of measurement rule affects the performance of safety verification for MDPs. 

# 1 Introduction 

Consider a robot that carries out a mission consisting of several tasks. At each step it chooses a route or an operating mode, the environment changes at random, and each task may end in a catastrophe of one of d

kinds, such as a collision, a fall, or a loss of communication; the mission ends when all tasks are completed or when a catastrophe occurs. A safety requirement constrains the probabilities p1, . . . , p d of the d catastrophes jointly: each pi may be at most a threshold θi, or a severity-weighted total P 

> i

wipi may be at most a threshold θ. The permissible vectors (p1, . . . , p d) then form a safe region, represented by a nonempty compact convex set. The requirement has to hold not only for one chosen control policy but for every way of resolving the choices, because the choices may be taken by a component whose behavior is unknown or adversarial. The robot and environment form a Markov decision process, and a scheduler resolves the choices; then we aim to ensure that the vector of failure probabilities of every scheduler lies in the safe region. The exact set of failure probabilities is unavailable a priori due to infinitely many schedulers and the huge state space. Here, counterexample-guided abstraction refinement (CEGAR) (Ball and Rajamani, 2002) is useful, which iteratively synthesizes the largest weighted probability. By reincarnating the idea of Hörmander (1955), we measure the set difference by the

hU (w) − hS (w). To compare violations across directions in a chosen geometry, we normalize the support gap to consider (hU − hS )/h E , where E is a user-chosen convex set, called an error unit . If E is the nonnegative part of the Euclidean unit ball, the normalizer hE (w) equals ∥w∥2, and we recover the projection rule of Watanabe and Unno (2026). If E is the unit cube [0 , 1] d, the normalizer hE (w) equals ∥w∥1, which is one whenever the entries of w sum to one. If E = S, the normalizer is the support function hS of the safe region itself, and the largest value of the normalized support gap is expressed through the gauge function of S.We show that the query based on E = [0 , 1] d and E = S can typically be computed more efficiently, which actually accelerates safety verification empirically. # 2 Related Work 

We consider safety verification through the lens of the set containment problem . Its basic form is to ask whether a convex set K (representing the system’s exact behavior) is a subset of another convex set S

(describing safety conditions), which has been studied from various angles. When K is completely known, the containment test is relatively easy. Specifically, general polyhedral or quadratic set containment is well-characterized via duality (Mangasarian, 2002; Jeyakumar, 2003), and its computational complexity depends on the structures of K and S (Gritzmann and Klee, 1994). We usually need to estimate system’s behavior K. While convex set estimation is common in statistical learning (Prince and Willsky, 1990; Guntuboyina, 2012; Hannah and Dunson, 2013), it does not guarantee 

soundness , namely, the estimated ˆK lacks the guarantee K ⊆ ˆK, which is crucial for safety verification. The specificity of safety verification is the downward-closedness assumptions on K and S (see Definition 3.3), which enables soundness guarantee. In this line, sandwich algorithms for convex multi-objective optimization (Solanki 2et al., 1993; Rennen et al., 2011; Lammel and Küfer, 2023) are well-known: therein, we iteratively approximate a Pareto front (or the behavior set K) by maintaining polyhedral inner and outer approximations. At each iteration, we solve a linearly scalarized problem, which yields a new supporting half-space to minimize the Hausdorff distance between the inner and outer approximations. Sandwich algorithms have been used for multi-objective probabilistic model checking of MDPs (Forejt et al., 2012; Quatmann, 2023; Hartmanns et al., 2026). Whereas sandwich algorithms are common, it does not scale to higher dimensions, limited up to 2 or 3 dims. More severely, they estimate a Pareto front without leveraging structures of the safe region S. Watanabe and Unno (2026) solve these issues based on a CEGAR framework (Ball and Rajamani, 2002; Clarke et al., 2003) for safety verification of probabilistic programs. In particular, they refine the outer approximation by selecting a query with the largest Euclidean violation, effectively leveraging structures of the safe region S.This is the closest work to ours, and we generalize their query selection from the convex-analytic viewpoint.

# 5 Unified Query Selection 

To test the set containment, we use support functions extensively. For nonempty compact convex U, S ⊆ Rd,the containment U ⊆ S is equivalent to hU (w) ≤ hS (w) for all w ∈ Rd (Rockafellar, 1970, Section 13). Under our downward-closed assumption (Assumption 4.1), we need to only consider the normalized direction w ∈ △ d

to determine the set containment. 

Lemma 5.1. Let B ⊆ Rd 

> +

be nonempty, compact, convex, and downward closed in Rd

> +

> +

be nonempty and compact. Then U ⊆ B holds if and only if hU (w) ≤ hB (w) holds for all w ∈ △ d.Proof. The forward implication follows from the definition of the support function. For the reverse implication, let x ∈ U \ B and let q be its Euclidean nearest point in B. We have q ≤ x: lowering any coordinate of q that exceeds the corresponding coordinate of x preserves membership in B and strictly decreases the distance. The projection inequality gives ⟨x − q, b − q⟩ ≤ 0 for all b ∈ B. Thus x − q is nonzero and nonnegative, and 

w := ( x − q)/∥x − q∥1 satisfies hB (w) = ⟨w, q ⟩ < ⟨w, x ⟩ ≤ hU (w), where the strict inequality follows by x̸ = q.This is a contradiction. By Lemma 5.1, a positive support gap hU (w) − hS (w) identifies a direction w ∈ △ d in which the outer approximation U violates the safe region S. Subsequently, we introduce the normalized support gap to obtain a scale-invariant score for comparing violations across directions in a chosen geometry.

# 7 Conclusion 

We studied a convex-analytic formulation to synthesize a query to test set containment, in particular for downward-closed sets. Empirically, the gauge rule exhibits clear benefits over the L2 rule. We conjecture that the condition number of S affects the query selection, and it is an interesting future work to investigate how it affects the choice of an error unit E.
