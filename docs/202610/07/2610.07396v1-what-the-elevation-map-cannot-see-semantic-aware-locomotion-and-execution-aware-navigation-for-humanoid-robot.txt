# Abstract 

Navigation for humanoid robots is critical, yet large-scale evaluation on physical hardware is often impractical due to cost and safety concerns, making simulation bench-marks essential. Existing VLN benchmarks achieve phys-ically executable navigation, but still assume (1) all haz-ards are observable from elevation maps; (2) realized mo-tions closely match desired motions. In real environ-ments, however, fallen bottles may be ambiguous in el-evation maps, while phones and water spills may be diffi-cult to differentiate; hazard avoidance by the locomotion policy can cause the robot’s actual trajectory to devi-ate from the path intended by the VLN policy. Such command-execution mismatch can accumulate and lead the robot toward unintended locations. To expose these failure modes, we introduce a benchmark that models both elevation-subtle hazards and execution deviations, together with a closed-loop VLN + locomotion control framework that continuously realigns high-level naviga-tion with the robot’s actual state. We evaluate navigation in simulation and further validate the locomotion policy on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Results show that semantic input reduces contact with hazards poorly represented in elevation maps, while anti-deviation im-proves navigation success. These findings highlight the need to evaluate humanoid navigation jointly in terms of route completion and hazard avoidance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Humanoid robots are increasingly expected to assist peo-ple in everyday household environments, where naviga-tion requires moving through rooms and corridors while avoiding furniture and floor-level hazards. Small objects and water spills in these environments may be poorly rep-resented in depth-derived elevation maps, posing chal-lenges beyond geometric obstacle avoidance. Although humanoids offer mobility advantages over wheeled plat-forms, large-scale real-world evaluation is costly, time-1arXiv:2610.07396v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 consuming, and potentially unsafe. Simulation bench-marks therefore enable repeatable, scalable evaluation be-fore physical deployment. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) allows robots to navigate according to natural-language instructions rather than predefined metric goals. Early VLN bench-marks moved agents between predefined viewpoints, ab-stracting away locomotion and obstacle interaction [1]. Continuous-environment VLN replaced this graph-based formulation with local actions in continuous 3D environ-ments [10]. More recently, physics-based benchmarks such as VLN-PE [19], VLNVerse [12], and VLN-CE-Isaac [3] further introduce robot kinematics, collision dy-namics, and continuous motion, advancing VLN toward physically executable navigation. Nevertheless, physics-based VLN evaluation retains two implicit assumptions. First, obstacle observability as-sumes that locomotion hazards are detectable from depth or elevation maps. Yet wet floors and small objects such as phones or cables may have weak or absent geomet-ric signatures, so geometric traversability does not im-ply safety. Second, command-execution consistency as-sumes that realized motion matches high-level commands. Safe humanoid locomotion may instead require sidesteps, shorter steps, or turns. Repeated avoidance can accu-mulate pose and heading errors, shift observations away from those expected by the VLN policy, and undermine navigation. These limitations are coupled: avoiding a semanti-cally detected hazard can alter the trajectory assumed by the VLN policy. Existing benchmarks emphasize geometric traversability and collisions rather than this perception-execution feedback loop. Strong simulation performance may therefore conceal missed non-geometric hazards or lost task progress when subsequent decisions ignore avoidance-induced deviations. We introduce a humanoid VLN benchmark that ex-poses these failure modes through hazards with different levels of geometric observability, including those weakly represented or absent in elevation maps. It distinguishes commanded from realized motion, enabling controlled evaluation of execution deviations caused by safety-aware low-level behavior. This formulation measures whether an agent reaches its language-specified destination while remaining safe and recovering its navigation state after local deviations. We develop a hierarchical framework coupling a high-level VLN module with a semantic-aware whole-body lo-comotion policy. The VLN module generates relative tar-get poses from language instructions and visual observa-tions, while the locomotion policy tracks the derived ve-locity commands with local terrain and hazard awareness. Closed-loop feedback of the robot’s realized state corrects avoidance-induced deviations. We evaluate navigation in simulation and validate locomotion on a physical Unitree G1 with hazards poorly represented by geometry alone. Our contributions are two-fold:

# 2 Related Works 

2.1 Humanoid Navigation Benchmark 

VLN datasets such as RxR [11] and REVERIE [17] extend language and object grounding without modeling hu-manoid execution. HumanoidBench [18] evaluates phys-ical control but not language-guided navigation. Recent physics-based VLN benchmarks incorporate robot em-bodiment and locomotion [19, 3, 12, 16], complementing these language- and control-focused evaluations. These benchmarks advance physical evaluation but cover a more limited range of perturbations and obstacles. We introduce richer perturbation and obstacle settings to jointly diagnose semantic hazards and safety-induced exe-cution deviations. We target hazards poorly represented in elevation maps, such as wet surfaces and small ob-jects, and evaluate safety and navigation recovery when low-level avoidance alters commanded motion. 

2.2 Humanoid Locomotion and Whole-Body Control 

Perpetual Humanoid Control (PHC) [13] provides motion imitation and fall recovery for simulated avatars. ExBody [4] combines upper-body imitation with velocity-tracking locomotion on hardware, while OmniH2O [6] learns de-ployable whole-body control from retargeted human mo-tion and privileged teachers. HOVER [7] unifies velocity, pose, and upper-body control interfaces; ExBody2 [8] and TWIST [23] emphasize motion tracking and coordinated locomotion–manipulation. Their primary focus is refer-ence tracking rather than semantic navigation hazards. Terrain-aware methods incorporate environmental geom-etry. 3.2 Semantic Branch for Privileged Lo-comotion 

Fig. 4 shows our locomotion training curriculum. We first learn how to use semantic hazard information for loco-motion under ground-truth perception, then transfer this capability to a waist camera-based student. 

3.2.1 Augmenting the geometric locomotion pol-icy 

We start from a pretrained AME policy [5] that can al-ready walk and traverse challenging terrain. We retain its network architecture and weights, and add an indepen-dent semantic branch. The geometric branch describes terrain shape, whereas the semantic branch provides a steppability representation that distinguishes safe sup-port from hazardous regions. The teacher generates ac-tions using ground-truth elevation and semantic maps to-gether with proprioceptive input. In the first stage, we augment the challenging terrain with hazards that are visible, ambiguous and invisible in elevation map. We train the augmented policy using PPO with ground-truth maps and two additional reward terms: a step-over clearance reward and a premature-descent penalty (Sec. 3.3). This stage builds semantic-aware hazard avoidance on top of the pretrained terrain-traversal capability. 

3.2.2 Training a robust semantic teacher 

We continue PPO training with curriculum-based ran-domization of dynamics and payload, proprioceptive ob-servation noise, pushes, and external forces, as detailed in Sec. 4.1. The policy retains ground-truth elevation and semantic maps while learning to handle these perturba-tions, yielding a robust semantic teacher.

3.2.3 Distillation for Deployable RGB-D Loco-motion 

The teacher in Section 3.2 already uses semantics to guide locomotion, but depends on ground-truth maps un-available on hardware. As shown in Fig. 5, small haz-ards may be weakly represented in reconstructed eleva-tion maps, while the safety relevance of geometrically flat hazards may not be apparent from height informa-tion alone. RGB observations provide complementary ap-pearance cues for identifying such hazards. We therefore transfer the teacher’s control behavior to a student using onboard RGB-D and proprioception, while retaining the action interface. 

DINO-augmented semantic perception We con-struct a new student from the robust teacher. Its geo-metric branch receives camera depth, while its semantic branch receives RGB and additional visual features ex-tracted by a frozen DINOv2-S backbone [15]. A trainable projection converts the DINO features into additional channels concatenated with the semantic CNN input. At deployment, we use Fast-FoundationStereo (FFS) [20] to obtain cleaner depth observations from stereo image pairs for the geometric branch. # 4 Experiments and Results 

4.1 Experiment Setup 

We evaluate humanoid locomotion in both simulation and physical-robot experiments, while VLN is evaluated only in simulation with physically simulated humanoid execu-tion. Fig. 3 provides an overview of the three environment settings. 

Baselines. Elevation-only is a student that retains only the elevation branch of the AME architecture [5] and has no semantic branch. It is distilled from the same ro-bust semantic teacher (Sec. 3.2) with the same DAgger schedule (Sec. 3.2.3), the same depth-camera and propri-oceptive inputs, and the same perturbations as Ours; the only difference is that Ours additionally receives RGB and DINOv2 features through the semantic branch. This comparison isolates the contribution of semantic percep-tion under identical supervision. The same Elevation-only checkpoint is used in simulation and on the physi-cal robot. Oracle is the semantic teacher with ground-truth elevation and semantic maps. It is evaluated only under plant-side perturbations (dynamics, payload, ex-ternal disturbances, and proprioceptive noise), bypassing perception perturbations: image noise, lighting, surface colors, camera pose, and camera timing (frame hold and latency).

4.1.3 Navigation 

We evaluate navigation only in simulation, using the training and unseen validation splits provided by NaV-ILA VLN-CE-Isaac [3] and automatically augment the scenes with script-generated obstacle placements, includ-ing hazards that are geometrically visible, ambiguous or invisible. For each episode, we place 40% of the added ob-stacles on the desired navigation path and the remaining 60% within 1 .0 m of the path. By concentrating obsta-cles on and near the reference route, this placement strat-egy creates a challenging benchmark for hazard avoidance along both the intended route and nearby detours. 

4.2 Locomotion Result 

4.2.1 Simulation Results 

We compare our semantic-aware policy (Ours) with Elevation-only and Oracle, as defined in Sec. 4.1. Table 5 reports three complementary metrics. Success rate (SR ↑) is the percentage of episodes that complete the full terrain-specific target distance without leaving the corridor, falling, or stepping on any obstacle or hazard. Foot contact with any hazard class— elevation-visible, elevation-ambiguous, or elevation-invisible—counts as failure, even if the robot remains upright. Progress ( ↑)is the farthest distance reached divided by the target dis-tance, capped at one and averaged over all episodes, ex-pressed as a percentage; it captures partial completion even in failed trials. Velocity tracking error (VTE ↓) mea-sures the discrepancy between commanded and realized velocity, capturing command-following accuracy rather than task completion or safety. Episodes terminate on hazard contact, fall, or leaving the corridor, so SR, HCR, and FR sum to 100% up to out-of-corridor terminations; these are rare (at most 1 .7%, Oracle at scale 1 .5×) and are not reported separately. Ours achieves higher SR and Progress than Elevation-only across all difficulty levels and perturbation settings. Under clean conditions, SR improves by 23.6, 28.5, and 25.8 percentage points on L1, L2, and L3, respectively, while Progress increases by 9.3–11.9 percentage points. VTE remains broadly comparable to Elevation-only, with slightly higher error in some settings. Hazard avoid-ance can require sidesteps or detours away from the com-manded motion, increasing VTE even when these devia-tions improve safety. This safety–tracking trade-off moti-vates our anti-deviation module, which uses localization feedback to compensate for the resulting position and heading errors during navigation, allowing local avoid-ance without letting execution deviations accumulate into navigation drift. (Table 10). Nevertheless, stronger perturbations and harder terrain reduce both SR and Progress for Ours. On L3 at scale 1.5, Ours achieves 56 .7% Progress but only 22 .7% SR, highlighting the distinction between partial traversal and completing the entire route without any failure. Table 6 evaluates locomotion on uneven ground, with a primary focus on obstacle and hazard avoidance. Ours outperforms Elevation-only in SR under all four condi-tions, with gains of 41.2, 40.3, 34.5, and 21.4 percentage points under clean conditions and perturbation scales of 0.5, 1 .0, and 1 .5, respectively. To quantify how much of the Elevation-only–Oracle gap is closed by Ours, we compute (HCR Elevation-only −

HCR Ours )/(HCR Elevation-only − HCR Oracle ) for each haz-ard class and condition in Table 6. Using the reported mean HCRs, Ours closes approximately 74–100% of the gap for elevation-invisible hazards, 51–61% for elevation-ambiguous hazards, and only 12–24% for elevation-visible hazards. The semantic branch therefore recovers a larger fraction of the gap where elevation cues are weak or ab-sent, while much of the gap on geometrically visible haz-ards remains. These ratios describe class-specific HCR improvements, not recovery of overall success rate.

4.3 Navigation 

We run two controlled ablations, removing the semantic branch (Elevation-only) or disabling anti-deviation while keeping all other components unchanged. SR requires reaching the goal without collision or fall but permits con-tact with water or oil stains. Safe SR additionally requires no hazard intrusion (any foot entering a hazard region). Progress measures normalized geodesic goal-distance re-duction; SPL weights success by path efficiency; FGD is the final geodesic distance to the goal. Table 9 highlights three findings. Across the four evalu-ated settings, semantic input raises Safe SR by 3 .34–12 .26 percentage points and reduces hazard intrusion and FR by 8 .63–11 .15 and 8 .64–9 .75 points, respectively. Colli-sion rate also decreases in every setting, although the re-duction narrows to 0 .84 points with ten obstacles at scale 1.0. Progress improves in three settings; the exception is ten obstacles at scale 0 .5, where Ours trades 4 .7 points of Progress for lower collision, hazard-intrusion, and fall rates. Increasing the obstacle count generally lowers Safe SR, while the gaps between SR and Safe SR show why goal completion alone is insufficient for evaluating safe humanoid navigation. Table 10 shows that anti-deviation substantially im-proves successful and safe completion. It raises Safe SR from 11 .98% to 23 .68%, SR from 19 .50% to 29 .80%, and SPL from 0 .169 to 0 .256. Collision rate falls from 35 .93% to 12 .26% and hazard intrusion from 18 .38% to 12 .81%, while FR remains broadly similar (8 .64% versus 10 .86%). Notably, these substantial gains in SR and SPL occur despite slightly lower average Progress (46 .12% to 43 .20%). Since Progress measures normalized geodesic goal-distance reduction, an agent can make progress to-ward the goal without reliably tracking the intended way-points or completing the task. This contrast suggests that, without continuous execution correction, the robot may still move generally toward the destination while ac-cumulating deviations that prevent successful arrival. By using localization feedback to continually correct position and heading errors relative to the intended target pose, anti-deviation helps translate partial progress into suc-cessful, path-efficient completion. Final goal distance re-mains similar (5 .16 versus 5 .38 m), while VLM early stops increase (18 .38% to 29 .24%) and timeout remains similar, indicating that the benefits are concentrated in successful and safe completion rather than uniform improvements across all metrics. 

4.4 Limitations 

Semantic hazard avoidance does not eliminate the teacher–student performance gap. Even on uneven ground under clean conditions, Ours achieves 50 .6% SR versus Oracle’s 93 .6%, a 43 .0-percentage-point gap, and its elevation-visible HCR remains 24 .7% versus 2 .2%. Thus, improved handling of ambiguous and invisible haz-ards is not sufficient for reliable route completion. # 5 Conclusion 

This work presents a benchmark and semantic-aware locomotion-navigation framework that address two gaps in humanoid navigation: hazards poorly represented in el-evation maps and deviations between commanded and re-alized motion. On hazards that are ambiguous or invisible in elevation maps, a depth-only student distilled from the same teacher contacts them roughly 2-3 × more often than our semantic-aware student, which closes 51-61% and 74-100% of the hazard-contact-rate gap to the ground-truth-map oracle in these two classes, respectively, but only 12-24% on geometrically visible hazards. These results suggest that semantic information addresses important geometric blind spots, although the residual gap cannot be attributed to perception coverage alone. On the physi-cal Unitree G1, the same policy traverses cluttered floors with water spills, phones, and fallen bottles where the depth-only baseline fails. In navigation, anti-deviation raises completion from 19 .5% to 29 .8% SR while reduc-ing hazard intrusion from 18 .38% to 12 .81% (Table 10), demonstrating the benefit of closed-loop execution cor-rection. Future work will extend the benchmark to model the physical effects of hazards, such as slipping and unstable contact. Persistent semantic mapping could retain previ-ously observed hazards outside the waist camera’s current field of view. Another direction is to incorporate hazard awareness into target-pose selection for anti-deviation, balancing trajectory recovery with safe locomotion. We also plan to validate the complete navigation framework on hardware. These directions would strengthen both the system and its evaluation, advancing humanoid nav-igation research field that accounts jointly for task com-pletion, hazard avoidance, and execution consistency.
