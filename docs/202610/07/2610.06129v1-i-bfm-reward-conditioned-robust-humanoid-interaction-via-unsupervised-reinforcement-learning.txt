Abstract — Behavioral foundation models (BFMs) have re-cently shown that a single humanoid policy can support diverse whole-body control, but extending such generality to physical interaction remains challenging. We introduce I-BFM, to our knowledge the first BFM for humanoid–object interaction. Rather than relying on task-specific policies or reference tracking, I-BFM learns a shared representation of the coupled dynamics among the humanoid, objects, and their contacts using forward-backward representations and unsupervised re-inforcement learning. Given a downstream task reward, the same policy can be directly conditioned on a latent command to execute closed-loop interaction without task-specific policy optimization. To improve interaction control over different time scales, we further train the policy with both short-horizon interaction targets and longer-horizon goal targets. A single I-BFM policy performs carrying, pushing, and kicking, while also supporting goal reaching, motion tracking, stylistic control, and long-horizon task chaining. More importantly, it remains effective after large deviations from nominal execution: on Carry, I-BFM achieves 94.3% nominal success and retains 89.3% success after robot falls, compared with 1.3% for a planning-based baseline. Real-world experiments on a Unitree G1 further demonstrate diverse loco-manipulation behaviors, rapid recovery from interaction failures and external distur-bances, and task chaining without task-specific retraining. 

I. INTRODUCTION Humanoid robots are envisioned as general-purpose agents capable of performing diverse tasks across different environ-ments. Beyond locomotion, physical interaction with objects is an indispensable capability for realizing this vision. A truly arXiv:2610.06129v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 general-purpose humanoid should not only move robustly, but also perform diverse interaction behaviors and effectively recover from unexpected failures during long-horizon tasks. Recent whole-body foundation models (BFMs) have demonstrated strong generality and robustness in hu-manoid motion [1]–[3]. However, extending these capabil-ities to physical interaction remains challenging. Existing approaches often rely on open-loop teleoperation and behav-ior cloning, which are difficult to scale and provide limited failure recovery. Another line of work focuses on humanoid-object co-tracking, where MoCap HOI trajectories are directly used as supervision. While these methods achieve high-quality interaction behaviors, they heavily rely on dense references and are inherently inflexible [4]–[6]. Some approaches in-troduce planners [7] to generate intermediate references for the tracker. However, planners introduce additional latency, especially after interaction failures, and constrain behavioral diversity to their predefined rules. More recently, style-learning-based approaches have shown some task-specific generalization, such as transporting boxes between arbitrary configurations, but remain insufficiently general and robust for long-horizon interaction. These limitations raise a fundamental question: Can we endow a general-purpose humanoid controller with inter-action capabilities while preserving its generality and ro-bustness? Our key observation is that these desired proper-ties—including generality, robustness and flexible behavior invocation—already emerge naturally in BFMs trained with forward-backward (FB) representations and unsupervised reinforcement learning (URL) [1]. FB learning enables a single learned representation to specify and retrieve diverse behaviors through simple reward functions, making it well suited for a range of interactive objectives. Meanwhile, URL optimizes for the FB objective rather than motion imitation, allowing the policy to prioritize task completion and recov-ery, and to deviate from reference motions when necessary. These properties motivate us to extend the same paradigm from general-purpose motion to physical interaction. Based on this insight, we propose I-BFM , which learns a unified, Interactive Behavioral Foundation Model through unsupervised RL with an FB representation. It jointly embeds the humanoid’s basic skills and interactive skills into a structured latent interaction space. In interactive scenarios, the learned representation captures the coupled dynamics of the humanoid and objects, together with changes in their contact relationships. By specifying simple high-level rewards, I-BFM can invoke and compose diverse interactive behaviors, allowing the humanoid to perform skills such as carrying, pushing, and kicking objects, while quickly recov-ering from disturbances and interaction failures. Specifically, our Local–Goal Objective Geometry Operator (LOGO) uses metric-conditioned local and goal intents with residual actor regularization to improve controllability and robustness. 

I-BFM consistently improves task success, object-placement accuracy, and disturbance recovery, while sup-porting skill chaining and controllable interaction styles. Real-world experiments further demonstrate diverse, robust interactions and rapid failure recovery (Fig. 1). These re-sults suggest that humanoid interaction can be learned as a reusable, object-centric, reward-addressable latent space rather than task-specific controllers or fixed motion plans. Our contributions are: 1) We introduce I-BFM , the first framework for building a promptable interactive BFM for general-purpose hu-manoid interaction. Using FB representations and URL, 

I-BFM enables zero-shot execution of diverse interactive behaviors through a simple reward interface. 2) We incorporate object-contact information into the inter-action state and LOGO as a local–goal metric-conditioned residual actor objective, improving controllability and robustness for humanoid-object interaction. 3) We demonstrate robust real-world humanoid behaviors with I-BFM , including carrying, pushing, kicking, long-horizon behavior chaining, and recovery from large dis-turbances, interaction failures, and falls. II. R ELATED WORKS 

A. Behavioral Foundation Models for Whole-body Control. 

Learning-based whole-body controllers have shown that motion priors can provide reusable control skills for hu-manoids, supporting diverse motion tracking and command-conditioned behaviors. Recent systems have further trans-ferred these capabilities to real humanoids for whole-body tracking, teleoperation, and data collection [3], [8], [9]. SONIC [2] further illustrates the potential of scaling mo-tion tracking and synthesis to broad humanoid behaviors. Building on this foundation, behavioral foundation models (BFMs) [1], [10], [11], seek a more scalable objective: learning a single general-purpose policy from large and diverse motion corpora that can be flexibly steered by latent behaviors, goals, or rewards. However, current BFMs remain largely body-centric, modeling humanoid motion without ex-plicitly capturing objects and contact dynamics. In contrast, 

I-BFM incorporates object and contact states, extending whole-body priors to persistent physical interaction. 

B. Humanoid–Object Interaction. 

Existing humanoid–object interaction methods commonly rely on teleoperation [2], [12], [13], behavior cloning [14], task-specific reward design [15]–[17], or dense motion-reference tracking [4]–[6]. For example, HDMI [4] learns interaction skills by co-tracking humanoid and object tra-jectories extracted from videos. Although these methods produce high-quality motions, their dependence on reference trajectories limits adaptation after contact loss or unex-pected object displacement. Reference-free methods such as PhysHSI [18] and LessMimic [19] reduce reliance on motion tracking through task rewards or geometric inter-action representations. OmniContact [7] improves flexibility by generating intermediate references online, but recovery requires additional planning and introduces latency. Rather than specializing inference for a particular interaction mode or replanning after failure, I-BFM uses one object-aware be-havioral policy to compose diverse behaviors, while directly reacting to contact disruptions within closed-loop control. 

C. Unsupervised RL and FB Representations. 

Unsupervised reinforcement learning aims to acquire reusable behaviors without task-specific policy training. Forward-backward representations provide a particularly flexible framework by embedding states, rewards, and poli-cies into a shared latent space, allowing new behaviors to be invoked through simple reward specifications [20]. Prior FB-based models have demonstrated promptable locomotion and whole-body control, but have not explicitly modeled coupled humanoid–object dynamics [1], [10]. I-BFM extends FB rep-resentations from body-only behavior generation to contact-rich physical interaction: its latent representation jointly incorporates humanoid and object states, enabling reward-conditioned task composition and recovery without retraining a task-specific policy or invoking online replanning. b) Task Rewards: We decompose each long-horizon interaction into phases and construct a reward for each phase by combining contact mode, interaction geometry, object motion, and stability. Shared locomotion rewards produce approach and turning latents. For Carry Box, phase rewards encourage grasping and lifting, goal-directed 3D transport, controlled lowering, release, and stable placement. For Kick Box, they select aligned, hand-free impacts that increase the box velocity toward the goal while preserving balance and box stability. B. Main Task Performance 

To evaluate interactive loco-manipulation, we compare I-BFM with prior humanoid interaction methods on three tasks in Table I. On Carry Box, I-BFM achieves a 94 .3% success rate and the lowest final object error of 0.11 m. This accuracy highlights its object-centric representation, which directly optimizes object-level task rewards. In contrast, OmniCon-tact suffers from tracking errors and residual inaccuracies not corrected by re-planning. By jointly learning interaction representations and low-level control, I-BFM avoids this separation and achieves higher precision. 

I-BFM also performs well on the other two loco-manipulation tasks, demonstrating that the learned represen-tation is not limited to a single interaction pattern. Because our method is trained with unsupervised RL, it explores diverse states and interaction outcomes, allowing new in-teractive tasks to be instantiated rapidly with minimal task-specific data or tuning. Notably, our training data does not include demonstrations of carrying boxes from one height to a target with a different height; thus, the height-offset Carry Box setting evaluates zero-shot generalization beyond the demonstrated interaction distribution. This further validates the generalization benefits brought by the combination of FB and unsupervised RL. Qualitatively (Fig. 4), I-BFM pro-duces natural, human-like behaviors comparable to PhysHSI and OmniContact. At the same time, it supports controllable interactive behavior patterns (Sec.V-G) and achieves substan-tially better recovery under object and robot perturbations (Sec.V-C). We attribute these properties to the FB-based representation, which enables interactive behaviors to be specified by high-level rewards and executed through aunified low-level controller. C. Robustness and Recovery 

We further evaluate whether I-BFM is robust to external disturbances, including object displacement and robot falls. During rollout, we perturb either the object or the robot and measure whether the policy can recover and still complete the task. As shown in Table I, I-BFM maintains strong recovery performance with little degradation from the nominal setting. In contrast, OmniContact drops to 60 .0% SR-O and 1.3% 

SR-R, while other baselines show even weaker recovery. Qualitatively, as shown in Fig. 5, after an object-fall perturbation, the robot may enter a state from which it cannot feasibly transition to the state assumed by the replanned trajectory. Even when replanning eventually finds a feasible recovery trajectory, the planning and execution delay can prevent timely recovery. Robot-fall recovery is even more challenging for methods that rely on nominal motion plans. In contrast, through broad state-space exploration during un-supervised RL, I-BFM continues optimizing the same high-level task objective despite large state deviations, without re-quiring alignment with a predefined trajectory. Recovery be-haviors therefore emerge naturally within the unified policy, demonstrating the advantage of reward-specified interactive skills over fixed reference trajectories.

We introduced I-BFM , an interactive behavioral founda-tion model that extends the generality and robustness of BFMs from humanoid motion to physical interaction. a) Carry Box: Each method is evaluated in MuJoCo environment. Box dimensions and mass are set to the mid-points of the corresponding training ranges specified in each method’s codebase; parameters fixed during training retain their training values. The robot starts from a standing pose at (0 , 0) with heading sampled uniformly from [−π, π ]. The initial box center is sampled with x, y ∼ U(−5, 5) and 

z ∼ U(0 .15 , 0.8) . The target center is (1 , 1, z g ), where 

zg ∼ U (0 .15 , 0.8) . Two support platforms, each measuring 

0.4 × 0.4 × 0.02 m, are placed beneath the initial and target box poses, with their top surfaces aligned with the corre-sponding box bottoms. Box–platform collisions are enabled, while robot–platform collisions are disabled. 

b) Push Box: The training environments differ across methods, including whether the box is wheeled. To standard-ize the evaluation object, we use a non-wheeled box with a mass of 0.7 kg and side lengths of approximately 0.4 m. In our tests, using OmniContact’s original box did not yield a higher success rate than using this standardized box. Under the shared initialization protocol, the robot starts from a standing pose at (0 , 0) with heading sampled uniformly from 

[−π, π ]. The initial box XY position is sampled uniformly from [−5, 5] 2, and the target XY position is fixed at (1 , 1) .The box rests on the ground, with both its initial and target center heights set to half its height. LessMimic Push is eval-uated separately using its native box and initialization due to inference limitations: the robot starts at (0 , 0) with fixed heading, the box center is initialized at (0 .4, 0, h/ 2) , and the target XY position is sampled uniformly from [−5, 5] 2,where h denotes box height. Its results are supplementary and not directly comparable under the shared protocol. 

c) Replanning: Replanning is enabled wherever sup-ported by the native inference implementation. OmniContact uses its native replanning mechanism. Methods without a separate replanning module retain their native closed-loop controllers. 

d) Metrics and Termination: The maximum episode duration is 60 s, corresponding to 12,000 simulation steps at a timestep of 0.005 s. Success requires an object-center-to-target distance below 0.2 m. Carry Box uses 3D distance, except for LessMimic, which uses XY distance. For the reported Push Box results, LessMimic uses XY distance and OmniContact uses 3D distance. Carry Box and LessMimic Push episodes terminate early and are counted as successful when the distance criterion holds for 100 consecutive simulation steps; otherwise, suc-cess is determined at the time limit. The reported OmniCon-tact Push evaluation runs for the full 60 s and determines success at the final step. We report success rate (SR) and final 3D object-to-target error ( ET

), measured at episode termination. 

e) Repetitions: Each evaluation comprises three runs of 100 episodes. The updated OmniContact Carry Box evalua-tion and the LessMimic and PhysHSI evaluations use seed blocks 0–99, 100–199, and 200–299. The reported Omni-Contact Push evaluation uses three blocks of 100 consecutive seeds starting at 166559744, 266559744, and 366559744. We report the mean and sample standard deviation of the three run-level success rates and mean final errors. 

f) Perturbations: We report success rates under object-fall and robot-fall perturbations, denoted by SR-O and SR-R, respectively. A perturbation is triggered once the box reaches the midpoint of its transport progress, i.e., when its XY distance to the target becomes smaller than half of its initial XY distance to the target. For Carry Box, SR-O applies a vertical downward force of 200 N to the box for 0.2 s, while SR-R applies a vertical downward force of 2,000 N to the robot pelvis for 0.3 s, causing the robot to fall. For Push Box and Kick Box, we instead apply the corresponding forces in a uniformly sampled horizontal direction and restrict the perturbed box position to remain within 2 m of its position in the unperturbed rollout. The success criterion is unchanged from the nominal evaluation.
