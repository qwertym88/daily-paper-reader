# Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning for Collision-Free Locomotion of an Underactuated Biped 

Jagannath Prasad Sahoo 1∗, Saurabh Kumar 1, Surya Prakash S.K. 2, Samiran Datta 1, Abhay Dwivedi 1 and Amit Shukla 1

Abstract — A bipedal robot cannot deviate from its path to avoid an obstacle without disturbing its balance, and this coupling is most severe on underactuated platforms such as the biped considered here, which has four actuated joints per leg and no hip or ankle roll. This paper presents a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) framework in which a High-Level (HL) policy observes the robot pose, 36 raycast proximity measurements, moving-obstacle states, and a receding-horizon local goal, and outputs a body-velocity command (vx, v y , ω yaw )

every ten control steps, while a velocity-conditioned Low-Level (LL) policy tracks each command through PD-controlled joint targets. Both policies are trained jointly with Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). Because the converged gait is task-agnostic, it is frozen and driven by classical planners over the same com-mand interface, yielding three controlled baselines, SAC+A ⋆,SAC+RRT ⋆, and SAC+APF. Across 100 evaluation trials per method in randomized PyBullet environments, the proposed method reaches the goal in 98.0% of static and 88.0% of dynamic trials, against at most 78.0% and 68.0% for the planner hybrids, with path lengths within 4% of the A⋆

reference, and ablations confirm that each observation channel and reward term contributes materially to this performance. 

This present project information and videos are available at HRL-Biped. 

Index Terms — Bipedal locomotion, hierarchical reinforce-ment learning, soft actor–critic, obstacle avoidance, underac-tuated robots. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Obstacle-aware walking remains an open problem for three compounding reasons. A biped is underactuated, so its floating base cannot be commanded directly and every avoidance maneuver perturbs balance. Its velocity set is gait-limited and asymmetric, since a biped needs several steps to change speed or heading, and on our platform the absence of roll joints further restricts lateral motion. Moving obstacles demand anticipation, since by the time proximity alone signals danger a walking robot may no longer have time to step aside. Existing approaches occupy two extremes (Fig. 1). Flat end-to-end RL policies map observations directly to joint targets [1], train slowly, generalize poorly to unseen layouts, and must relearn locomotion whenever the task changes.

Decoupled pipelines instead track a geometrically planned path with an independent gait controller [2]–[4], ignoring balance coupling and, for global planners, moving obstacles. Neither extreme addresses all three difficulties at once, which motivates separating navigation from gait generation without severing the feedback between them. Given an underactuated biped with eight actuated leg joints, a known static occupancy map, and moving obstacles whose trajectories are unknown in advance, the controller must reach a goal at least 6.0 m away without collision or fall, sensing static geometry through proximity measure-ments and moving obstacles through their relative position and velocity, while generating joint-level actions at 50 Hz. We solve this with a two-level hierarchy in which both levels are learned and trained jointly. A lightweight planning layer computes a global path on the known static map and extracts a receding-horizon local goal. The high-level (HL) policy decides where to go next, emitting a body-velocity command u = ( vx, v y , ω yaw ) from the robot pose, raycast distances, moving-obstacle states, and the local goal. The low-level (LL) policy decides how to walk, converting each command into leg-joint targets and executing it for 

H = 10 consecutive control steps before the HL policy is queried again. This abstraction shortens the effective navigation horizon tenfold, and joint training lets the gait arXiv:2610.05855v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 adapt to the command distribution the navigation layer actually issues. Because the converged LL policy is a pure velocity-conditioned gait skill, it can be frozen and driven by classical planners over the same interface, which we exploit to build three controlled baselines, SAC+A ⋆, SAC+RRT ⋆,and SAC+APF, in which only the command generator differs. The contributions of this work are as follows.  

> •

A jointly trained two-level HRL framework in which an HL SAC policy outputs body-velocity commands from pose, raycast, dynamic-obstacle, and local-goal observations, and a velocity-conditioned LL SAC policy executes each command for ten control steps.  

> •

An HL observation and reward design covering both static layouts and moving obstacles, including a time-to-collision (TTC) term inducing anticipatory avoidance.  

> •

A controlled benchmark against SAC+A ⋆, SAC+RRT ⋆,and SAC+APF hybrids driving the identical frozen gait, isolating the navigation layer’s contribution.  

> •

An ablation over HL observation channels and reward components quantifying their effect on success, colli-sion, and failure rates. II. R ELATED WORK 

Obstacle avoidance began with classical planning. Global methods such as A ⋆ [2] and RRT ⋆ [3] compute near-optimal paths on a known map, and reactive methods such as artificial potential fields [4] and the dynamic window approach [5] respond to sensed obstacles online. These assume a base that executes commanded velocities faithfully, the reactive ones suffer local minima and oscillation, and applied to a biped they decouple avoidance from balance. CLF-CBF quadratic programs [6] restore real-time safety guarantees but still treat the gait as a separate tracking problem. Reinforcement learning then removed the need for hand-designed reactive rules, maturing first on wheeled robots for mapless navigation [7], goal-driven exploration [8], and crowd navigation [9]. In parallel, model-free RL produced robust legged locomotion, including periodic-reward gaits on Cassie [10], transformer-based humanoid locomotion [11], footstep-conditioned walking [12], and massively parallel training for quadrupeds [13], [14]. In our own prior work we trained multi-skill locomotion for the same underactuated platform using waypoint-based reward shaping [15]. The two lines rarely meet: navigation policies assume a statically stable base, locomotion policies do not avoid obstacles, and the one flat-RL attempt at bipedal avoidance [1] is limited to fixed, known obstacle positions. Hierarchical RL bridges the two. Temporal abstraction via options [16] and goal-conditioned hierarchies such as HIRO [17] showed that a coarse-timescale high-level pol-icy simplifies long-horizon tasks. DeepLoco [18] learned a footstep-level planner over a stepping controller, hierarchical commands have been used for quadruped navigation [19], and recent bipedal social navigation adopts a related de-composition [20]. Our work targets an underactuated biped without roll joints, combines raycasts with explicit dynamic-obstacle states at the HL, trains both levels jointly, and

D. Joint two-timescale training 

Both policies train simultaneously in the same episodes (Algorithm 1). The LL policy is updated at 50 Hz from Eq. (3). The HL policy is updated at 5 Hz from the macro-step return of Eqs. (1) and (5). Joint training couples the two levels: the gait adapts to the navigation layer’s actual command distribution, and the navigation layer learns the gait’s evolving feasibility limits through Eq. (8), with the HL policy acting as an automatic curriculum. Episodes terminate on goal arrival, fall, or timeout. After convergence the LL policy is frozen and reused by all baselines of Sec. IV-E.

The learned navigation layer’s advantage rests on three mechanisms. First, it commands only what the gait can exe-cute: joint training and Eq. (8) let it internalize the platform’s gait-limited, asymmetric command set, whereas the pure-pursuit trackers of A ⋆ and RRT ⋆ demand infeasible turns, surfacing as falls and timeouts even on static maps. Second, it anticipates rather than reacts: the TTC term of Eq. (9) yields before proximity alone would trigger, whereas global planners see moving obstacles only at replanning instants, too late for a robot needing several steps to change heading,

Third, the hierarchy assigns each problem to its natural timescale, and the ablation, in which every removed component shifts failures toward falls or timeouts rather than collisions, shows the advantage comes from this coupled structure rather than any single term. Several limitations remain in this work. As this study is simulation-only. Sim-to-real transfer would require address-ing model mismatch, sensing noise, and inter-level latency. The raycasts and dynamic-obstacle states of Eq. (4) are privileged simulator quantities standing in for a LiDAR and object tracker, chosen to make that substitution direct, though onboard perception remains future work. The planning layer assumes a known static map, shared symmetrically by our method and the baselines. Because the LL gait is co-trained with the HL policy, the frozen gait reused by baselines may track their commands slightly less faithfully. Results use a single training seed, so seed variance is unquantified, and the policy actuates only leg joints, leaving upper-body and multi-contact avoidance open. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework for collision-free locomotion of an underactuated bipedal robot in static and dynamic environments. A high-level SAC policy maps pose, raycast, dynamic-obstacle, and local-goal observations to body-velocity commands, and a velocity-conditioned low-level SAC gait policy executes each command over ten control steps, with both trained jointly at their respective timescales. The shared command interface enabled a controlled comparison against SAC+A ⋆,SAC+RRT ⋆, and SAC+APF hybrids driving the identical learned gait, in which the proposed method attained 98.0% success in static and 88.0% in dynamic scenarios within 4% of the shortest-path reference in path length. Ablations established that the TTC term, proximity penalty, posture shaping, and reward clip each contribute materially to this performance.
