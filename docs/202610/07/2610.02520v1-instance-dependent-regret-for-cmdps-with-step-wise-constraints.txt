Abstract 

We study online learning in episodic tabular constrained Markov decision processes with step-wise safety constraints . In such a setting, the constraints induce a safe subgraph that shapes the variance of cumulative rewards under feasible policies and, consequently, the difficulty of learning. Exploiting this structure, however, requires learning which actions are safe while controlling constraint violations. We propose 

Safe Variance-Adaptive Exploration (SVAE), an efficient algorithm that learns candidate safe subgraphs and performs variance-adaptive optimistic planning within them. With high probability, SVAE achieves cumulative regret of order ˜O(√SAH min {VΣ, K Var ⋆} + S√AH 3 min {K, C} + S2AH 2) over K episodes, where H is the horizon of a single episode, while S and A are the numbers of states and actions, respectively. Here, Var ⋆ is the maximum return variance among safe policies, VΣ is the variance accumulated before the first unsafe action is encountered, and C captures the statistical complexity of eliminating actions incorrectly considered potentially safe. SVAE additionally attains ˜O(H√SAK + S2AH 2) step-wise constraint violation and a gap-dependent violation bound that is polylogarithmic in K. Finally, we establish a lower bound showing that dependence on these instance-specific quantities is unavoidable. 

# 1 Introduction 

Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) are widely used to model sequential decision making in robotics, autonomous driving, and wireless networks [Achiam et al., 2017, Liu et al., 2021, Bura et al., 2022]. The objective is to maximize expected cumulative reward subject to safety or resource constraints, commonly imposed on expected cumulative costs [Altman, 1999, Miryoosefi and Jin, 2022, Stradi et al., 2025a]. The potential harm from individual unsafe decisions motivates step-wise safety constraints, which impose a mean-cost threshold at each decision [Xiong et al., 2023, Shi et al., 2023]. Violation is measured by summing positive excesses above this threshold without cancellation [Wei et al., 2024]. In this work, we study online learning in episodic tabular constrained MDPs under this kind of step-wise constraint. Instance-dependent regret bounds distinguish the statistical difficulty of individual problems—namely, instances—and provide sharper guarantees on easier ones [Zanette and Brunskill, 2019, Dann et al., 2021]. In unconstrained reinforcement learning, variance-dependent analyses relate regret to the uncertainty induced by rewards and transitions [Talebi and Maillard, 2018, Zhou et al., 2023b, Zhao et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2025]. Such bounds can retain worst-case guarantees while improving on low-variance instances, including nearly constant regret in deterministic environments [Zhou et al., 2023b, Zamir et al., 2026]. In CMDPs, unknown costs and transitions determine which policies are feasible [Bura et al., 2022, Yu et al., 2025]. Exploratory policies can be unsafe, so their returns need not share the low variance of safe policies. Whether the benefits of variance-dependent learning can be retained under step-wise constraints is still unclear. Step-wise safety constraints can be represented by a safe subgraph that retains actions satisfying the cost threshold and leading only to states with feasible future decisions [Xiong et al., 2023, Shi et al., 2023]. Our 1arXiv:2610.02520v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 key observation is that even though learning under unknown constraints generally poses additional challenges, safety constraints can still simplify reward learning by removing stochastic branches or reducing differences in expected future rewards across possible next states under feasible policies. Even when the rewards and the transition kernel are fixed, changing costs can alter the safe subgraph and thereby reshape variances. However, small cost gaps and rare transitions to states with no feasible policy can still make safety identification difficult. Understanding how the safe subgraph relates to the variance and the statistical complexity is therefore central to instance-dependent regret analysis. These observations lead to the following questions: 

(i) Is it possible to design a computationally efficient algorithm that achieves variance-dependent regret bounds and sublinear cumulative step-wise violation? (ii) How does the induced safe subgraph shape the statistical complexity of learning under step-wise constraints? 

To answer these questions, we first characterize how the induced safe subgraph shapes the variances and the safety identification complexity. We then propose Safe Variance-Adaptive Exploration (SVAE), which separates safe-subgraph learning from variance-adaptive planning. The algorithm learns candidate safe actions using variance-independent cost lower confidence bounds and observed successors, and plans optimistically over these sets using empirical reward and transition variances. We further evaluate SVAE and examine the effect of safe-subgraph properties on regret in synthetic experiments (Appendix H). Our contributions can be summarized as follows. • Safe-subgraph structure and variance In Section 3, we represent step-wise safety constraints through their induced safe subgraph and define two distinct variance quantities: ( i) the maximum safe-policy return variance Var ⋆ is the largest variance of the total episode return over all safe policies, and ( ii ) the trajectory-based total variance VΣ sums reward variances and transition variances before the first unsafe action in each episode. We establish a tight bound on transition variance in terms of stochastic branching depth and differences in expected cumulative rewards across successor states within the safe subgraph. • Safety identification complexity In Section 3.3, we introduce a reward-independent instance quantity 

C to measure the difficulty of ruling out unsafe actions at states reachable under safe policies. Lower bounds for valid elimination establish its necessity. • Efficient variance-adaptive exploration In Section 4, we develop SVAE, an efficient algorithm that exploits low return variance within the unknown safe subgraph without prior knowledge of a safe policy. SVAE maintains candidate action sets that retain all safe actions with high probability and uses the resulting candidate subgraph to adapt its exploration bonuses to empirical reward and next-state value variances. • Instance-dependent learning guarantees In Section 5.1, we show that our algorithm attains, for a CMDP with S states, A actions, horizon H, a cumulative regret bound over K episodes of order 

˜O(√SAH min {VΣ, K Var ⋆}+S√AH 3 min {K, C} +S2AH 2). Here ˜O hides polylogarithmic factors. Regret is polylogarithmic in K on fixed instances with Var ⋆ = 0, while both regret and cumulative step-wise violation retain ˜O(√K) worst-case dependence. Step-wise violation is also polylogarithmic in K for a fixed positive cost gap (Theorem 3). • Regret lower bound In Section 5.2, we establish a regret lower bound, showing that both return variance and safety identification dependencies are unavoidable. 

## 1.1 Related Work 

Safe reinforcement learning Safe RL is a fundamental area of reinforcement learning, with diverse constraint formulations and measures of constraint violation. For episode-level constraints, regret and constraint-violation guarantees have been established in tabular settings [Efroni et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2021, Bura et al., 2022, Wei et al., 2022, Stradi et al., 2024, 2025b] and with linear function approximation [Ding et al., 2021, Ghosh et al., 2022, Kitamura et al., 2025]. For instantaneous constraints, Amani et al. [2021] and Roknilamouki et al. [2025] ensure constraint satisfaction with high probability using known safe actions 2or policies. Without a known safe policy, Xiong et al. [2023] and Wei et al. [2024] bound cumulative positive step-wise violation. Xiong et al. [2023] study tabular MDPs with costs that depend only on the state. Wei et al. [2024] consider linear MDPs and assume that a feasible action exists at every state and step. We study tabular MDPs with unknown state–action costs and require only the existence of a safe policy from the initial state. 

Variance-dependent regret Variance-dependent regret bounds can improve on worst-case rates in low-variance environments. Such guarantees have been established for episodic tabular MDPs [Zhou et al., 2023b], linear-mixture MDPs [Zhao et al., 2023], and infinite-horizon MDPs [Zamir et al., 2026], with recent work also combining variance and suboptimality gaps [Chen et al., 2025]. In particular, Zhou et al. [2023b] derive bounds involving a maximum policy-return variance measure and a variance sum along the learner’s trajectories. For CMDPs, Yu et al. [2025] use total-variance analysis to improve the horizon dependence of a worst-case regret bound under episode-level constraints. Jiang and Ye [2024] obtain a different form of instance dependence through an optimal-basis characterization of the CMDP linear program. We control step-wise violation and derive a regret bound that separates safe-policy return variance from the complexity of ruling out actions outside the true safe set. 

Graph structure and safe exploration Graph structure has been used to guide safe exploration and refine regret bounds. Safe-region methods use reachability and returnability to expand the region that can be explored safely [Turchetta et al., 2016, Wachi and Sui, 2020]. Shi et al. [2023] use safe subgraphs for learning under instantaneous constraints, assuming known transition supports and a known seed safe subgraph. For unconstrained MDPs, Bourel et al. [2020] combine variance-aware concentration with transition-support adaptation and obtain regret bounds involving local diameter and effective support. Our structural analysis examines how safety constraints change the graph available to safe policies and how this change affects their transition variance. We use this relation to bound the transition-variance contribution to regret in terms of stochastic branching and continuation-value differences within the safe subgraph. Due to space constraints, additional related works are provided in Appendix A.

# 3 Safety-Induced Variance and Identification Complexity 

Safety constraints shape the statistical difficulty of reinforcement learning, not only the set of feasible policies. They can impede exploration, but by restricting behavior they can also remove sources of reward uncertainty and simplify learning inside the feasible region. Under step-wise safety, these two effects are organized by the hidden safe subgraph. We show that its stochastic branching and successor-value differences control the transition variance available to reward learning, while actions outside the graph must be excluded based on cost or successor-viability evidence. This yields tight structural variance bounds together with a complexity for the separate task of safety identification. 43.1 Variance under Step-Wise Safety

# 4 Safe Variance-Adaptive Exploration 

In this section, we provide our main algorithm, Safe Variance-Adaptive Exploration (SVAE). Section 3 shows that the safe subgraph can reduce reward uncertainty, while identifying it remains difficult even when the return variance is small. On the reward side, empirical variance can sharpen optimistic exploration, whereas on the cost side, empirical variance alone cannot reliably rule out unsafe actions. An unsafe action can exhibit zero empirical cost variance until a rare high-cost outcome is observed, as shown in Appendix E. SVAE therefore adopts an asymmetric design, combining variance-independent cost lower confidence bounds for safe-subgraph learning with variance-adaptive bonuses for reward exploration. We present SVAE in Algorithm 1.

We next establish a regret lower bound that captures the dependence on both reward variance and safety identification complexity. 

Theorem 4 (Lower bound) . Let S ≥ 8, A ≥ 2, H ≥ 64 , and K ≥ SA be integers. Fix v ∈ [0 , H/ 128] and Γ ∈

Theorem 4 demonstrates that both maximum safe-policy return variance and safety identification complexity contribute to the difficulty of learning. The proof uses two families of CMDPs with the prescribed variance and identification complexity. Constrained policy optimization Policy optimization methods approach constrained learning through different policy updates and penalty constructions. Achiam et al. [2017] use trust-region updates in Constrained Policy Optimization, while Tessler et al. [2019] use an adaptive penalty in Reward Constrained Policy Optimization to guide learning toward constraint satisfaction. For discounted CMDPs, Ding et al. [2025] analyze the convergence and sample complexity of natural policy gradient primal–dual methods with tabular and function-approximation parametrizations. In online CMDPs with adversarial losses and constraints, Stradi et al. [2025c] provide the first policy optimization primal-dual method to attain best-of-both-worlds results. 

Safe exploration and policy evaluation Safety during data collection is distinct from feasibility of the policy returned after exploration. Miryoosefi and Jin [2022] reduce constrained RL and approachability to reward-free RL oracles without requiring safe exploration. Huang et al. [2023] instead use a known safe baseline to satisfy an expected cumulative-cost budget in every exploration episode with high probability, while Xiong et al. [2023] study reward-free exploration with cumulative step-wise violation control. For conservative exploration, Li et al. [2023] maintain an episode-wise performance threshold with high probability using a known baseline policy or one learned from suitable offline data. For policy evaluation, Mukherjee et al. [2022] use reward-variance estimates to guide sampling in tree-structured MDPs. Mukherjee et al. [2024] impose a cumulative safety constraint relative to a known default policy and, under their tractability assumptions, bound the mean squared error of SaVeR. In these evaluation problems, the goal is to reduce estimation error for a fixed target policy, rather than learn which safe policy to execute. 

Safe control Control-theoretic and formal methods reason about safety through the future consequences of an action. Berkenkamp et al. [2017] combine learned dynamics with Lyapunov analysis to expand a region of attraction while maintaining stability. Alshiekh et al. [2018] use a shield to restrict or correct actions according to a temporal-logic specification. Reachability-based methods characterize states from which a violation can still be avoided. Ganai et al. [2023] combine reachability estimation with policy optimization, while Qadri et al. [2025] show that inverse constraint learning can recover a dynamics-dependent backward reachable tube rather than the underlying failure set. These approaches study safety through stability, specifications, or reachability, rather than through the variance-dependent regret of an online learner. They provide a related view of why local cost feasibility alone need not ensure safe continuation. 

Variance-dependent reinforcement learning Variance has been used both to sharpen worst-case regret bounds and to obtain guarantees that adapt to a particular instance. Azar et al. [2017] use empirical next-state value variance in finite-horizon exploration bonuses, while Talebi and Maillard [2018] establish variance-aware regret bounds for undiscounted MDPs. Zhou et al. [2023a] study variance-dependent learning in latent MDPs whose contexts are revealed in hindsight. With known transitions, Li et al. [2026] obtain data-dependent regret bounds for adversarial losses and variance-dependent bounds for stochastic losses. A different trajectory model appears in branching RL, where an episode generates a tree rather than a single path and the analysis requires a corresponding law of total variance [Du and Chen, 2022]. That model differs from the stochastic transitions along a single trajectory considered here. Thus, both the variance quantity and its role in a guarantee depend on the underlying MDP and observation model. 

Instance-dependent sample complexity Sample-complexity bounds for CMDPs capture several sources of difficulty beyond return variance. With generative-model access, Vaswani et al. [2022] distinguish relaxed and strict feasibility in discounted CMDPs and quantify the role of the Slater constant in learning a near-optimal policy with no constraint violation. Wei et al. [2026] study constrained average-reward MDPs under the same sampling model, with bounds involving bias span, transient time, and a feasibility margin. A sample with zero empirical variance may come from a cost distribution whose mean exceeds the safety threshold. We compare two distributions that can produce the same constant sample to obtain a necessary additive term in the confidence bound. # H Experiments 

In this section, we conduct experiments to illustrate our theoretical findings and evaluate the performance of our algorithm. We focus on the following four questions: 

Q1: How does SVAE compare with baselines in regret and step-wise violation? 

Q2: Can SVAE exploit low return variance induced by safety constraints? 

Q3: Can safety identification remain difficult when safe-policy return variance is zero? 

Q4: Does recursive elimination exclude unsafe branches while preserving safe continuations? 

Appendix H.1 describes the experimental setup and baselines. We evaluate regret and step-wise violation on stochastic navigation tasks in Appendix H.2 (Q1). We then study variance adaptation, safety identi-fication complexity, and recursive elimination using controlled CMDPs in Appendices H.3–H.5 (Q2–Q4). Hyperparameter selection and implementation details are given in Appendix H.6. 

## H.1 Experimental Setup 

Learning setting The reward means, mean costs, and transition probabilities are unknown. All algorithms learn from online interaction without a known safe policy. Rewards are Bernoulli in Q1 and deterministic in Q2–Q4, where we study the safe subgraph and safety identification without reward noise. The reward means remain fixed throughout learning. Costs are Bernoulli with mean ch(s, a ), and next states follow the transition kernel. Estimates are maintained separately for each ( h, s, a ). We set τ = 0 .6, δ = 0 .05, and run each algorithm for K = 10 ,000 episodes. 

Baselines For navigation (Q1), we compare SVAE with SUCBVI [Xiong et al., 2023]. Both methods use the same recursive safety filter, so the comparison focuses on their planning rules. Our SUCBVI implementation estimates unknown rewards and costs separately for each ( h, s, a ). We also include MVP-V [Zhou et al., 2023b] and a variant with the recursive safety filter applied at every episode, denoted by MVP-V + filter .42 Ablations We consider two variants of SVAE with Hoeffding-type bonuses. SVAE (Hoeffding) replaces the empirical reward and transition variances in (10) with 1 /4 and ( H − h)2/4, respectively. SVAE (Hoeffding-P) 

replaces only the transition variance, with P denoting the transition component. Both retain the finite-sample correction and recursive safety filter. We also consider SVAE w/o propagation , which retains variance-adaptive planning but eliminates actions only through their own cost lower confidence bounds. Q2 compares SVAE with both bonus variants, and Q4 compares it with the variant without propagation. Q3 evaluates SVAE as the environment parameters vary.

## H.6 Additional Experimental Details 

Hyperparameter selection We scale the planning bonus by α and the cost-confidence radius by β. For navigation, each method is evaluated with 18 configurations. Methods with safety filtering use six planning multipliers combined with three cost-confidence multipliers, while unconstrained MVP-V uses 18 planning multipliers. To be eligible, configurations with safety filtering must have mean expected violation of at most 0.02 per step. They must also retain all truly safe actions and keep a nonempty candidate set at every visited state that admits a safe continuation. Eligible configurations are ranked by increasing strong regret R+,and unconstrained MVP-V configurations by decreasing policy value. We first evaluate all configurations on two development environments with two seeds per environment. The best three are then evaluated on two separate validation environments, again with two seeds per environment. Both stages use K = 10 ,000 episodes. After validation, we select the best configuration and fix its hyperparameters before testing. For navigation, ( α, β ) is (0 .0003 , 0.30) for SVAE and (0 .001 , 0.30) for SUCBVI.
