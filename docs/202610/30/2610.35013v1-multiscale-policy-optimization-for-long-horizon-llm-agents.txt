# Abstract 

Long-horizon LLM agents interact with their environments through complete assistant turns while generating each action token by token. We observe that critic values vary primarily across turns, motivating optimization anchored at turns with finer credit allocation among tokens. We propose Multiscale Policy Optimization (MPO), combining Multiscale Advantage Estimation (MAE) and turn-level importance sampling to align credit assignment and policy updates at the turn scale. ## 1. Introduction 

An LLM agent interacts with its environment through complete assistant turns, such as search queries, tool calls, and final responses, while its policy generates each turn token by token. Learning from a terminal task reward therefore involves two scale choices. Credit must connect the final outcome to interactions across the trajectory and distinguish the tokens within each interaction. Policy updates must also account for changes in complete-output probabilities while retaining those local distinctions. These choices motivate a policy-optimization method that uses both the interaction and generation scales. Two common families of RL methods leave a gap between these scales. Critic-free, group-relative methods , including GRPO, DAPO, and GSPO [30, 44, 50], derive one shared advantage per generated sequence from group-relative outcome rewards. Despite differences in their update objectives, this shared credit signal does not distinguish the contributions of individual turns or tokens within a rollout. Critic-based, token-level methods , including PPO, VC-PPO, VAPO, and SAO [9, 29, 45, 46], use a learned value function to estimate advantages along the token sequence. Their common formulation treats each generated token as an action, providing finer credit without explicitly separating within-turn generation from turn-level environment interaction. Agentic RL therefore calls for credit assignment that captures both the contribution of each complete interaction and the relative contributions of tokens within it. 

Observing credit structure across turns. Figure 2 illustrates learned values that vary within turns and exhibit larger changes across some environment interactions. Across 2,048 trajectories and 16,323 turns from eight checkpoint batches of one search-agent run, differences between turn means account for a median of 89.5% of the critic’s token-weighted within-trajectory value variance. Between-turn variance exceeds within-turn variance in 93.8% of trajectories. This 2structure motivates propagating long-range credit across turns while retaining local token information. Appendix C provides additional statistics and detailed analyses of critic behavior in multi-turn agent interactions. 

Multiscale Policy Optimization. Treating complete assistant turns as macro actions, we es-tablish a turn-level improvement bound and show that token surrogates incur an additional within-turn distribution-shift penalty for exact advantages under the same KL constraints. Guided by this analysis and the observed critic structure, we develop Multiscale Policy Opti-mization (MPO), combining multiscale credit assignment with turn-level importance weighting. Multiscale Advantage Estimation (MAE) uses turn-level GAE to propagate credit across inter-actions, then refines token credit through a centered local correction that preserves the turn mean. We derive this allocation as a constrained projection using a single critic. Turn-level importance weighting applies the complete-turn probability ratio while retaining each token’s MAE advantage in its policy gradient through a stop-gradient construction. Our contributions are threefold: • We analyze policy improvement for LLM agents at turn and token scales, deriving a turn-level improvement bound and identifying the additional discrepancy that token-level surrogates introduce through within-turn distribution shift. • We propose MPO, which combines multiscale advantage estimation (MAE) with turn-level importance sampling to align credit assignment and policy updates with complete turns while preserving token-specific credit. • We demonstrate consistent performance gains over strong RL baselines across search, tool-use, and automation tasks with both 4B and 30B-A3B models. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Tool use and multi-turn agent learning 

Early work connected language-model reasoning with actions and external tools [26, 31, 42]. Reflection, memory, planning, reusable skills, and multi-agent collaboration supported adapta-tion from experience and sustained interaction [8, 20, 33, 36, 39], establishing core interaction patterns for agents operating over multiple turns. Modern coding agents and execution harnesses integrate repository editing, code execution, testing, and browsing to resolve software issues and complete practical web and research tasks [1, 34, 37, 41, 49]. Coding agents also support algorithm discovery [19]. Agents also use execution feedback and accumulated experience to refine their tools, workflows, and memory [11, 24, 25, 40, 47]. These systems motivate optimizing agents across complete interaction turns and the tokens generated within each turn. 

2.2. RL in LLM Posttraining 

Classical policy-gradient methods optimize expected return through reward- or advantage-weighted updates [35, 38]. Advantage estimation and conservative, trust-region, or proximal updates provide foundations for stable policy optimization [14, 27–29]. Building on GRPO [30], DeepSeek-R1 [7] demonstrated the potential of large-scale RL for reasoning. Subsequent methods improve importance weighting and trust-region control 3[5, 21, 44, 50], as well as critic learning and long-response optimization [3, 6, 45, 46]. Risk-based objectives further address exploration and solution diversity [12, 23]. Policy optimization also targets visually grounded causal reasoning [16]. RL also supports multi-turn agents that learn through tool use and environment feedback [13, 18, 51]. Long horizons and sparse task rewards make credit assignment across decisions especially important [2, 4, 48, 52]. Recent work further develops stable policy updates for asyn-chronous agent training [9, 10]. MPO aligns advantage estimation and importance correction with complete turns, while retaining centered token-level credit within each turn.

## 4. Policy Improvement Across Turns and Tokens 

We analyze how turn-level and token-level surrogates relate to the same task-return objective. Following the surrogate-and-distribution-shift analysis of TRPO [27], we first establish a finite-horizon turn-level improvement bound, then identify the additional discrepancy introduced by token surrogates. These results motivate combining multiscale credit assignment with turn-level importance weighting in MPO. Full proofs and the extension to estimated turn advantages are provided in Appendix A.

PPO and MPO both train the critic against terminal returns with 𝜆 critic = 1, yet MPO exhibits higher explained variance on average during late training at both model scales. A plausible explanation is that turn-aligned policy updates change the visited histories and subsequent outcomes, making their return structure easier for the critic to capture. Better value estimates may in turn improve the actor’s credit signal. This interpretation concerns the interaction between policy learning and critic fitting: explained variance is measured on each policy’s own rollouts and also depends on return variance, so it does not isolate a causal improvement in critic accuracy.

6.5. Ablation Studies 

We ablate cross-turn propagation, within-turn trace length, and local refinement strength on 4B Search by varying 𝜆 , 𝜆 intra , and 𝛽 one at a time. The critic target, initialization, and remaining training setup are held fixed. Tables 2 and 3 report pass@1 and pass@3 on all three Search benchmarks. The reference setting is (𝜆 , 𝜆 intra , 𝛽 ) = (0.80, 0.95, 1.00 ) and reproduces MPO’s 4B results in Table 1. Each setting has one run. Validation learning curves for these settings appear in Appendix D. 

Turn-scale propagation, 𝜆 . The intermediate setting 𝜆 = 0.80 performs best among the tested values. Increasing it to 0.85 reduces BrowseComp-Plus, GAIA, and HLE pass@1 by 1.72, 2.21, and 1.05 points, respectively, and also reduces all three pass@3 scores. Moving closer to one thus hurts performance over this part of the tested range, consistent with a longer trace carrying more 13 variability from distant interactions. Reducing 𝜆 to 0.60 also lowers performance, suggesting that shortening the trace too much discards useful cross-turn credit. The results favor balancing these effects rather than maximizing the propagation range. 

Within-turn trace, 𝜆 intra . Reducing 𝜆 intra from 0.95 to 0.90 lowers pass@1 by 1.83, 1.55, and 1.77 points on BrowseComp-Plus, GAIA, and HLE, and lowers pass@3 on every benchmark. This shorter trace limits how far local TD information reaches within a generated turn. Setting  

> 𝜆 intra

= 1 also reduces all six scores relative to 0.95, including a 0.41-point drop in BrowseComp-Plus pass@1. Thus, 0.95 performs best on all six metrics in this sweep. 

Different decay rates for different time scales. The two coefficients count different events:  

> 𝜆

decays once per interaction, whereas 𝜆 intra decays once per generated token. A trajectory contains far fewer interactions than tokens, and each interaction itself requires many generated tokens. Using the same coefficient would therefore impose very different credit ranges. For example, the nominal geometric trace length 1 /( 1 − 𝜆 ) is five turns at 𝜆 = 0.80, while 𝜆 intra = 0.95 gives twenty tokens; reducing the latter to 0.90 halves that local range. A smaller turn coefficient can therefore retain credit across several interactions, while a larger local coefficient avoids restricting allocation to only a few tokens. This difference motivates tuning the two scales separately and explains our choice of 𝜆 < 𝜆 intra : multiscale policy optimization uses distinct propagation ranges for environment interaction and autoregressive generation. 

Local refinement strength, 𝛽 . Setting 𝛽 = 0 removes within-turn credit allocation from MAE, leaving only turn-level GAE: every token in a turn receives the same turn advantage. Even without within-turn allocation, this turn-level variant outperforms the standard PPO baseline with token-level GAE in Table 1 on all six Search metrics. Its pass@1 gains over PPO are 0.95, 2.49, and 0.66 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus, GAIA, and HLE, respectively. However, removing local allocation still substantially reduces performance relative to full MPO (Table 3). Compared with 𝛽 = 1.00, pass@1 drops by 6.28, 5.27, and 2.04 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus, GAIA, and HLE, respectively, and pass@3 also decreases on all three benchmarks. These results show that allocating credit within a turn provides benefits beyond propagating credit across turns alone. Introducing local refinement with 𝛽 = 0.50 partially recovers the loss, while 𝛽 = 1.00 performs best on all six metrics. Increasing 𝛽 further to 1.50 and then 2.00 progressively reduces both pass@1 and pass@3 across all three benchmarks. Since the local correction is centered, these changes preserve the turn’s mean advantage while amplifying differences among its tokens. The results therefore support within-turn credit allocation with a moderate refinement strength; removing it or giving it excessive weight both hurt performance. 

## 7. Conclusion 

We introduced MPO to organize LLM-agent policy optimization across turns and tokens. Its MAE component combines cross-turn credit propagation with mean-preserving local allocation, and its update aligns complete-turn importance correction with token-specific gradients. Our analysis motivates these scale choices, while experiments show gains across Search and agent tasks and continued benefits from increased sampling. The ablations support treating interaction and generation as distinct credit horizons. Together, these findings motivate using both scales when optimizing long-horizon LLM agents. 14 References

The median share of variance explained by between-turn differences is 89.5% when weighting by token count and 90.2% when weighting turns equally, indicating that the pattern is not driven solely by differences in turn length. 34 Local token variation remains relevant. Panel (c) compares jumps from the last token value of one turn to the first of the next with the within-turn P95–P05 value span. The mean boundary jump exceeds the mean within-turn span in only 22.4% of trajectories. Panel (d) further shows that boundaries account for 9.2% of pooled squared adjacent value changes; small within-turn fluctuations accumulate over many more tokens. Thus, differences in turn-level value do not imply that most local value movement occurs at boundaries.
