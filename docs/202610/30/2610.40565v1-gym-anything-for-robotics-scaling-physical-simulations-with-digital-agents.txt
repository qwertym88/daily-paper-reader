# Gym-Anything for Robotics: Scaling physical simulations with digital agents 

Pranjal Aggarwal Sean Welleck 

Carnegie Mellon University 

Abstract 

Realistic, fast robotics simulations could enable large-scale training of embodied AI models that transfer to the real world. However, despite decades of progress, available simulations cover only a narrow range of real-world tasks and physical phenomena. The core bottleneck is that creating better simulations requires sig-nificant expert effort and therefore does not scale. To address this, we introduce Gym-Anything for Robotics, a framework in which coding agents create realis-tic robotics simulations. Our core insight is that simulation creation is a coding agent task, so improvements in coding agents translate directly into richer and more faithful simulations. We select simulations based on U.S. GDP data to cover economically valuable physical work, define what makes a good simulation and a good task, and develop an adversarial creation-audit loop that checks each simula-tion and task against these definitions. Using this pipeline, we build RoboWorld, a set of 25 simulations with 25 test and 175 training RL environments of vary-ing difficulty. Even the best frontier models fail on every RoboWorld test task, achieving an average reward of only 0.06. Building on RoboWorld, we introduce several new ways for frontier models to improve, including in-context learning across repeated attempts, a privileged teacher that reads the simulator state and teaches a student in context, self-assisted learning from the model’s own privi-leged experience, and code policies that generate demonstrations across varied scenes to train vision-language-action models (VLAs) such as π0.5. Each of these substantially improves performance. We release our pipeline, RL environments, and benchmark to facilitate future research. 1

1 Introduction 

Simulation is one of the most promising paths to training embodied AI at scale. Unlike the real world, a simulator can run thousands of episodes in parallel, reset instantly, expose the full ground-truth state, and provide rewards without human supervision [Todorov et al., 2012, Makoviychuk et al., 2021, Tao et al., 2025]. For these advantages to matter, however, simulations must faithfully represent the real world. Although the field has developed various hand-built environments [James et al., 2020, Yu et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2023, Li et al., 2023, Nasiriany et al., 2024], a key bottleneck is that building additional, more realistic simulations requires significant time and expert effort. Indeed, despite decades of human progress, today’s simulations cover a narrow range of real-world tasks and physical phenomena (Tables 2 and 1), often with limited visual fidelity. Simulation as a path to embodied AI is hindered by the challenge of building diverse, realistic simulations at scale. Our core insight is that simulation creation can be framed as a coding agent task . Building a sim-ulation means writing code, including assembling assets into scenes, configuring physics, defining tasks, and implementing rewards. Recently, coding agents have become increasingly capable of a wide range of coding-related work [Jimenez et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024a, Wang et al., 2025a, 2024a,c]. We hypothesize that frontier agents are already capable of building diverse robotics sim-ulations with high visual fidelity and realistic physics. Moreover, framing simulation creation as a coding agent task allows future improvements in coding agents to translate into better simulations, thereby providing a means for scalable, realistic simulation creation that is expected to improve over time. Building on these insights, we introduce Gym-Anything for Robotics, a framework in which coding agents create realistic robotics simulations of real-world physical work (Figure 2). Building simulators for real physical work requires modeling a wide variety of materials and phys-ical phenomena. For instance, a realistic titration requires the solution to form drops, mix, and turn pink rather than red at the endpoint. To organize the coverage of such phenomena in our simulation environments, we ground the selection of simulations in U.S. GDP data [Aggarwal et al., 2026, Pat-wardhan et al., 2025]. In brief, we consider each occupation, decompose the physical portion of the occupation’s tasks into atomic physical actions, and weight the actions by economic value. We then select simulations that cover the most valuable physical actions and any robotic and physics capa-2bilities that these actions require. A coding agent then builds each simulation in code, assembles scenes from real assets, and configures the robot and physics (§3). However, simulations built by coding agents frequently contain errors that make them unrealistic. For instance, a liquid simulation may not handle surface tension or inter-particle interactions cor-rectly, or objects may pass through walls. Therefore, we develop an agentic creation-audit loop [Ag-garwal et al., 2026] for detecting issues in the simulations and fixing them. Specifically, we define criteria for what constitutes a good simulation, including realism, solvability, speed, and robustness to exploits, and then run a creation agent and four audit agents in an adversarial loop. The creation agent must produce evidence that its simulation is good, such as renders, physical measurements, and assisted demonstrations. The four audit agents, including an adversarial agent that tries to break the simulation, then check the evidence and report issues for the creation agent to fix. Furthermore, for each simulation, we use coding agents to create multiple tasks and control their difficulty (§4). This is important since tasks that are all too easy or all too hard provide little signal for evaluating or improving models [Shao et al., 2024, Yu et al., 2025]. As with simulation creation, generated tasks may have issues such as being unsolvable or having incorrect rewards. We therefore define what makes a good task, and hand-curate checklists for six independent audit agents, which check each task against this definition, including solvability and reward correctness. We further ensure that each task’s reward measures both progress and the final outcome, computed from the physical state of the simulation. For instance, a cleanup task can credit each object placed in its correct location rather than only a fully cleaned scene. Finally, every task can be run with different robots, from single arms to bimanual arms and humanoids, and under variations of object poses, appearance, and physics, which change the scene but not the task itself. We use Gym-Anything for Robotics to construct RoboWorld, a collection of 25 simulations spanning domains such as manufacturing, construction, food service, laboratories, and logistics, with 25 test and 175 training RL environments of varying difficulty. RoboWorld is far from solved: even the best frontier models score 0% on the full benchmark, while on an easier subset, the best model reaches 32%. Beyond benchmarking, we introduce several new ways for models to learn on RoboWorld (§5). To do so, we exploit privileged access to the full ground-truth state, which is readily available in our simulations and a benefit of the simulation paradigm. We first show that models can improve on RoboWorld tasks through in-context learning, and that in-context learning is accelerated by access to the privileged simulation state. Since privileged state is unavailable at test time on a real robot, we develop three ways to leverage it as a learning signal, including (1) a teacher-student setup where the teacher has privileged access and teaches a student model through qualitative hints alone, (2) a “self-learning” setup where a model learns from its own privileged experience, and (3) a setup in which privileged code policies train the vision-language-action model (VLA) π0.5 [Physical Intelligence et al., 2025], which observes only camera images and robot proprioception. In summary, we contribute (1) Gym-Anything for Robotics, a modular framework and multi-agent pipeline for automatically building high-quality robotics simulations, together with our definitions of what makes a good robotics simulation and a good task; (2) RoboWorld, 25 GDP-grounded simulations with 25 test and 175 training RL environments, each supporting swappable robots and variations of object poses, appearance, and physics; (3) an evaluation showing that frontier models perform poorly on RoboWorld, with no model scoring above 0% on the full benchmark; and (4) several new ways for models to learn in simulation, showing that frontier models improve through in-context learning and do so faster with privileged state, that a frontier model with privileged state can teach a student model without it, even through verbal hints, that a model can learn from its own privileged experience, and that code policies can train VLAs such as π0.5.

2.1 Problem Setup Simulation. We refer to a simulation E as a scene implemented in code, consisting of objects with physical models (such as rigid, articulated, deformable, or liquid), a robot, cameras, and a physics engine that advances the physical state s of the scene (Figure 3). Each simulation specifies a default robot, which can be swapped for other robots from a shared catalog, from single arms to bimanual arms and humanoids. Each simulation further supports variations of object poses, appearance, and physics, which change the scene in which a task is performed but not the task itself. For instance, our welding simulation is a robotic welding cell, in which a UR10e arm carrying a welding torch and an FR3 arm with a gripper work at a fixture table holding a steel plate and a bracket. Three cameras observe the cell, and the physics engine melts and solidifies the steel under the welding arc. The same cell can also be run with five other robot setups, from a single FR3 arm to a Unitree G1 humanoid, and its 10,000 variations shift the plate, change how far the wire sticks out of the torch, and add a draft that can blow the shielding gas away from the weld. 

Task. A task T = ( Es0 , p, V ) consists of a simulation E with initial state s0, a natural-language instruction p, and a verification function V that maps the physical states of an episode to a binary success label and a reward r ∈ [0 , 1] . The reward measures both progress and outcome, and every successful completion receives r = 1 . For instance, in one welding task, s0 has the bracket tacked to the plate, p asks to “weld a continuous 1/4 in fillet along the near side in the horizontal position, end to end, and fill the crater,” and V inspects the solidified weld under the AWS D1.1 welding code, checking that it is continuous and fused, reaches the specified size, and stays within the limits on undercut and porosity. If the weld fails, r is proportional to the share of the required weld that is sound. Since swapping the robot or applying a variation changes only E and s0, this task can be run with any of the six robots and from any of the 10,000 variations. 

Interaction. At each step t, the agent receives an observation ot, consisting of images from the robot’s cameras and robot feedback such as joint positions, and issues a robot command at, such as a joint target, an end-effector motion, or a gripper command. The simulation advances physics while executing at and returns ot+1 . Physics advances only through commands, so the time an agent spends deciding its next action does not change the scene. An episode starts from s0 and ends when the agent finishes or exhausts its time budget, after which V determines the score. 

Restricted and privileged access. Unlike the real world, a simulation can expose its full ground-truth state, such as object poses, contact forces, and material states. We therefore provide two levels of access to each simulation. Restricted access provides only what is available on a real robot: the task instruction, camera images, robot feedback, and documentation of the permitted robot commands. Privileged access additionally exposes the simulator state and the task evaluation. Unless stated otherwise, evaluated agents use restricted access, and we study how privileged access can be used for learning in §5. 42.2 GDP-Grounded Simulation Selection 

For robots trained in simulation to be broadly useful, simulations must cover the wide variety of physical work that people value, both in their jobs and in everyday life. Similar to prior work [Ag-garwal et al., 2026], we therefore ground our selection in public U.S. data (Figure 2), combining the tasks of each occupation [Peterson et al., 2001], the economic value of each occupation [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025b, U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, 2025], and the time people spend on everyday activities [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025a]. However, what matters more than the tasks themselves are the atomic capabilities that they require, both from the simulation and from the robot. For instance, tightening a bolt requires a simulation that implements thread engage-ment and fastener friction, and a robot that can sense and limit torque. The same capabilities recur across many occupations and activities, so a simulation that provides them supports far more than a single task. This leads to a simple question: which set of simulations provides the capabilities needed for the most valuable physical work? To answer it, we use an LLM to decompose each task into atomic physical actions, such as threading a bolt or pouring a liquid, and weight each action by the value of its task. We then group actions that a single simulation can reproduce, and identify the physics and robotic capabilities that each group requires. For instance, the action group tighten contains 529 actions from different tasks, including tightening bolts with a wrench, driving screws, and threading spark plugs. Finally, we select action groups with an optimization that maximizes the total value of actions whose required capabilities are all supplied by the selected simulations. To ensure diversity, we run this selection in tiers covering the economic core, strategically important and STEM domains, all 22 major occupation groups, niche occupations, underrepresented capabilities such as fluids and cloth, and everyday activities. Notably, we do not restrict the selection to what current simulators support, since coding agents implement the physics each action group requires (§3). In total, we select 120 action groups, each defining one simulation. In this work, we build 25 of them, and we continue to add more simulations on our project website. Appendix A describes the full pipeline. 

3 Scaling Robotics Simulations 

Building a realistic simulation is hard and laborious, even for experts [Li et al., 2023, Nasiriany et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2024c]. It requires researching how an activity is performed, assembling a scene from real assets, modeling the physics of the materials involved, and verifying that the result behaves physically. It often requires implementing physics that existing engines lack, such as cutting or chemical reactions. The key idea is that creating a simulation is itself a coding agent task. Building a simulation means writing code, and verifying it means running the simulation and measuring physical quantities, which coding agents can do. However, before building simulations at scale, we must define what we are building, i.e., what makes a good robotics simulation? 

To our knowledge, there is no established definition, so we define one through ten criteria in four categories. A robotics simulation must meet all ten criteria in order to be considered useful for evaluating and training robot policies: • Realism. (a) The simulation is physically real to the degree that is required to represent a meaningful task. For instance, a titration simulation need not model every possible chem-ical reaction, but the solution must form drops, mix, and turn pink rather than red at the endpoint. (b) The scene looks real: ideally, a model shown a render and a real photograph cannot tell which one is real. (c) The scene and its tasks are relevant to the real-world activ-ities of the action group. (d) Physical parameters, such as masses and friction coefficients, are grounded in digital evidence wherever such evidence exists. • Dynamics and solvability. (e) The interaction dynamics are realistic, such as objects settling and staying at rest, and liquids mixing. (f) Every interaction that is required by the tasks is physically achievable, as shown by assisted demonstrations in which phantom forces are allowed. For instance, for writing on a whiteboard, it suffices to show that a hand can hold the marker, the cap can be removed, the tip can leave marks under contact, and the eraser can remove them (Figure 5). Creation-audit loop. Naively prompting even state-of-the-art coding agents results in simulations that fall short of these criteria. For instance, the agent may replace missing mechanics with anima-tions or attachments, tune parameters until the behavior looks right, or report behavior that it never verified. Moreover, unlike software, where the application is real and a screenshot shows its true state, the physics of a simulation is written by the agent itself, so a render can show an object that has no physical effect. Similar to prior work [Aggarwal et al., 2026], we therefore use a creation-audit loop (Figure 4). A creation agent receives a selected action group, researches how its activities are performed, designs a concrete scenario, and builds the simulation together with evidence that it meets each criterion, such as videos, physical measurements, and assisted demonstrations. Four independent audit agents, one per category, then check the simulation against the criteria above, including an adversarial agent that tries to break the simulation with legal robot actions, and report issues, which the creation agent fixes. We run three such rounds for every simulation, and all agents share a memory of lessons, so that each simulation benefits from earlier ones. In the instantiation of our pipeline described here, all agents are Claude Opus 5.5 [Anthropic, 2025a], run via a coding agent harness such as Claude Code [Anthropic, 2025b]. 4 Scaling Tasks 

For each simulation, we create multiple tasks. Recall from §2.1 that a task consists of an initial state, a natural-language instruction, and a verification function. Once a simulation is built, creating a task reduces to writing these, together with any task-specific setup of the scene. For instance, a task in the wine-pouring simulation asks: “Pour a 150 mL glass of red wine from the bottle on the coaster into the Bordeaux glass at spot 2 (135-165 mL), without spilling or leaving drips running down the bottle, then stand the bottle back on its coaster and leave the glass upright on its spot.” Crucially, the physics of the simulation stays fixed, since a simulation should not change depending on whether a model can complete a task. As with simulations, we first ask: what makes a good task? 

7Figure 5: The whiteboard simulation with each of its six robot setups. All six share the same classroom, markers, eraser, and physics. As with simulations, there is to our knowledge no established definition, so we define what a good task is through six criteria. A task must meet all six to be considered useful for evaluating and training robot policies: • Meaning. The task is a realistic and meaningful job, described naturally as one would describe it to a person, not as a checklist of verifier conditions. • Setting. The scene, objects, and arrangement suit the specific task. For instance, a free kick that curls the ball requires a defensive wall, and a dribbling task requires cones. • Verifier correctness. The verifier is deterministic and computed from the physical state of the simulation, without a VLM judge. It has neither false positives, such as accepting a cup placed over a ball for a task of putting the ball in the cup, nor false negatives, such as requiring the ball to rest at the exact center of the cup when the task did not ask for it. • Solvability. As for simulations, assisted demonstrations show that every interaction that the task requires is physically achievable, without requiring an end-to-end solution. • Reward quality. The reward gives continuous partial credit that reflects meaningful progress, and full credit to every valid solution, irrespective of the strategy used. For in-stance, a cleanup task credits each object placed in its correct location. • Difficulty coverage. Tasks that are all too easy or all too hard provide little signal for evaluating or improving models [Shao et al., 2024, Yu et al., 2025]. We therefore measure the difficulty of each task with a weaker and a stronger model, Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5, and target a spread of success rates for both. This gives a ladder of difficulty that remains informative for both weaker and stronger models. 

Task creation-audit loop. Similar to simulations, a task creation agent proposes tasks for each simulation, targeting five tasks per simulation that are diverse in the capabilities that they test, their difficulty, and the components of the simulation that they involve. Six independent audit agents, one per criterion, then check each task and report issues, which the task creation agent fixes, for at most three rounds. In addition, the agent uses recorded evaluation trajectories to create tasks of targeted difficulty. For instance, after observing a solver agent’s (in our instantiation, Claude Haiku’s) attempts at applying labels, the agent creates tasks targeting success rates of 100, 50, and 0%: repairing a single lifted label corner, repairing several lifted regions, and picking up a printed label and applying it around a curved specimen tube, respectively. See Appendix C for further details. 

An example task. Consider the whiteboard simulation shown in Figure 5. By default, a Unitree G1 humanoid stands about a meter from a classroom whiteboard, with six capped markers and a magnetic eraser in the tray. RoboWorld. Applying this pipeline to the simulations constructed in §3, we obtain RoboWorld, a collection of 200 RL environments across 25 simulations, 25 for testing and 175 for training, each with a deterministic verifier and a continuous reward. Each of the 200 environments supports swappable robots, from single arms to bimanual arms and humanoids, and variations of object poses, appearance, and physics. These variations are not considered to be separate tasks. Rather, they give many initial states of the same task, for training policies that generalize. We further take an easier subset of 25 tasks from the training split. Table 2 compares RoboWorld with existing robotics benchmarks. Several of them simulate liquids or cloth, are built by AI, or check their physics against real measurements, but RoboWorld is the only one that does all of these and also selects its simulations from job data. We release RoboWorld both as a benchmark and as RL environments for training. 95 Learning in Simulation with Frontier Models 

Realistic simulations open up a new way to learn physical skills: practicing in simulation, without the cost and risk of a real robot. Prior work mostly uses simulation to train small policies with reinforcement learning, often over millions of trials per task [Tobin et al., 2017, Peng et al., 2018, Makoviychuk et al., 2021]. Frontier models can learn in a different way, since they can reason about a task, write code, and improve from feedback within a single context. We therefore study how frontier models can learn in simulation, and how what they learn can be passed to the models and policies that control a real robot. To do so, we use three features of simulation that the real world lacks: privileged access to the full ground-truth state, instant resets to the same initial state, and initial states that are easy to vary. We first study how frontier models improve on RoboWorld tasks through in-context learning, and how privileged state accelerates this improvement. Since privileged state is unavailable on a real robot, we then develop three ways to pass its benefits to models and policies that lack it: coaching through qualitative hints, learning from a model’s own privileged experience, and training VLAs from privileged code policies (Figure 2). In particular, since a simulation provides privileged state and can be run at scale, a frontier model can write code policies whose demonstrations train small policies that can be deployed on a real robot, raising the success of π0.5 from 0% to 53% (§6.3). 

In-context learning. Since a simulation can be reset instantly, a frontier model can attempt the same task repeatedly and improve from its own failures within a single context, without any weight updates [Brown et al., 2020, Shinn et al., 2023]. After each attempt, the simulation is reset to the same initial state, and the agent continues in the same conversation and workspace, retaining its observations, code, and notes from earlier attempts. We run three attempts of three hours each. To measure the value of the ground-truth state, we compare agents with restricted access against agents with privileged access, which can additionally read the simulator state during each attempt. 

Teaching a student with privileged state. Since privileged state is unavailable outside simulation, we next study whether a model with privileged access can teach a student model that lacks it, similar to teacher-student training with privileged information in robot learning [Chen et al., 2020, Lee et al., 2020]. First, a teacher model with privileged access attempts the task, without knowing that it will later teach. Once it finishes, we fork its conversation and workspace into a coach, so that the coach keeps what the teacher learned about the task. A fresh student model with restricted access then attempts the task under the same budget, and may ask the coach for at most ten hints. Before each reply, the coach reads the current privileged state of the student’s simulation, so it can see mistakes the student cannot observe. It then replies with a single qualitative hint about the student’s mistake or approach. Hints never contain positions, coordinates, or numerical robot commands, and the coach cannot act on the simulation, so the student must still act on its own observations. We compare coached students against solo students with the same model, budget, and initial states. In our experiments, Claude Opus 5.5 serves as both the teacher and the student. 

Learning from privileged experience. We further study whether a model can transfer its own privileged experience to restricted execution, without a separate teacher. We resume the conversation and workspace of a teacher that succeeded with privileged access, and ask it to perform the same task with only restricted access on new variations of the scene, such as changed initial object poses. We compare its performance against students that attempt the same tasks without privileged experience. 

Training policies from code. In all settings above, a frontier model controls the robot step by step, which is slow and costly. Simulation enables a different approach: a frontier model with privileged access writes a policy as code [Liang et al., 2023], and the demonstrations of this code policy train a small VLA that can be deployed on a real robot. A code policy is a Python program that reads camera images, robot state, and privileged task-object state, and outputs only ordinary robot commands, so it cannot move objects, apply forces, change physics, or reset the simulation. For instance, a code policy removes the lid of a gift box with two arms by reading object poses and contact forces, solving the inverse kinematics of the arms, and sending joint targets. Since a code policy runs without model calls, it can be run cheaply across thousands of varied initial states. 6 Results 

6.1 Experimental Setup Library. To evaluate many kinds of policies, from frontier models to code policies and learned visuomotor policies, we build the Gym-Anything for Robotics library, which gives every simulation the same interface. As a result, any policy can run on any simulation without simulation-specific code. Behind this interface, each simulation can use its own physics engine and custom solvers, and its robot can be swapped for others, from single arms to bimanual arms and humanoids. The same simulation can also be run with restricted access, as on a real robot, or with privileged access to its full state (§2.1), so it serves evaluation, privileged experiments, and training alike. Appendix D provides further details. 

Robot interface. Each simulation runs as a server, and a policy connects to it over an authenticated HTTP API, whose key grants restricted or privileged access (§2.1). To support policies ranging from frontier models to code policies and VLAs, a policy can command the robot at several levels. It can set joint targets or end-effector coordinates, which a controller tracks, or send joint torques and, on humanoids, individual motor commands. Like a computer-use agent, it can also press game-style keys, such as W, A, S, and D to move the end effector, each held for a set number of control ticks. 

Agents. We evaluate three frontier models with extra-high reasoning effort: GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Terra, and Claude Opus 5.5 [Anthropic, 2025a]. Each runs as a coding agent in its own harness, Claude Code [Anthropic, 2025b] for Claude Opus 5.5 and Codex [OpenAI, 2025] for the GPT models, and controls the robot with restricted access through a small set of tools, such as observe ,which returns camera images and robot feedback, and act , which executes a robot command. The learning experiments (§6.3) use Claude Opus 5.5 in every role. 

Evaluation. Each attempt runs on an independent copy of the simulation, starting from the task’s initial state, with a wall-clock budget of three hours. The verifier scores each attempt after the agent finishes. During the attempt, agents do not observe rewards. We report two metrics: (1) success rate, i.e., the fraction of attempts that the verifier labels as successful, and (2) average reward, i.e., the mean of the continuous reward r ∈ [0 , 1] , which captures partial progress.

Frontier models improve through in-context learning, and privileged state makes it faster. 

With restricted access, Opus 5.5 steadily improves across attempts, from 0.14 to 0.22, a 54% rela-tive improvement without any weight updates. With privileged access, Opus 5.5 improves substan-tially faster: its first attempt already achieves 0.34, outperforming the restricted agent after all three attempts, and its third attempt reaches 0.41. For instance, on football and welding, the privileged agent achieves full reward from its first attempt, while the restricted agent does not reach full reward on either task even after three attempts. Since the two settings differ only in access to the simulator state, this gap shows that the ground-truth state of a simulation is a strong learning signal. 

Privileged state transfers to a student even through verbal hints. Since privileged state is un-available outside simulation, we next examine whether its benefits can be passed to a model that lacks it. Remarkably, the coached student, which never observes the privileged state and receives only qualitative hints, achieves 0.29 on its first attempt, a 2.1 × improvement over the solo agent and higher than the solo agent after all three attempts. This recovers 75% of the gap between the solo and privileged agents from the very first attempt. On football and welding, the coached student achieves full reward on every attempt, matching its teacher. We attribute these gains to the design of the coach: before each hint, it reads the true state of the student’s simulation, and can there-fore point out mistakes that the student cannot observe directly. A single hint can also change the student’s whole strategy. For instance, in towel folding, GPT-6 Astra failed for over four hours to hold the towel with both grippers at once. Frontier models write code policies whose demonstrations train small VLAs. We test this on two tasks in a gift box simulation with two FR3 arms: removing the lid and placing it on the table without disturbing the box, and opening the box to move a named block into a tray. Code policies written with privileged access succeed on 96.7% of 2,048 varied initial states, 99.8% for lid removal and 93.7% for block retrieval. We keep 1,000 successful rollouts, 500 per task, spread evenly across eight groups of physics variations, and train π0.5 on them. Although π0.5 observes only three camera views and robot proprioception, the trained policy raises success on these tasks from 0% to 53% (Figure 7, right). It also keeps this success on variations that it never saw in training: with unseen object poses, appearance, and physics, it succeeds in 55% of attempts. Moreover, π0.5 matches the success rate of Claude Opus 5.5 controlling the robot step by step, and comes close in average reward, 0.87 against 0.93. It also completes a task in about two minutes, 14 times faster than Claude Opus 5.5, which takes about 29 minutes. This shows that, with simulation, a frontier model can write code policies that produce thousands of demonstrations, which can train a small, fast policy such as π0.5 that can in principle be deployed on a real robot. 

6.4 Simulation Quality Our simulations outperform the strongest prior simulators of the same interactions. For each of our 25 simulations, research agents searched the web for prior simulators of the same core phys-ical interaction, such as liquid pouring or cloth folding, and selected the strongest one with public code and a paper, ranked mainly by the physical accuracy of its solver and fixed before any compar-ison was run. Claude Opus 5.5 then compared each pair blind, with the two simulators labeled only A and B and their order swapped across two runs, using a prompt separate from the audit prompts that guided our simulations, so that the comparison does not favor our own criteria. Our simulations lose to the prior simulator in only 14% of comparisons on visuals and 24% on physics, and win 80% and 76%, respectively (Figure 9). 

Audits catch incorrect physics. We attribute the realism of our simulations to the creation-audit loop (§3). When auditors found a physics error, it was typically in the first round. For instance, in welding, the physics engine moved a loose bracket, while the heat model and the renderer kept a copy of it at its starting pose, so the arc welded a bracket that was no longer there. In ingredient mixing, a tilted pan lost most of its sauce through its steel wall before the sauce could reach the rim. The creation agent fixed both errors in the next round. 

Adversarial audits break most simulations with legal robot actions. The adversarial audit agent (§3) tries to break each simulation using only legal robot actions, and it found a real defect in about three quarters of our simulations. For instance, in football, pushing the ball into the turf made the turf model launch it out at high speed without any robot contact. In ingredient mixing, lowering the bowl past the edge of the counter left the sauce floating at counter height. The creation agent repaired these defects in the next round and verified each repair by replaying the audit’s own reproduction. 14 Task audits catch imperfect verifiers. The task audits (§4) test each verifier on constructed states, such as an untouched scene or a partly finished task. They often found several issues in a verifier. For instance, in barcode scanning, most checks only tested that nothing had gone wrong, so an untouched shelf scored higher than a partly scanned one. In football, the verifier ruled genuine goals just inside a post as misses, so these episodes could never succeed. These audits ensured that rewards are progressive, i.e., that more progress earns more credit, which is important for training with RL. 

7 Related Work 

Simulation for robot learning. Fast physics engines [Todorov et al., 2012, Makoviychuk et al., 2021, Tao et al., 2025, Genesis Authors, 2024] support many robotics benchmarks, but these bench-marks are built mostly by hand and therefore cover a narrow slice of physical work (Table 2). Most target rigid and articulated objects in tabletop, household, or workplace scenes [Yu et al., 2020, James et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2023, Li et al., 2021, Nasiriany et al., 2024, 2026, Lan et al., 2026, Gajo et al., 2025], while others target a single class of deformable materials, such as liquids [Xian et al., 2023, Li et al., 2025b, Gong et al., 2023, Li et al., 2025a], cloth [Seita et al., 2021, Lu et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025b, Hu et al., 2025], or plasticine [Huang et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2022], and only a few combine several [Lin et al., 2020, Gu et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, Li et al., 2026]. BEHAVIOR-1K [Li et al., 2023] comes closest to broad coverage, with liquids, cloth, and activities from time-use surveys. In contrast, RoboWorld is built by coding agents, which implement physics that existing engines lack, and its simulations are selected by the economic value of physical work. 

Reliable physics in simulation. Prior work makes simulated physics more faithful through system identification [Tan et al., 2018], randomization [Tobin et al., 2017, Peng et al., 2018], real-to-sim reconstruction [Torne et al., 2024], and specialized solvers [Xian et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2024b, Heiden et al., 2021], and recent benchmarks measure the sim-to-real gap directly for cloth [Blanco-Mulero et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2025], but each of these requires experts to model the physics of a single task or material. LLM-based generators instead scale simulation creation by writing task code, demonstration programs, scenes, or rewards [Wang et al., 2024a, Hua et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2026, Yang et al., 2024b, Wang et al., 2024c, Ma et al., 2024], but they build on the fixed physics of an existing engine and check their output through policy success or visual inspection rather than physical measurements. Building on the creation-audit loop of Gym-Anything [Aggarwal et al., 2026], our coding agents implement the physics itself, and audit agents check it through physical measurements, assisted demonstrations, and adversarial robot actions. 

Frontier models learning in simulation. Frontier models act in simulated and physical worlds by writing code [Liang et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023] or by controlling robots directly [Gemini Robotics Team, 2025], are evaluated as embodied agents [Yang et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2024], and improve from repeated attempts in context [Brown et al., 2020, Shinn et al., 2023]. Separately, robot learning uses the privileged state of simulators to train students from privileged teachers [Chen et al., 2020, Lee et al., 2020, Pinto et al., 2018] and to generate demonstrations at scale [Mandlekar et al., 2023, Nasiriany et al., 2024], but these teachers pass their knowledge to students through weight updates. We bring the two lines together, showing that privileged state helps frontier models learn in context, reaches a student through qualitative hints alone or through the model’s own privileged experience, and trains VLAs through privileged code policies. Appendix G provides an extended discussion of related work. 

8 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced Gym-Anything for Robotics, a scalable framework in which coding agents build realistic robotics simulations. By framing simulation creation as a coding agent task and checking each simulation and task in a multi-agent loop of creation, auditing, and repair, Gym-Anything for Robotics addresses a central bottleneck in robot learning: the difficulty of building realistic simulations at scale. Applying this framework, we built RoboWorld, a GDP-grounded col-lection of 200 RL environments across 25 simulations spanning liquids, cloth, and physics that exist-ing engines lack, with swappable robots and train and test splits. We further showed that privileged simulator state more than doubles the first-attempt reward of frontier models, and that it can teach a student through hints alone and train π0.5 from 0% to 53% success. At the same time, RoboWorld 15 is challenging even for frontier models, which fail every test task, indicating that realistic physical work remains far from solved. More broadly, our results suggest that simulation has been held back not by the idea of simulation, but by unrealistic simulations, and that coding agents can now build realistic ones in which far more capable frontier models learn in ways that the real world does not allow. We hope that Gym-Anything for Robotics, RoboWorld, and the released code provide a foundation for future work on realistic robot learning, including more capable embodied agents, new ways to learn from privileged state, and broader coverage of physical work.

This appendix provides the complete details of the selection pipeline summarized in §2.2. 

Estimating the value of tasks. For paid work, we use the O*NET database, which describes 1,016 occupations through 18,796 task statements, each rated for importance and frequency [Peterson et al., 2001]. Similar to prior work [Aggarwal et al., 2026], we estimate the economic value of each occupation as its wage bill (employment × mean wage) from Bureau of Labor Statistics data [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025b], scaled to national accounts data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis [U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, 2025]. We then distribute each occupation’s value across its tasks in proportion to their importance and frequency ratings. However, paid work misses much of what people physically do, such as cooking, cleaning, and caring for others. For everyday life, we therefore use the American Time Use Survey (ATUS), which estimates the time the U.S. population spends on each of 465 activities [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025a]. We keep the two streams separate throughout, since dollars and population-hours measure different quantities and combining them would require an arbitrary exchange rate. 

Decomposing tasks into atomic physical actions. Not all tasks are physical, and a physical task usually involves several distinct physical actions. We use an LLM to estimate the fraction of each task that requires physical interaction, and to decompose its physical portion into atomic physical actions, each a single physical state change such as threading a bolt into a nut or pouring a liquid into a container. Grouping actions and identifying capabilities. The same physical action often appears across many occupations and activities. We therefore group action occurrences that a single parameter-ized simulation can reproduce. Sharing wording or purpose is not enough: two occurrences are grouped only if their state change, contact sequence, effector, material regime, and completion con-dition remain the same when objects and scale are substituted. Tiered selection. We select action groups across six tiers, each maximizing the coverage it adds given all previous tiers: (1) the economic core, maximizing coverage of U.S. economic value (20 action groups), (2a/2b) strategically important domains (Healthcare, Education, Protective Services, Transportation) and STEM domains (Engineering, Computing, Science), maximizing the coverage of the weakest domain (20 each), (3) cycling through all 22 SOC major occupation groups (23), (4) 22 actions specific to niche occupations that broader tiers leave uncovered (9), (5) the weakest robotic and physics capability families, such as fluids, cloth, granular materials, and cutting (8), and (6) everyday activities, maximizing the coverage of the weakest of 14 ATUS activity domains (20). Since capabilities complement each other, we solve each tier as an exact batch optimization rather than adding one action group at a time. The main text reports the selection with coverage defined by both physics and robotic capabilities. We also run the selection with coverage defined by physics capabilities alone and by robotic capabilities alone, each again selecting 120 action groups, and obtain 195 distinct action groups across the three runs. 

B Simulation Creation: Full Details 

This appendix provides further details of the creation-audit loop summarized in §3. 

Agents. Each agent in our framework is an instance of Claude Opus 5.5 [Anthropic, 2025a] with extra-high reasoning effort, run via a coding agent harness such as Claude Code [Anthropic, 2025b], and differentiated by the objective described in its prompt. All agents work in a shared workspace containing our library, existing simulations, and the shared memory. We keep the prompts short and direct: rather than prescribing how to implement a simulation, they describe what a good simulation is, and audits check whether the result satisfies it. 

Creation agent. The creation agent receives the complete record of an action group, including all of its action variants, required capabilities, and grounding, rather than only its name. It then works in two phases within the same session. In the first phase, research and design, the agent reads ev-ery action and capability in the group and researches how the activity is performed: its real setting, objects, equipment, dimensions, materials, mechanisms, and the robot typically used, using primary documentation, published research, and other digital evidence. It then designs a concrete scenario that exercises the capabilities of the action group, and works through the equations, numerical im-plementation, and engine support the scenario requires before running expensive experiments. In the second phase, the agent builds, demonstrates, and documents the simulation. It assembles the scene from real assets, typically combining a photorealistic Gaussian-splat backdrop [Kerbl et al., 2023], which carries no physics, with explicit physical bodies for every object the robot can touch. It implements the task-relevant physics, including physics the engine does not provide, integrates a de-fault robot from the shared catalog, and defines tasks. Crucially, every change in the world must be physically caused by the robot: missing mechanics may not be replaced by animations, attachments, or scripted outcomes. The agent then collects evidence, including videos, physical measurements, and assisted demonstrations, and writes a report covering the scenario, sources, equations, param-eter choices, experiments, and limitations. We send the build prompt only after the research phase finishes, without announcing it earlier, so that the agent researches diligently instead of hurrying to build. 

Blind nudge. However, the agent often stops with a simulation that falls short of our existing sim-ulations. Similar to the re-prompting used in prior work [Aggarwal et al., 2026], once the build phase finishes, we send a single fixed message that points the agent to our strongest existing simula-tions and states that its simulation is a regression from them and needs improvement across multiple aspects. The message contains no diagnosis, so the agent must identify the shortcomings itself. 

Audit agents. Each of the four audit agents checks one category of criteria from §3. In each round, fresh audit agents examine the current simulation and write reports, which are returned to the same creation agent to repair the code, scene, and evidence. The audit agent for dynamics and solvability judges the dynamics from the videos and scripts of the creation agent, and from its own runs of the simulation, assisted by a video model. The audit agent for speed and configuration requires that a simulation run at most 200 times slower than real time. The audit agent for robustness reads the code and searches for legal robot actions that produce non-finite values, energy blow-ups, or physically impossible motion, and returns its evidence to the creation agent. The written audits further let us independently verify the quality of each simulation. 

Shared memory. As in prior work [Aggarwal et al., 2026], agents accumulate lessons in a shared memory of Markdown files, so that lessons from one simulation guide all subsequent ones. For 23 instance, after we found colliders that passed audits and appeared in renders while having no physical effect, the memory records that a component exists only once a physical probe shows it acting on another body. 

C Task Creation: Full Details 

This appendix provides further details of the task creation-audit loop summarized in §4. 

Fixed physics. The task creation agent may change task descriptions, verifiers, task catalogs, and task-specific scene setup, but not the physical parameters, contact and material models, solver set-tings, or robot dynamics of existing objects. 

Task creation agent. Each task either represents realistic work or probes a nuanced robotic ca-pability. Ordinary workmanship, such as not leaving air bubbles under a label, stays implicit in the task description. 

Verifier correctness. Where possible, verifiers measure the physical mechanism or sensor signal that defines completion rather than only object poses. For instance, a barcode label counts as applied only if the barcode is decoded from a rendered camera image. In practice, the auditor found that the verifiers of a labeling simulation rejected otherwise valid completions in which the part had moved by 60 mm, because of a 50 mm displacement limit that the task never stated. 

Reward quality. The reward does not prescribe a method, an order of steps, or a reference trajec-tory. 

Difficulty measurement. We evaluate Claude Haiku 5.5 with four seeds and Claude Opus 5.5 with two seeds, using restricted access and a six-hour wall-clock budget per attempt. Across the tasks, we target Haiku success bands of 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100%, and Opus bands of 0, 50, and 100%. The verifier scores each attempt after the agent finishes, and no reward reaches the agent during the attempt. The target success bands are approximate guidelines across all tasks rather than exact rates for each task. 

Audit rounds. In each round, fresh audit agents review the tasks, and their reports are returned to the same task creation agent for repair. 

D The Gym-Anything for Robotics Library 

This appendix provides further details of the library summarized in §6.1. 

Structure. Similar to prior work [Aggarwal et al., 2026], the library separates three parts: the core runtime, simulation packages, and agents. The core provides the registry of simulations, session management, authentication, decoding of robot commands, recording, a command-line interface, and a browser dashboard. Each simulation package contains its scene, robots, physics, sensing, task definitions, verifiers, and scene variations. Agents are independent clients, either Python plugins or separate processes that connect over HTTP, so an agent can be replaced without changing any sim-ulation. The Python API, the command-line interface, and the HTTP API all construct simulations through the same registry. 

Physics. Simulations can use different physics engines and custom solvers behind the same inter-face. All simulations use a command-driven clock: physics advances only while a robot command executes, so an idle session does not change. 

Access control. Restricted credentials receive only the task instruction, camera images, robot feed-back, and documentation of the permitted robot commands. They cannot read simulator geometry, the session history, or the verifier. Privileged credentials additionally expose the simulator state and the task evaluation, which returns the reward and an explicit success label without advancing time. 24 Tasks. Each simulation owns its task catalog, which maps named tasks or numbered scene varia-tions to an instruction, an initial state, and a verifier. 

Recording. Every attempt records each command request and response, the camera images at command boundaries, the launch configuration and source version, and the final evaluation, so that each result can be inspected and reproduced. 

Agent tools. For LLM agents, we expose restricted access through six tools: help (permitted robot commands, units, limits, and robot and camera calibration), task (the task instruction), observe 

(current camera images and robot feedback), act (execute a robot command), time left (remain-ing wall-clock budget), and finish (end the attempt). 

Robot commands. Every policy, from a frontier model to a VLA, controls the robot through the same set of commands (Table 4). Each simulation accepts the subset that its robot supports, which the help tool lists together with units and limits. Commands range from task-space targets, which a controller tracks, to joint torques and, on humanoids, individual motor commands. Arm motions can also stop early on contact, on contact between the two arms, or when the grip is lost. Like a computer-use agent, a policy can instead hold game-style key chords for a chosen number of control ticks (Table 5), which move the end effector at 5 mm/s and rotate it at 0.04 rad/s by default. F Additional Details on Learning in Simulation 

An example of coaching. In towel folding, GPT-6 Astra attempted the task as a coached student with restricted access. After more than four and a half hours and five hints, it still could not hold the towel with both grippers at once, and asked its coach for a single qualitative change. The coach replied: 

“Try a staged fold using one gripper: place one end of the long edge onto its opposite coun-terpart, release onto the table, then regrasp and bring the remaining section over. Keeping most fabric supported reduces the load on each grasp and avoids needing two sustained holds.” 

The student then folded the towel with its left gripper alone, while its right arm stayed idle. Its score rose with each following action, and it succeeded about 40 minutes after the hint. 

G Extended Related Work 

This appendix expands on the related work in §7. 

Simulation for robot learning. Simulation has long been a central tool for robot learning, and physics engines such as MuJoCo [Todorov et al., 2012], Isaac Gym [Makoviychuk et al., 2021], ManiSkill3 [Tao et al., 2025], and Genesis [Genesis Authors, 2024] make it fast, with GPU-parallel engines running thousands of environments at once. Benchmarks built on these engines cover table-top manipulation of rigid and articulated objects [Yu et al., 2020, James et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2023], 37 household environments [Li et al., 2021, Nasiriany et al., 2024, 2026], and specific workplaces such as laboratories [Li et al., 2025a, Lan et al., 2026] and retail stores [Gajo et al., 2025]. A separate line of benchmarks targets deformable materials, such as liquids [Xian et al., 2023, Li et al., 2025b, Gong et al., 2023], cloth and garments [Seita et al., 2021, Lu et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025b, Hu et al., 2025], plasticine [Huang et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2022], or several of these [Lin et al., 2020, Gu et al., 2023, Chen et al., 2023, Li et al., 2026]. BEHAVIOR-1K [Li et al., 2023] comes closest to broad coverage, simulating both liquids and cloth and grounding its 1,000 household activities in time-use surveys. However, these benchmarks are built mostly by hand, so each covers only the materials and activities that its authors chose and could implement, and adding a new kind of physics requires substantial expert effort. Moreover, none of the benchmarks in Table 2 selects its tasks or simulations by the economic value of physical work, and only RGBench [Hu et al., 2025], which targets garments, checks its simulated physics against real measurements. In contrast, Gym-Anything for Robotics uses coding agents to build each simulation, including physics that existing engines lack, selects simulations based on occupation and time-use data, and checks the physics of each simulation against physical theory and, where available, real measurements. 

Reliable physics in simulation. Whether simulated physics can be trusted is a long-standing ques-tion in robotics, since policies trained in simulation degrade when its physics differs from the real world. A large body of work narrows this sim-to-real gap by identifying physical parameters from real data [Tan et al., 2018], randomizing them during training [Tobin et al., 2017, Peng et al., 2018], or reconstructing real scenes in simulation [Torne et al., 2024], while specialized solvers model materials that general-purpose engines handle poorly, such as liquids [Xian et al., 2023], thin shells [Wang et al., 2024b], and cutting [Heiden et al., 2021]. These methods can produce faithful physics for a specific task, but each requires experts to model and tune the physics by hand. Recent benchmarks also measure this gap directly, by comparing simulated cloth with real cloth manipu-lated by robots [Blanco-Mulero et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2025], but they cover a single material. A more recent line of work uses LLMs to generate simulations, writing task code [Wang et al., 2024a, Hua et al., 2024], demonstration programs [Chen et al., 2026], scenes [Yang et al., 2024b, Wang et al., 2024c], or reward functions [Ma et al., 2024]. However, these methods generate tasks and scenes on top of the fixed physics of an existing engine, which largely limits them to rigid and ar-ticulated objects, and they check their output through policy success, visual inspection, or human review rather than by measuring the simulated physics. Our work builds on the creation-audit loop of Gym-Anything [Aggarwal et al., 2026], which verifies environments built from real software, and extends it to physics, where coding agents implement the physics itself and audit agents check it through physical measurements, assisted demonstrations, and adversarial robot actions. 

Frontier models learning in simulation. Frontier models increasingly act in simulated and phys-ical worlds, either by writing programs that call perception and control primitives [Liang et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2023] or by controlling robots directly [Gemini Robotics Team, 2025], and sev-eral benchmarks evaluate multimodal models as embodied agents [Yang et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2024]. Such models can also improve without weight updates, by learning from repeated attempts in context [Brown et al., 2020, Shinn et al., 2023] or by building a library of skills as code, as Voyager [Wang et al., 2023] does in Minecraft. Separately, robot learning has long used the priv-ileged state of simulators, both by training a teacher with access to the true state and distilling it into a student that observes only sensors [Chen et al., 2020, Lee et al., 2020, Pinto et al., 2018], and by generating demonstrations in simulation at scale to train visuomotor policies [Mandlekar et al., 2023, Nasiriany et al., 2024]. However, these two lines of work rarely meet: privileged teachers pass their knowledge to students through weight updates, while frontier-model agents have been studied mainly in games and rigid-object scenes, without privileged state as a learning signal. We bring the two together on realistic simulations, showing that privileged state accelerates the in-context learn-ing of frontier models, that a privileged frontier model can teach a student through qualitative hints alone, that a model can learn from its own privileged experience, and that privileged code policies can train VLAs that observe only cameras and proprioception. 

Economic grounding of benchmarks. A growing body of work connects AI capabilities to the economy through occupation data, most prominently by estimating which occupations and tasks in O*NET [Peterson et al., 2001] are exposed to language models [Eloundou et al., 2024]. These stud-ies measure exposure rather than guide the design of benchmarks, but recent benchmarks have begun to use economic value directly: GDPval [Patwardhan et al., 2025] evaluates models on tasks from 44 38 occupations across the nine sectors that contribute most to U.S. GDP, and Gym-Anything [Aggar-wal et al., 2026] selects software for computer-use environments by its attributed GDP. In robotics, BEHAVIOR-1K [Li et al., 2023] grounds its household activities in time-use surveys, but to our knowledge no prior robotics simulation benchmark draws on occupation data. Gym-Anything for Robotics extends GDP-grounded selection to physical work, combining the tasks and wages of each occupation with the time that people spend on everyday activities [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025a], and selects simulations that supply the physical capabilities that the most valuable actions require. 39
