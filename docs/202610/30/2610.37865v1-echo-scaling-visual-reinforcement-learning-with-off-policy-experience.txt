# Echo: Scaling Visual Reinforcement Learning with Off-Policy Experience 

Gagan Khandate 

gagank@cs.columbia.edu 

Abstract: Scaling visual reinforcement learning (RL) with GPU-accelerated simu-lation is computationally expensive due to rendering and visual-policy inference costs. Consequently, two-stage teacher–student distillation is commonly used to train visual policies, but introduces an imitation gap. In this preliminary study, we introduce Echo , a single-stage off-policy RL framework that scales experience collection without proportionally increasing visual computation . Through in-verse distillation , a state-based auxiliary policy imitates the evolving visual policy and collects inexpensive, unrendered experience. Echo leverages this experience for off-policy value learning, directly improving the visual policy through a shared privileged critic. We instantiate Echo with FastTD3 and evaluate it on three visual control tasks and two dexterous manipulation tasks in Genesis. Relative to SDPG 

under the evaluated configurations, Echo reduces time to reference returns by an average of 60% across the three locomotion tasks and 56% across both hand tasks , while improving peak mean returns by 62% and 12% , respectively. Both hand tasks also achieve higher mean returns at matched simulator-transition bud-gets. These initial results suggest that scaling off-policy experience is a promising approach to efficient direct visual RL.

Massively parallel simulation has expanded the scale of reinforcement learning (RL) and underpins many recent advances in robot learning, including simulated driving [7], locomotion [20, 26], and contact-rich assembly [27]. While parallel physics simulation enables collecting large volumes of experience, visual control introduces additional computational costs from observation rendering and visual-policy inference , which can grow substantially with model size. Consequently, scaling visual RL remains considerably more expensive than scaling state-based RL. Teacher–student distillation is a common approach to training visual control policies using privileged simulator state [10, 15, 5]. A state-based teacher first learns the task and subsequently supervises a visual student through its actions. However, the teacher may condition its behavior on information unavailable to the student, making certain decisions difficult to reproduce [8]. This information asym-metry creates an imitation gap , further compounded by distribution shift resulting from student errors [12]. Directly optimizing the visual policy avoids this teacher–student mismatch, but reintroduces the computational costs of visual experience collection. Recent works have explored more efficient approaches to direct visual RL. Squint combines parallel simulation with low-resolution visual observations [1], although aggressive downsampling may discard visual details essential for precise manipulation. More recently, SDPG reduces rendering costs by estimating policy improvements from perturbed, unrendered rollouts using zeroth-order optimization [17]. While zeroth-order methods avoid differentiating through contact dynamics, their update directions are estimated from finite samples of perturbed trajectories. In contact-rich manipulation, small perturbations can induce different contact modes and substantially different returns, potentially increasing estimator variance. Critic-based first-order methods offer an alternative by providing learned, differentiable optimization signals, although their effectiveness depends on the accuracy of the value function. Furthermore, the on-policy formulation of SDPG limits the reuse of previously collected experience. Recent advances in off-policy RL have demonstrated large scale off-policy experience can be effec-tively combined with massively parallel simulation. Methods such as FastTD3 [14] and FlashSAC [28] leverage parallel experience collection, replay-based value learning, and efficient critic optimiza-tion to achieve rapid learning in high-dimensional robotic control tasks. These advances demonstrate the potential of off-policy RL to exploit large volumes of simulation experience through repeated value and policy updates. However, directly extending this paradigm to visual RL remains com-putationally expensive, as collecting additional experience using a visual policy requires rendering observations and evaluating the policy at every environment step. This raises a central question: can we scale off-policy experience collection for visual RL without proportionally scaling rendering and visual-policy inference? 

We present a preliminary study of Echo , a single-stage off-policy RL framework that addresses this challenge through inverse distillation . Unlike conventional teacher–student distillation, where a privileged state-based teacher supervises a visual student, Echo reverses the direction of supervision: an auxiliary state-based policy learns to imitate the evolving visual policy . This state-based imitator enables inexpensive experience collection without camera rendering or visual-policy inference ,while approximately tracking the state distribution induced by the visual policy. Crucially, the additional unrendered experience is not merely used to supervise the visual policy, but directly contributes to its improvement through off-policy reinforcement learning. Specifically, Echo learns a shared privileged critic from both rendered and unrendered experience 

stored in replay. The state-based imitator serves two complementary purposes: (1) collecting inexpensive transitions that remain approximately in-distribution with the evolving visual policy, and (2) providing continuation actions for temporal-difference updates of the state-based critic. The visual policy is then improved directly through critic-guided first-order updates using a smaller set of paired state–observation samples. By separating large-scale experience collection from expensive visual-policy evaluation, Echo combines the scalability and experience reuse of off-policy RL with 2the benefits of direct visual policy optimization, without requiring a separately trained privileged teacher. Our contributions are: • Scalable off-policy visual RL. We introduce Echo , a framework that decouples the scale of experience collection from visual rendering and inference, enabling large-scale off-policy value learning from inexpensive, unrendered simulation transitions. • Inverse distillation for experience collection. We propose a state-based imitator trained to track the evolving visual policy, reversing conventional teacher–student supervision. The imitator enables approximately on-distribution auxiliary rollouts and supplies continuation actions for off-policy critic learning, allowing unrendered experience to directly improve the visual policy. • Preliminary evaluation on visual control and dexterous manipulation. We instantiate

2 Related Work 

Privileged learning and policy distillation. Simulation provides privileged state information that can facilitate visual policy learning without being available at deployment. Asymmetric actor– critic methods use privileged state for value estimation while restricting the actor to deployment observations [11]. Alternatively, teacher–student distillation trains a privileged state-based policy and transfers its behavior to a visual student [9, 10]. More recent approaches train the teacher and student concurrently, combining action supervision with direct policy improvement [15, 5]. However, privileged teachers may rely on information unavailable to the visual student, creating an imitation gap [8]. Related approaches have also leveraged privileged policies for experience collection. Schwab et al. employ feature-based auxiliary policies to generate experience for vision-based control [4], while APRiL trains state-based and visual agents with shared experience [3]. In contrast, Echo 

employs inverse distillation , where a state-based auxiliary policy imitates the evolving visual policy to collect inexpensive experience for off-policy value learning, rather than supervising the visual policy. 

Scaling off-policy reinforcement learning. Massively parallel GPU simulation has enabled rapid reinforcement learning for robotic control [19, 20]. Recent methods such as FastTD3 [14] and Flash-SAC [28] extend these scaling benefits to off-policy RL, leveraging parallel experience collection, replay buffers, and efficient critic optimization. By reusing collected transitions across optimization updates, off-policy methods can improve sample efficiency while benefiting from high simulation throughput. However, applying this paradigm directly to visual RL incurs substantial rendering and visual-policy inference costs as experience collection scales. Echo addresses this bottleneck by collecting additional state-based experience for off-policy critic learning without requiring visual observations at every simulation step. 

Efficient visual reinforcement learning. Recent methods have reduced the computational costs of direct visual RL. Squint uses low-resolution visual observations with massively parallel simulation to reduce rendering and inference costs [1]. SDPG limits rendering to a subset of environments and estimates policy improvements from unrendered perturbation rollouts using zeroth-order optimization [17]. While these approaches reduce visual computation, they do not exploit large-scale unrendered experience through off-policy value learning. 4.1 Inverse Distillation for Rendering-Free Experience Collection 

The first component of Echo is a state-based auxiliary policy that follows the evolving visual policy, rather than serving as a privileged teacher. By approximating visual-policy actions from simulator state, this auxiliary policy can collect additional experience without rendering observations or evaluating the visual network. 

Idea 1 Distill the evolving visual policy into a state-based auxiliary policy that collects unrendered experience. 

Rendered and Unrendered Experience. We partition N parallel environments into two streams. In M rendered (nominal) environments, the visual policy πθ (o) acts using visual observations, and we retain paired states and observations. In the remaining N − M environments, the auxiliary policy 

μψ (s) acts directly from privileged simulator state, avoiding observation rendering and visual-policy inference. Both streams use exploration noise, with actions clipped to the task’s action bounds. A shared replay buffer D stores transitions (s, a, r, s ′, d ) from both streams, where s′ denotes the successor state before any reset and d indicates true termination. Time-limit truncations retain bootstrapping. A separate rendered replay buffer Dnom stores paired state–observation samples (s, o )

for visual-policy optimization. Thus, all collected experience contributes to critic learning, while updates requiring visual observations are restricted to rendered samples. 

Inverse Policy Distillation. The auxiliary policy learns to reproduce the current visual policy’s noise-free actions using privileged simulator state. Fresh rendered state–observation pairs define the nominal distribution ρnom , yielding the imitation objective

4.2 Shared Critic Learning and Visual Policy Improvement 

The second component of Echo is off-policy value learning using a shared privileged critic trained on experience collected by both the visual policy and its state-based imitator. The critic enables the visual policy to benefit from large-scale unrendered experience through first-order policy improvement, without requiring visual observations for every transition. 

Idea 2 Scale off-policy value learning with rendered and unrendered experience, and improve the visual policy directly through critic gradients. 

Learning the Shared Critic without Successor Images. We train the shared critic using transitions sampled from both rendered and unrendered replay. Although unrendered transitions lack visual observations, their privileged successor states remain available. The state-based imitator provides approximate continuation actions for the visual policy, allowing us to construct temporal-difference (TD) targets without rendering successor observations or evaluating the visual policy. 5 Experimental Results 

Our experiments investigate whether scaling off-policy experience through inverse distillation im-proves the efficiency of direct visual reinforcement learning. We evaluate Echo on visual control and dexterous manipulation tasks in Genesis, addressing three questions: 1. Learning efficiency: How does Echo compare with SDPG in wall-clock training time, simulator-transition efficiency, and achieved returns? 2. Auxiliary policy design: How does exponential moving average (EMA) stabilization of the state-based imitator affect learning performance and variability across seeds? 3. Learning dynamics: How accurately does the state-based imitator track the evolving visual policy, and how do their critic action gradients compare?

5.2 Tasks 

We evaluate three visual locomotion tasks (Hopper, Walker, and Ant) and two dexterous manipulation tasks (Allegro Hand and Shadow Hand) using the Genesis implementations released by You et al. [17]. The locomotion tasks are adapted from the MuJoCo benchmark, while the dexterous manipulation tasks involve in-hand cube reorientation with egocentric visual observations. Training budgets are one hour for Hopper, 2.5 hours for Walker, four hours for Ant, and three hours for each hand task. We report returns against both wall-clock training time and total simulator transitions, including auxiliary experience collected by Echo .

Simulator-transition efficiency. The advantages extend beyond wall-clock training time. 5.4 Design Choices and Ablations Auxiliary-policy averaging. We examine the effect of exponential moving average (EMA) stabi-lization of the auxiliary policy by comparing τμ = 0 .1 against τμ = 1 , corresponding to no averaging. In both configurations, the online imitator collects auxiliary experience, while EMA changes the continuation actions used in critic targets. EMA produces task-dependent results (Figure 3). On Hopper and Ant, it increases final mean returns by 210 and 369, respectively, and reduces across-seed variation, although the reduction on Ant is small. 5.5 Learning Dynamics 

To better understand the interaction between inverse distillation and off-policy value learning, we analyze auxiliary-policy imitation error and critic action-gradient alignment (Figure 6). 

Tracking the visual policy. The state-based imitator is trained to follow an evolving visual pol-icy rather than a fixed behavioral target. After initial transients, imitation errors remain nonzero throughout training. Online and EMA imitation errors become similar later in training, with larger early differences on the dexterous manipulation tasks. These measurements show that auxiliary imitation remains imperfect while visual-policy learning progresses. However, imitation error is measured on states visited by the visual policy, and therefore does not establish accurate tracking on auxiliary-rollout states or unbiased temporal-difference targets. 

Action-gradient alignment. We examine whether the shared critic produces consistent local action-improvement directions at actions selected by the visual policy and its state-based imitator. Using paired rendered replay samples, we compare critic action gradients across three seeds per task (Appendix B). Mean cosine similarity remains positive after the initial transient, with nearly identical trends for the online and EMA imitators. At the final common observation time, online/EMA cosine similarities are 0.56/0.55 on Hopper, 0.76/0.75 on Walker, 0.61/0.60 on Ant, 0.73/0.72 on Allegro Hand, and 0.76/0.75 on Shadow Hand. Gradient dot products exhibit greater variation in magnitude and across seeds. 6 Limitations and Future Work 

Our experiments compare complete implementations of Echo and SDPG , without isolating the contribution of auxiliary experience from differences in architectures and training configurations. A matched rendered-only FastTD3 baseline would help quantify this contribution. Next, inverse distillation introduces approximation error because the state-based imitator may not reproduce the visual policy across all states encountered during auxiliary rollouts. While our diagnostics show positive alignment of critic action gradients despite imperfect imitation, they do not establish critic accuracy or accurate tracking on auxiliary-rollout states. Looking ahead, Echo offers several promising research directions. First, by directly optimizing the observation-based policy rather than distilling a privileged teacher, Echo may help reduce the imitation gap arising from information asymmetry in conventional teacher–student learning. Second, the framework could extend to richer sensory modalities, such as high-resolution tactile sensing with DIGIT sensors, where simulating observations and evaluating sensory policies can be computationally expensive. Finally, decoupling experience collection from policy inference may enable scalable direct RL for larger vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action model (WAM) architectures, whose inference costs make conventional large-scale online training increasingly expensive. These directions remain to be investigated experimentally. 11 7 Conclusion 

We presented a preliminary study of Echo , a framework for scaling direct visual reinforcement learning through large-scale off-policy experience. At its core, inverse distillation trains a state-based auxiliary policy to imitate the evolving visual policy, enabling inexpensive, unrendered experience collection for off-policy value learning. A shared privileged critic leverages this experience to directly improve the visual policy through first-order gradients, decoupling the scale of experience collection from the costs of visual rendering and inference. Our preliminary experiments show average reductions in time to reference returns of 60% across the three locomotion tasks and 56% across both hand tasks, with 62% and 12% higher peak mean returns, respectively, compared with SDPG . Both hands also achieve higher mean returns at matched simulator-transition budgets. These findings provide initial evidence for scaling off-policy experience without proportionally increasing visual computation. We hope that this approach offers a promising alternative to reliably scale direct visual reinforcement learning.

Interpreting Privileged Critic Gradients. To interpret how a privileged critic can guide an observation-based policy, consider the observation-level value function induced by the fitted critic on the rendered replay distribution: 

¯Qϕ1 (o, a ) = Es|o [Qϕ(s, a )] . (9) Holding the replay distribution and critic fixed, and assuming differentiation and conditional expecta-tion can be interchanged, we obtain 

∇a ¯Qϕ(o, a ) = Es|o [∇aQϕ(s, a )] . (10) Thus, averaging critic gradients over paired state–observation samples provides an estimate of the gradient of the induced observation-level objective. This explains how privileged state information can guide an observation-based policy without becoming a deployment input. The interpretation does not imply that the fitted critic is accurate or that its gradients are unbiased estimates of the true return gradient. 

Approximation from Auxiliary Continuation Actions. The state-based imitator approximates the evolving visual policy but need not reproduce its actions exactly. Consequently, TD targets constructed using μ ¯ψ (s′) may differ from targets obtained using the visual policy’s continuation actions. In particular, the imitator is supervised on rendered visual-policy experience, whereas critic replay also contains states visited by the auxiliary policy. Low imitation error on nominal states therefore does not guarantee accurate continuation actions throughout replay. In an idealized memoryless setting, a uniform bound on continuation-action error, together with suitable regularity assumptions, can bound the resulting value discrepancy. However, the inverse distillation objective alone does not guarantee such a bound over the full replay distribution.
