# Remember Where You Walk: Learning Spatial Memory for End-to-End Legged Robot Exploration 

Leon Magnus ∗,1, Nico Bohlinger ∗,1, Jan Peters 1,2,3,4, Oleg Arenz 1

Abstract — Humanoids and other legged robots operating in complex environments must remember information that leaves their field of view and only becomes relevant much later. Rein-forcement learning policies address this using memory networks such as LSTMs that are trained end-to-end with the policy to memorize such task-relevant aspects. Yet, such architectures often fail to preserve sufficient information over long horizons. In this work, we propose a network architecture based on Hadamard memory that specifically targets robot navigation. Our Local Spatial Matrix Memory (LSMM) enables RL agents to store generic task-relevant information at specific, position-based memory cells, enabling flexible and spatially grounded memorization. We evaluate LSMM on the task of autonomous maze exploration, where it matches the maze coverage of strong recurrent and finite-history baselines while being the only architecture that remembers the maze layout, reducing the reconstruction error threefold. We provide videos and code for our experiments at: https://leonmgns.github.io/ RememberWhereYouWalkWebiste/ 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Thanks to breakthroughs in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), legged robots can now traverse rough terrain, jump over obstacles, and navigate toward goal positions using policies trained in highly-parallelized simulations or directly in the real world [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. These policies usually follow velocity commands or short-horizon waypoints [6], [7], leaving the higher level planning to a human operator and making the system not fully autonomous. Autonomous exploration of an unknown environment is a fundamentally different problem. Now, the robot must not only reason and control its body in a local frame, but also decide where to go and remember where it has already been, to navigate and explore efficiently [8]. This turns locomotion into a long-horizon partially observable navigation problem in which the current local observation is not enough [9]. Memory-augmented policies address this problem by leveraging an observation-action history with architectures such as Long Short-Term Memorys (LSTMs) [10], Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) [11], Transformers [12], or simply concatenating history for an Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) [13]. However, unstructured recurrent states, through LSTMs or GRUs, easily mix or overwrite spatially distinct infor-mation, as credit-assignment is tricky, and finite-context

Classical navigation systems avoid this problem by explicitly registering and using observations in spatial maps, but typically separate mapping, planning, and locomotion into individual components, losing out on potential end-to-end efficiency [15], [16], [17]. In this work, we introduce LSMM and evaluate it for end-to-end maze exploration. The layout of our matrix memory is aligned with the environment’s frame and indexed using a position estimate. At every step, the policy modifies only a small window in the memory around the robot’s position through learned calibration and update matrices, while the rest of the memory is not updated. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) [18] encoder transforms the spatial memory into features used by both the policy and an auxiliary maze-reconstruction head. This combines the information persistence of index-based updates and the locality of map-based navigation with learned latent representations and end-to-end locomotion control, without any explicit path planning or waypoint commands. We formulate long-horizon, goal-free maze exploration as an end-to-end locomotion problem. We train our method for the Unitree Go2 quadruped in procedurally generated mazes and compare LSMM against unstructured recurrent and arXiv:2610.37203v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 finite-history architectures. Our experiments provide compre-hensive empirical evaluations in simulation and deployment in the real-world, demonstrating LSMM’s capabilities on maze reconstruction, exploration efficiency, robustness to localization noise, and generalization to unseen mazes. II. R ELATED WORK 

Partial observability in DRL is commonly addressed by conditioning the policy on a finite observation history or a recurrent latent state [19]. Recurrent architectures such as LSTMs and GRUs have been widely used for navigation tasks [10], [11], [9], [20], while attention-based policies can leverage longer observation sequences [12], [21]. Le et al. show that LSTMs, GRUs, and many other recurrent architectures update their memory in the same underlying way, calibrating the previous memory element-wise and then writing new information additively [22]. They formalize this update rule as the Hadamard Memory Framework (HMF) and derive Stable Hadamard Memory, whose calibration keeps the memory from decaying or exploding over long horizons. However, all of these memories aggregate obser-vations over time and maintain no explicit link between the stored information and the spatial location where it was acquired. Spatial neural memories address this limitation by aligning the latent state with the geometry of the environment. Neural Map stores features in a two-dimensional memory indexed by the agent’s position for maze navigation [23]. MapNet similarly constructs a self-centered latent map and learns to register new observations within it [24]. Active Neural SLAM combines a learned mapping module with global and local policies, but keeps an explicit analytical planner between the map and the executed actions [25]. Our LSMM keeps the spatial indexing of these methods but does not need the analytical planner of Active Neural SLAM, since its memory features enter the locomotion policy directly. Unlike Neural Map, which writes a single feature vector into the agent’s current cell, it retrieves a whole window of cells around the robot’s estimated position, applies the HMF update rule inside this window, and leaves the rest of the memory unchanged. The memory and the locomotion policy are trained jointly, and an auxiliary head reconstructs the maze layout from the memory features, which encourages it to retain task-relevant information about the maze. Classical legged-navigation stacks put the locomotion pol-icy as the last layer in their pipeline, where it executes veloc-ity or waypoint commands produced by separate localization, mapping, and planning modules [15], [16], [17]. Learning-based approaches tried to merge parts of this pipeline. Rudin et al. train a single policy that reaches a commanded target position within a given time [7], and Zhang et al. learn a navigation policy that steers a separate locomotion policy to such a target [26]. In both cases the target position itself still comes from an external planner or operator. For exploration this planner typically builds an occupancy map and repeatedly picks the next target on the frontier between the mapped and unexplored space of the environment [8]. We remove this remaining component, since the spatial memory takes over the role of the occupancy map and the decision where to move next is learned jointly with the locomotion. III. LEGGED ROBOT EXPLORATION 

We propose the LSMM, a spatially indexed latent memory that allows a single policy to remember its environment, decide where to go, and control the robot’s joints at the same time. First, we formalize the exploration task and the interface of the policy. Then, we introduce the local memory update of LSMM and describe how memory, encoders, and policy are trained jointly with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [27] and an auxiliary maze reconstruction objective.

C. Local Spatial Matrix Memory 

The LSMM is a tensor Mt ∈ [0 , 1] H×W ×C whose first two dimensions are aligned with the environment frame. We use H ×W = 50 ×50 cells with C = 3 channels, so one cell covers 0.2 × 0.2 m of the maze. The channels carry learned features without a predefined meaning, and all entries are initialized to 0.5 at the beginning of an episode. Updating the full memory at every step, as done by the recurrent architectures in the HMF [22], would constantly rewrite information about locations the robot currently can-not observe. E. Rewards 

Each training episode is either a goal episode, in which the robot is commanded toward a neighboring maze cell, or an exploration episode, in which it receives no command and must cover as much of the maze as possible. The reward 

rt consists of task rewards, which depend on this episode type, and shaping penalties for the gait and the navigation. In exploration episodes, the robot collects a reward for being in rarely visited cells, which decays with the time already spent in the current cell, and a reward for the overall maze coverage. In goal episodes, it is instead rewarded for moving toward the commanded cell and reaching it. F. Curricula 

Learning to walk and to explore at the same time is difficult, as the exploration rewards provide no useful signal while the robot cannot walk yet. Therefore, we bootstrap training with goal episodes, in which the command ct

contains the position of a reachable neighboring maze cell in the robot’s base frame. The least visited neighboring cell is selected to already bias the policy toward exploration, and once it is reached, the next goal is sampled immediately. The last entry of ct flags the episode type, and in exploration episodes the command is the constant vector [0 , 0, 1] , so the policy must decide on its own where to move. The fraction of exploration episodes grows with a performance-based curriculum coefficient [30], [31], which increases when the robot covers at least 80 % of the maze in a goal episode or 60 % in an exploration episode, and decreases on low coverage or early termination. The same coefficient ramps up the shaping penalties, so that they do not suppress the learning of exploration early in training. A second coefficient grows the same way and controls the fraction of the maze’s walls that are present, so the policy first learns to walk and follow goals in open spaces before it faces more and more complicated mazes. At the end of training, effectively all episodes are exploration episodes, and the deployed policy never depends on an external goal.

Our experiments evaluate how LSMM explores compared to unstructured memory architectures, what the different memories actually retain about the environment, and whether the learned behavior transfers to the real robot. We compare LSMM against an LSTM, a Spatially-Enhanced Recurrent Unit (SRU) [20], a Transformer, and an MLP that receives a fixed window of stacked past observations. All baselines receive the same observations, including the position estimate, are trained with the same auxiliary losses and the hyperparameters of Table I, and differ from LSMM only in their memory mechanism. To keep the comparison fair, we give every approach the same hardware budget: for each architecture, we choose the largest memory capacity for which training still fits on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 with 24 GB of VRAM. This results in a hidden state of size 1 000 for the LSTM, SRU and a context of the 10 most recent observations for the Transformer and the fixed-window MLP. Larger configurations exceed the available GPU memory. We train three seeds per architecture and evaluate each deterministically in 100 environments on previously unseen mazes.

C. Real World 

We deploy the LSMM policy on the Unitree Go2 in an unseen outdoor maze, as shown in Figure 1. We reduce the size of the maze from 10 × 10 m to 5 × 5 m due to constraints on the available space. We run the policy locally on the onboard CPU and estimate the robot’s pose from an AprilTag mounted on the robot, which is tracked by an external camera placed roughly eight meters above the ground (see Figure 1(a)). Using this pose estimate and the wall positions pre-measured in a fixed maze frame, we sample the height map around the robot at every time step, reproducing the height-scan observation from simulation without onboard terrain perception. The policy does not receive a goal or any an externally generated navigation command and explores the maze fully autonomously. It selects directions at intersections, traverses longer corridors, and continues exploring after returning to previously visited parts of the maze. The snapshot of the matrix memory in Figure 1 shows how the policy modifies the cells along its trajectory while cells outside the visited region retain their initial value. V. L IMITATIONS & F UTURE WORK 

The LSMM memory covers a fixed area that is aligned with the environment frame and sized to the maze, so explor-ing environments of unknown sizes would require to grow or shift the memory with the robot. The update further depends on an external position estimate, and although training with 

±0.20 m of noise makes the policy tolerate imperfect local-ization, we do not yet estimate the position from onboard sensing alone. Finally, our mazes are flat and small enough that reactive strategies reach similar coverage, so we expect the benefit of a spatial memory for pure exploration to grow with the size of the environment. In future work, we want to scale LSMM to larger and rougher environments, estimate the position internally from the robot’s own sensing, and use the reconstructed map for downstream tasks such as navigating back to previously seen locations. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced LSMM, a spatially indexed latent memory that lets a single policy explore unseen mazes end-to-end, from onboard observations to joint-level actions. By apply-ing the HMF update only to a local window centered on the robot’s position estimate, information about a location persists in the memory until the robot returns there. In our experiments, unstructured recurrent and finite-history baselines match LSMM in pure maze coverage, but they explore reactively and forget the layout they have seen. LSMM instead builds a persistent map of the maze while exploring, reduces the reconstruction error by a factor of three, spreads its visits most evenly over the maze, and additionally stores physical properties of the environment. We deployed the policy on a Unitree Go2 and demonstrated goal-free exploration of a real outdoor maze without an explicit map, planner, or waypoint commands.
