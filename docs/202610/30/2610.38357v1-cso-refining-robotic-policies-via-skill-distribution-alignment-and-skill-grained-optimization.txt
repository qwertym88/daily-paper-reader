# Abstract 

Discretizing continuous actions into discrete skills using methods like VQ-VAE has emerged as a powerful paradigm for robotic manipulation. However, quantization errors yield a suboptimal distribution for the skill prior, degrading its performance. While reinforcement learning offers a path for refinement, its direct application faces unstable encoder updates and a granularity dilemma in importance sampling. To address these challenges, we introduce Cascaded Skills Optimization (CSO), a two-stage post-training framework. First, Rejection-Sampling Supervised Fine-tuning aligns the model’s observation-to-skill mapping with the distribution of successful online trajectories via supervised fine-tuning. Second, with the observa-tion encoder frozen, Skills Policy Optimization computes an independent, clipped importance ratio for each complete skill, enabling stable and efficient updates. With a lightweight QueST-style policy, CSO raises overall LIBERO success from 80.1% to 97.5%, improves performance on RoboTwin2.0, and achieves 61.0% success across five physical single-arm tasks. Additional comparisons with FAST, discrete BEAST, and fixed-buffer IQL further validate the effectiveness of our two-stage post-training strategy. 

# 1 Introduction 

Modeling complex manipulation behaviors has long been a central problem in robotics [18, 9]. A promising direction is to learn structured action representations through Transformer-based archi-tectures [3, 29]. Temporal action chunks provide useful abstractions for control [43]; skill-based approaches additionally map continuous trajectories into discrete latent representations [17]. A quantized autoencoder, such as a VQ-VAE [38, 13], learns a vocabulary of fundamental tokens. These tokens are then used to form "skills", which are reusable motion primitives represented as token sequences [28, 19]. This hierarchical framework simplifies the problem, enabling an autoregressive policy to tackle complex tasks by sequentially composing tokens to generate a skill [28]. However, while this discretization paradigm is powerful, policies trained purely via imitation learning (IL) with these discrete skills exhibit a notable limitation. The VQ-VAE’s discretization process introduces inherent quantization errors, meaning the skills are only approximations of the expert’s continuous actions [17]. These reconstruction errors compound with policy errors during online execution. On one hand, the inherent imprecision of the tokens that constitute a skill can cause the decoded action to fail the task. On the other hand, these quantization errors can accumulate over an episode, progressively pushing the agent into states outside the expert data distribution. This resulting distribution shift severely hinders the generalization of the IL-trained policy [15].

To overcome the limitations of the initial policy, a natural remedy is to refine it with online rein-forcement learning (RL) [11, 22, 37]. However, directly applying RL to this setting presents several challenges. A primary issue is the unstable update of the observation encoder. Online data collection often yields an imbalanced mix of task outcomes (e.g., many successes on easy tasks, many failures on hard ones). This provides inconsistent gradient signals to the crucial observation encoder, leading to inefficient training and often requiring complex hybrid frameworks to stabilize [11, 6]. A second structural challenge arises from a mismatch between the unit of decision-making (a skill) and the unit of optimization (a token). Token-level methods apply a separate importance ratio to each generated token [34, 24], which treats the components of a jointly executed skill separately. Episode-level methods such as GSPO instead use one length-normalized ratio and a shared clipping decision for the complete sequence [44]. Robotic episodes exhibit length asymmetry : time-outs are often longer than successes. This motivates skill-level optimization, which assigns each complete skill its own ratio and clipping decision while sharing the terminal advantage. We compare token-, skill-, and episode-level optimization from the same R-SFT checkpoint. To address these key challenges, we propose Cascaded Skills Optimization (CSO ), a two-stage post-training framework. Our key insight is that effective refinement requires two steps: first, stabilizing the model on successful online data, and second, aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of decision-making. Specifically: 1) To address distributional shift and encoder instability, we introduce Rejection-Sampling Supervised Fine-tuning (R-SFT ) for skill-based policy refinement. This stage aligns the model’s observation-to-skill mapping with the distribution of successful online trajectories via supervised fine-tuning, inspired by similar techniques in LLMs [40]. The encoder is then stabilized and frozen, providing a robust foundation for the subsequent RL stage and avoiding the inconsistent gradients of direct online fine-tuning. 2) To solve the granularity mismatch, we introduce the Skills Policy Optimization (SPO ) algorithm. By computing and clipping an importance ratio for each complete skill, SPO aligns optimization with execution and enables stable, data-efficient policy improvement. We validate our framework through extensive experiments on challenging manipulation benchmarks. In its lightweight instantiation, CSO demonstrates substantial improvements over its underlying imitation-learned policy, boosting the average success rate by 17.4 absolute percentage points across all four LIBERO suites [21]. We further validate the method on RoboTwin2.0 [5] and confirm its real-world applicability by successfully deploying the learned policy on a physical robot. To summarize, our main contributions are: 1. To circumvent unstable encoder updates in direct reinforcement learning, we introduce 

R-SFT . This rejection-sampling fine-tuning stage first stabilizes the observation encoder on successful online trajectories to establish a robust foundation for policy optimization. 2. To resolve the granularity dilemma between internal tokens and complete trajectories, we propose SPO , a novel algorithm that performs stable and fine-grained policy optimization via skill-level importance sampling. 3. We design and validate CSO , a complete two-stage framework that systematically refines skill-based policies by decoupling representation stabilization from policy optimization, demonstrating substantial performance improvements on challenging benchmarks and real-world robotic tasks. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Action Representations in Imitation Learning 

Action chunking, as in ACT [43], represents temporally extended control, while VQ-BeT [17], QueST [28], and STAR [19] learn discrete action representations using quantized autoencoders [38, 13]. Generalist policies such as RT-2 [4], Octo [29], and OpenVLA [14] further connect perception and language to control. F1 [25] uses generated visual foresight to guide action prediction. Alternative representations include FAST’s frequency-based compression [31], BEAST’s B-splines [45], and Hermite trajectory priors for smoothness and inter-chunk continuity [26]. Reconstruction quality and closed-loop execution provide complementary criteria for evaluating action representations. Our matched comparison shows that R-SFT and full CSO improve policy success with FAST and discrete 2BEAST (Section 5.6). We evaluate FAST and discrete BEAST through a shared autoregressive policy interface. 

2.2 Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models 

Work in LLMs shows that RL after pre-training and SFT can unlock stronger reasoning and interactive adaptation [30, 10, 35, 20, 23]. Beyond PPO [34] and DPO [32], value-free methods such as Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning, GRPO, and GSPO have become increasingly influential [40, 35, 44]. Our setting differs because sequence-level optimization creates a granularity mismatch in robotics, which SPO resolves with skill-level updates. 

2.3 Reinforcement Learning for Robotic Policies 

Recent work increasingly applies RL to refine pretrained robotic policies [11, 22]. Prior approaches adapt PPO-like algorithms [24], dense rewards [41], learned critics [36], or preference-style op-timization [42, 7], often with substantial auxiliary machinery [11, 6]. Concurrent work such as RIPT-VLA [37] also studies online RL for VLAs. Offline RL methods such as IQL [16] improve a policy using a fixed dataset. CSO instead alternates online collection and skill-level updates after supervised alignment. Our IQL comparison matches the initial checkpoint, skill interface, and collection budget, and compares fixed-data with adaptive-online refinement strategies (Section 5.6). 

# 3 Preliminaries 

We develop a post-training method that refines imitation-learned policies through online interaction. We first define this base policy architecture and then formulate the online policy optimization problem. 

3.1 Skill-Based Policies via Action Discretization 

We consider a base policy trained on an expert dataset D, containing trajectories of observation-action pairs {(Ot, a t)}Tt=1 and a language instruction L. An observation Ot includes multi-view RGB images and proprioceptive states, while an action at is a continuous vector. Following prior skill-based policies such as QueST [28], the policy is trained in two stages. 

1. Skill Codebook Learning. A quantized autoencoder learns a discrete vocabulary of skills. An encoder ϕθ maps a continuous action chunk at:t+k−1 to a latent representation, which is then discretized by a quantizer (e.g., FSQ [27]) into a sequence of skill tokens Z = ( z1, . . . , z n). A decoder ψθ reconstructs the action chunk ˆat:t+k−1 from Z. The autoencoder is trained by minimizing the reconstruction loss: 

Lrecon (θ) = ∥ψθ (Quantize (ϕθ (at:t+k−1))) − at:t+k−1∥1. (1) 

2. Skill Prior Learning. A multimodal autoregressive model, the skill prior πφ, is trained via imitation learning (IL) to predict the expert’s skill token sequence Zexpert . In our framework, this module can be instantiated either as a lightweight Transformer or as a larger VLM backbone such as Qwen3-VL-2B. It is conditioned on the observation history Ot−h:t and the task embedding e. The training objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood: 

LIL (φ) = − log πφ(Zexpert |O t−h:t, e ). (2) During inference, the policy πφ generates a skill ˆZ, which the frozen decoder ψθ translates into executable continuous actions.

# 4 Method 

To overcome the challenges of refining imitation-learned, skill-based policies, we propose Cascaded Skills Optimization (CSO ), a two-stage post-training framework. Our approach is designed to systematically address the core issues of encoder instability and the granularity dilemma in policy optimization. First, Rejection-Sampling Supervised Fine-tuning (R-SFT ) stabilizes the model’s representation by aligning it with a distribution of successful online behaviors. Second, with a robust foundation in place, Skills Policy Optimization (SPO ) resolves the granularity dilemma by performing policy optimization on the natural decision-making unit, i.e., the skill. 

4.1 Stage 1: Stabilizing Representations with Rejection-Sampling Supervised Fine-tuning (R-SFT) 

The initial policy, parameterized by φIL , suffers from quantization errors and distribution shift. With sparse binary rewards, groups that are uniformly successful or unsuccessful have zero relative advantage. Other groups provide variable learning signals; jointly adapting perception and the skill prior can destabilize online refinement. The objective of R-SFT is to circumvent this problem by first refining the policy parameters from φIL to a more robust set φR-SFT using a stable, supervised signal. 

Curated Data Collection for Robust Alignment. To build a diverse success dataset Dsucc and mitigate overfitting, we employ a curated sampling protocol. At the action level, we execute a stochastic policy (via high-temperature sampling) to encourage exploration. At the state level, we apply an "N-strikes" rule: an initial state is discarded if all N attempts from it fail, and a new one is sampled. This strategy promotes a broad search for successful trajectories across initial states. The stored labels are the skills actually executed in complete successful trajectories. The action tokenizer and decoder remain fixed throughout R-SFT and SPO. 

Supervised Alignment and Encoder Stabilization. With the curated dataset Dsucc of successful state-skill pairs {(st, Z t)}, we refine the policy parameters by minimizing the imitation learning objective on this new data distribution:

4.2 Stage 2: Resolving the Granularity Dilemma with Skills Policy Optimization (SPO) 

With a stable representation, we introduce Skills Policy Optimization (SPO ) to effectively optimize the unfrozen skill prior . SPO is designed to resolve the granularity dilemma by choosing the skill as the optimization unit between internal tokens and complete trajectories. 

Dynamic rollout sampling. For online data collection, groups of trajectories from trivially solved or consistently failed initial states produce all-zero advantages. To avoid zero-advantage groups, we employ a dynamic rejection strategy (Algorithm 1): any group of trajectories where all rollouts receive the same reward is discarded, and a new initial state is sampled. This focuses optimization on tasks at the frontier of the policy’s capability. 

The Granularity Spectrum. Let us consider a trajectory τ where the policy generates a sequence of skills (Z0, Z 1, . . . , Z H ). The policy likelihood on its observed states factors as QHt=0 πφ(Zt|st).This conditional policy likelihood is evaluated on states sampled through environment rollouts. • Token-level optimization would perform updates based on ratios ρ(zjt ), clipping internal token decisions separately. • Trajectory-level optimization uses a single ratio ρ(τ ), sharing a clipping decision across an episode whose number of decisions varies between short successes and long time-outs.

5.2 Overall Performance 

As shown in Table 1, the QueST-style instantiation of CSO achieves 97.5% overall success on LIBERO, a 17.4-point improvement over its QueST initial policy, with gains in all four suites. On LIBERO-Long, success increases from 69.1% to 96.0%, a gain of 26.9 absolute percentage points. On LIBERO-Long, CSO substantially outperforms prior methods, indicating improved robustness to compounding errors in long-horizon tasks. We also observe strong multi-task results on LIBERO-90 (97.7%) for the QueST-style instantiation. Additional MetaWorld ML45 results are reported in Appendix D.3.

5.5 Analysis of Granularity in Policy Optimization 

We next analyze the central design choice in SPO: skill-level optimization granularity. We compare it against trajectory-level updates (GSPO) and token-level updates (GRPO), with all methods initialized from the same R-SFT checkpoint. Table 4 compares the best evaluated clipping ranges on LIBERO-Long. SPO reaches 96.0%, compared with 94.5% for token-level GRPO, 93.2% for episode-level GSPO, and 94.3% for critic-based PPO. Figure 2 shows the separate LIBERO-90 learning comparison. We report the clipping-range sweep in Appendix D.8. 

The Mechanics of Granularity: Length Asymmetry. Successful trajectories terminate upon task completion, whereas failures often continue until time-out. Episode-level GSPO shares one clipping decision across this variable-length sequence. SPO instead computes and clips a ratio for each fixed-length skill of eight tokens. This gives each environment-level decision its own importance weight and clipping decision, aligning policy optimization with skill execution.

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced CSO, a two-stage framework that refines skill-based robotic policies by decoupling representation stabilization from policy optimization. CSO effectively addresses encoder instability and the granularity dilemma. Across LIBERO, RoboTwin2.0, and physical tasks, it consistently outperforms strong baselines, demonstrating that this decomposition is practically effective and well-suited for skill-based control. Additional comparisons with stronger action representations and fixed-buffer IQL further validate the framework. 

# 7 Limitations and Future Work 

CSO requires initial success support for each task to bootstrap R-SFT. It remains effective with as few as 50 successful online trajectories per task (Appendix D.6); offline RL warm-starting or human correction could extend it to harder exploration settings. The frozen tokenizer’s precision and motion coverage, along with the choice of temporal parameters, bound refinement. Physical validation covers five single-arm tasks; broader manipulation settings, automated resets, and sim-to-real transfer offer directions for extending deployment and reducing online collection cost (Appendices K and L).

# B Benchmark Environments 

We evaluate CSO across multiple simulation benchmarks to test its effectiveness in diverse manipula-tion scenarios. 

B.1 LIBERO Benchmark 

LIBERO is a comprehensive benchmark for language-conditioned manipulation tasks that evaluates different aspects of robotic manipulation capabilities through five distinct task suites: • LIBERO-Spatial : Contains 10 tasks focusing on identical objects in different spatial layouts, testing the understanding of spatial relationships. • LIBERO-Object : Comprises 10 tasks with consistent layouts but varying objects, evaluating the ability to generalize across different object types. • LIBERO-Goal : Features 10 tasks sharing the same object categories and spatial layouts but with different goals, assessing the capability to learn diverse task-oriented behaviors. • LIBERO-Long : Presents 10 challenging long-horizon tasks involving diverse object cate-gories and layouts, testing temporal reasoning and sequential manipulation skills. • LIBERO-90 : Encompasses 90 tasks with extremely diverse object categories, layouts, and task goals, providing a comprehensive evaluation platform. For our experiments, we utilize 50 expert demonstrations per task from the official dataset. Each demonstration includes multi-view RGB images (224x224 resolution), proprioceptive states (7-dimensional joint positions and 1-dimensional gripper state), and corresponding continuous action commands. 

B.2 MetaWorld ML45 

MetaWorld ML45 [39] is a challenging multi-task learning benchmark designed to evaluate the ability of a single policy to master various manipulation skills simultaneously. The ML45 split comprises 45 training tasks (ML45-train) and 5 unseen test tasks (ML45-test). Our experiments focus on ML45-train, which requires a single multi-task policy to learn to solve all 45 distinct manipulation tasks. These tasks are performed by a Sawyer robotic arm in simulation and cover a wide range of skill types, such as: • Simple Movements : reach-v2 , push-v2 

• Pick and Place : pick-place-v2 

• Interaction with Articulated Objects : door-open-v2 , drawer-close-v2 ,

window-open-v2 

• Tool Usage : hammer-v2 , stick-push-v2 

• Fine Manipulation : button-press-topdown-v2 , peg-unplug-side-v2 

15 Each task features randomized target and object initial positions, demanding strong generalization capabilities from the policy. The core challenge of ML45 lies in the policy’s ability to infer the correct behavior from task instructions (e.g., "close the drawer") and solve all 45 tasks within a shared model parameter space, avoiding interference between tasks. For training, we use scripted expert policies provided in the official MetaWorld codebase to collect 100 expert demonstrations per task. 

B.3 RoboTwin2.0 Benchmark 

RoboTwin2.0 [5] is a simulator benchmark focused on visually grounded manipulation and embodiment-sensitive interaction tasks. We use it as a complementary testbed to evaluate whether policy improvements transfer beyond standard offline imitation-learning benchmarks. Following the common protocol, we report task success under both a clean evaluation setting and a more challenging randomized setting with perturbed initial configurations. 

# C Real-World Setup 

To validate the effectiveness of our approach in real-world scenarios, we used the Cobot Agilex ALOHA robot , a dual-arm manipulator platform. In our experiments, we exclusively used its single arm for manipulation, to maintain consistency with the single-arm setup in simulation environments. We designed five distinct manipulation tasks, described below. For each task, we collected 50 demonstrations through human teleoperation. • Cube on Cube : Stack the yellow cube on top of the red cube. • Cube in Bowl : Place the red cube into the bowl. • Press Button : Press the red button on the table. • Cube on Bottle : Balance the red cube on top of a medicine bottle. • Sequential Placement : A long-horizon task requiring the policy to first place the yellow cube into the bowl, and then move the bowl onto the plate.

E.2 CSO Framework Training 

After offline imitation learning, CSO post-training uses two stages. The action tokenizer and decoder are fixed throughout both stages. 

Stage 1: Rejection-Sampling SFT (R-SFT). 

• Data Collection: For the default simulation setting, we aim to collect 200 successful state-skill trajectories per task (20 per task in the physical setting), which are stored in a replay buffer Dsucc . We employ an "N-strikes" sampling rule with Nstrikes = 10 , meaning we attempt up to 10 rollouts from an initial state. If any are successful, all successful trajectories are added to the buffer. • Fine-tuning: Once the buffer is filled, the observation-to-skill policy (including observation encoders, excluding the action tokenizer and decoder) is fine-tuned on Dsucc for 20 epochs. We use the AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate that decays from 

1e-4 to 1e-5 using a cosine schedule. 

Stage 2: Skills Policy Optimization (SPO). 

• Encoder Freezing: Upon completion of the R-SFT stage, all parameters of the visual and proprioceptive encoders are frozen .• Online Optimization: We perform a total of 30 iterations. In each iteration, we collect a batch of data using the old policy πφold . In the default simulation setting, each batch contains 150 retained groups of eight trajectories from a shared initial state per group. Thirty iterations retain 36,000 trajectories; the approximately 41,200 attempted rollouts also include rejected zero-advantage groups. Physical collection uses the smaller budget in Appendix L. • SPO Update: The unfrozen skill prior is updated using our SPO objective. In this objective, the importance sampling ratio is clipped to the range [0 .93 , 1.15] . We use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-6.

# G Collection Protocol and Success Support 

R-SFT stores complete successful trajectories, while every attempted rollout counts toward interaction cost. Under the Nstrikes = 10 protocol, the stochastic policy is executed repeatedly from one initial state before a new state is sampled. The default simulation buffer target is 200 successes per task; LIBERO-Long therefore retains 2,000 successes from approximately 5,380 attempts, an observed retention fraction of about 37.2%. The 69.1% initial-policy evaluation score and the 37.2% stochastic collection retention rate use their respective evaluation and sampling protocols. The success buffer favors configurations solvable by the stochastic collection policy. Successful collection therefore relies on initial policy support for each task. The physical buffer contains 20 successes per task, collected directly on the robot. Subsequent SPO also uses physical rollouts. The cost measurements cover direct collection on the physical robot. The representation comparison holds the observation backbone and autoregressive policy interface fixed, while the IQL comparison contrasts fixed-buffer learning with adaptive online refinement from the same R-SFT checkpoint. Stage-level cost accounting covers the measured post-training collection and optimization runs.
