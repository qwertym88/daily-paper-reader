# Humanoid Dodgeball: Learning Fast Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance Motions for Humanoids 

Aoqian Zhang 1,* , Chunzheng Wang 1,* , Haoran Chen 1,* , Annan Tang 2,Cheng Xiang 3, Fan Shi 1, and Shuzhi Sam Ge 1

Abstract — Humanoids have become increasingly agile and ca-pable in real-world environments. To operate safely in dynamic scenarios, they must rapidly evade fast-moving obstacles while maintaining stability and physically feasible whole-body motion. Learning such behaviors remains challenging due to the high-dimensional dynamics of humanoid systems and the need to jointly balance reactive obstacle avoidance, motion naturalness, and control robustness. In this work, we present Humanoid Dodgeball , a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for agile dynamic obstacle avoidance in humanoids. Our method enables highly dynamic whole-body evasive maneuvers against incom-ing projectiles through threat-aware reward shaping, while multiple direction-specific adversarial motion priors regularize the learned behaviors toward stable and diverse human-like mo-tions. Extensive simulation and hardware experiments demon-strate that the proposed framework substantially improves dynamic obstacle avoidance performance across diverse obsta-cle speeds and launch distances, while preserving robust and physically plausible humanoid motions. Supplementary videos are available at https://humanoid-dodge.github.io/ .

I. INTRODUCTION Recent advances in reinforcement learning for humanoids have significantly improved humanoid whole-body motion tracking and control through training on large human mo-tion datasets [1]–[3]. These developments have enabled hu-manoids to acquire a wide range of dynamic and human-like skills, including athletic sports behaviors such as soccer [4] and tennis [5], highly dynamic parkour locomotion [6], and complex whole-body tasks such as skateboarding [7]. To further improve the safety and robustness of humanoid deployment in complex and human-centered environments, recent research has explored protective falling strategies that reduce damage to both the robot and its surroundings during unavoidable failures [8], as well as collision-free navigation and traversal in cluttered environments with static obstacles [9]. These advances represent important steps toward de-ploying humanoids in real-world environments where safety, robustness, and physical interaction are critical. Despite these remarkable advances, humanoids still lack the ability to safely and robustly react to fast-moving dy-namic objects. The capability to evade incoming obstacles is a fundamental and natural behavior observed in legged ani-mals, including humans, and is essential for agile operation in

RELATED WORK Dynamic obstacle avoidance has been widely studied across different robotic platforms. In aerial robotics, Falanga arXiv:2610.40003v1 [cs.RO] 29 Oct 2026 et al. proposed an event-camera-based framework for quadro-tor obstacle avoidance that leverages the microsecond-level response of event cameras to generate low-latency reactive avoidance behaviors using artificial potential fields [10]. More recently, Fan et al. introduced an end-to-end rein-forcement learning framework that uses multi-frame Li-DAR observations to directly generate control commands for avoiding high-speed dynamic obstacles [11]. In quadrupedal robots, He et al. proposed Agile But Safe (ABS), a dual-policy reinforcement learning framework that combines agile locomotion and recovery policies through a learned reach-avoid value network for high-speed collision-free navigation [12]. Wang et al. further proposed Omni-Perception, an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that directly processes raw LiDAR point clouds using a hierarchical per-ception network for omnidirectional obstacle avoidance [13]. To address the slow response of navigation-based avoidance strategies, Xu et al. proposed the Reflexive Evasion Robot (REBot) framework, which introduces a biologically inspired finite-state-machine architecture for reflexive evasion behav-iors [14]. REBot enables rapid obstacle avoidance through shorter reaction times and faster whole-body quadruped maneuvers. Dynamic obstacle avoidance is significantly more chal-lenging for humanoids than for aerial and quadruped robots due to humanoids’ high-dimensional whole-body dynam-ics, ground contact limitations, and strict balance con-straints. When faced with fast-moving incoming obsta-cles, humanoids cannot simply evade threats through low-dimensional velocity modulation or direct body translation. Instead, they must coordinate each body part and foot placement in a fully integrated manner to perform agile evasive maneuvers while maintaining locomotion stability and physically plausible motion. Concurrently with our work, Yang et al. proposed a perception-aware reinforcement learn-ing framework for humanoid dynamic obstacle avoidance that combines control barrier function (CBF)-inspired reward shaping with adversarial motion priors (AMP)-based motion regularization [15]. However, it considers a limited set of obstacle trajectories, which limits the policy’s ability to generalize to more diverse projectile conditions. Compared with existing approaches, our method enables more effective dynamic obstacle avoidance for humanoids by achieving higher avoidance success rates and robust evasive behaviors against obstacles approaching from diverse directions and speeds. C. Adversarial motion priors 

We use adversarial motion priors (AMP) [16] to regularize the learned dodging behaviors toward dynamically feasible and natural human-like motions. We use 90 human evasive motion clips from the dataset in [17], spanning four dodge directions: left, right, backward, and forward. For each direc-tion, multiple motion styles are included to capture diverse realizations of the evasive behavior, such as dodging with an upright torso or combining the dodge with downward motion of the head and torso. All motion clips are retargeted to the robot’s morphology using the method in [18]. 

Discriminator training. We employ four direction-specific AMP discriminators, each associated with one car-dinal dodge direction: left, right, forward, or backward. Each discriminator is trained using joint-state transitions from the reference motion dataset corresponding to its as-sociated dodge direction and transitions generated by the policy. D. Training details 

Training environment setup. We train the policies in the mjlab simulator [19] using 4096 parallel environments and evaluate our framework on two humanoid platforms, Unitree G1 and EngineAI PM01. To expose the policies to diverse dynamic obstacles, an obstacle is initialized at a randomly sampled position relative to the robot. Its initial velocity is generated using one of two schemes: 1) the obstacle is directed toward a randomly selected body part with a randomly sampled speed; or 2) the obstacle is aimed toward the robot root in the horizontal plane while an additional vertical velocity component is applied, producing a parabolic projectile trajectory. We further randomize the obstacle radius and the gravitational acceleration acting on the obstacle to increase the diversity of projectile dynamics. The detailed domain randomization settings are summarized in Table II. 

Policy network. The policy actor and critic are param-eterized by multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with hidden-layer sizes [512 , 256 , 128] . Both networks receive the current observation ot together with a 20-step observation history. The history is processed by a temporal encoder consisting of a per-frame linear embedding, followed by a two-layer 1-D temporal convolutional network and a final linear pro-jection that produces a 128-dimensional latent representation. Notably, the obstacle velocity is not provided to the actor; instead, projectile motion is inferred implicitly from the his-tory of obstacle positions. V. H ARDWARE DEPLOYMENT 

We deploy the trained policy on the EngineAI PM01 humanoid to evaluate its performance under real-world con-ditions. A motion capture system tracks the robot and the incoming obstacle. The obstacle position is transformed into the robot’s coordinate frame and provided to the policy. We evaluate obstacle avoidance by throwing a ball with a radius of 0.12 m toward the robot from different directions and at different speeds. The ball speed recorded by the motion capture system ranges from 3.0 m/s to 10 m/s. As shown in Fig. 7, the robot responds with diverse whole-body evasive maneuvers, including forward and lateral steps combined with ducking. Out of the 30 trials, the robot successfully dodges 22 times, achieving an overall success rate of approximately 73% . Out of the 8 failure cases, the robot gets hit by the ball 6 times and falls to the ground while dodging twice. Most hit failures occur when fast projectiles (typically > 8 m /s) are directed toward the torso; although the threat successfully triggers an evasive response, the remaining reaction time is sometimes insufficient to avoid contact. Another failure mode occurs when a fast-rolling ball approaches the feet: although the robot steps laterally to evade it, the swinging foot inadvertently contacts the ball. These results demonstrate that the learned policy transfers to hardware and generates coordinated evasive behaviors in response to incoming projectiles, with a modest performance gap consistent with expected sim-to-real differences. VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented Humanoid Dodgeball , a reinforcement learning framework for reactive whole-body avoidance of fast-moving projectiles. By combining threat-aware reward shaping, direction-specific adversarial motion priors, and capture-point guidance, the framework learns diverse eva-sive maneuvers while promoting balance and post-evasion recovery. Simulation experiments on Unitree G1 and En-gineAI PM01 demonstrate higher avoidance success rates than the evaluated baselines across all tested time-to-hit ranges. Ablation studies further support the contributions of the reward design and direction-specific motion priors to avoidance performance and stability. Hardware experiments on PM01 demonstrate coordinated avoidance behaviors. Fig. 7. Hardware demonstrations on the EngineAI PM01 humanoid under diverse projectile conditions. (a) A ball aimed toward the robot’s center is avoided by stepping forward. (b) A projectile directed toward the head is avoided through a coordinated forward step and ducking motion. (c) A ball aimed at the right shoulder is avoided by stepping laterally to the left. (d) A projectile targeting the head is avoided through a rightward step combined with a ducking maneuver. These examples demonstrate that the learned policy produces diverse whole-body evasive strategies in response to different obstacle trajectories and incoming directions. 

Despite these results, avoidance remains challenging under short reaction times, and improved success comes at the cost of higher motor power and longer recovery times than PAC-MAN. Future work will explore policies that better balance dodge success rate and power consumption, replace motion capture with onboard perception, and reduce the sim-to-real gap through improved actuator modeling.
