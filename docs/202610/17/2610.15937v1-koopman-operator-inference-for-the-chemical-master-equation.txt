Koopman Operator Inference for the Chemical Master Equation 

Aditya Dendukuri ∗ and Linda R. Petzold †

Abstract. We infer kinetic rates of stochastic reaction networks from molecular-state pairs separated by a fixed time lag, without constructing the large state space of the Chemical Master Equation. Extended dy-namic mode decomposition estimates the finite-time evolution of selected functions of the molecular state. A matrix logarithm then gives a projected generator whose species rows determine the rates through the known reaction stoichiometry. A finite set of functions introduces a systematic bias. We construct additional functions from the reaction channels and prove that each level of closure moves the first bias term to higher order. Spectral formulas identify the omitted modes responsible for this bias and show when the logarithm amplifies sampling error. Exact calculations and experiments on five reaction networks confirm the predicted error behavior and show when the added functions improve finite-sample rate recovery. 

Key words. Koopman operator, extended dynamic mode decomposition, chemical master equation, stochastic reaction network, matrix logarithm, reaction rate inference

1. Introduction. The Chemical Master Equation (CME) governs the probability distri-bution of molecular copy numbers in a well-mixed stochastic reaction network [8, 18]. On a finite state space, the probability vector p(t) satisfies 

ddt p(t) = A(c)p(t),

where the reaction stoichiometry, propensities, and rate vector c determine the generator 

A(c) [9]. Our inverse problem is to recover c from pairs of molecular states observed at a fixed time separation. The molecular state space grows rapidly with the number of species and their copy num-bers. A likelihood based on the full CME requires a transition model whose dimension is the number of retained states. Finite-state projection reduces this cost by truncation [19]. Moment methods replace the distribution by a finite set of moments, but generally require a closure approximation [6, 23]. We instead pose the inverse problem on selected functions of the molecular state. The number of these observables, which we also call the dictionary, need not grow with the number of molecular states. Let X(t) denote the vector of molecular copy numbers at time t, and let x denote a possible initial state. An observable is a function f of these copy numbers. Its conditional expected value after time t is (Ktf )( x) = E[f (X(t)) | X(0) = x].

7. Discussion and Conclusions. The method recovers reaction rates from paired molecu-lar states without constructing the full CME transition matrix. Mass-action structure places the rates in selected rows of the Koopman generator. EDMD and a matrix logarithm estimate these rows from finite-time data. Generator paths that leave the retained observable space and return to the species rows describe the finite-time projection error. Closure through depth r represents these paths through the order stated in Theorem 5.4. The first remaining coefficient is −Br+1 /(r + 1)!, which gives order hr+1 at odd depth and at least order hr+2 at even depth. The block-resolvent formula describes the same error spectrally. Each omitted mode contributes a pole μℓ and a coupling residue Cℓ. In the dimerization calculation, the residues, rather than the decay rates alone, rank the modes by their effect on the inferred rates. Closure suppresses their shortest paths into the extracted species rows, while the resolvents in (5.8) quantify the remaining amplification near the logarithm branch cut. The finite dimerization calculation verifies these orders without state-space truncation or Monte Carlo error. It also shows why the observation interval and closure depth must be selected together. After optimizing the interval, depth-two closure first improves on the ordinary logarithm at about 1 .00e6 pairs. At 1 .00e10 pairs, its predicted mean squared error is 26 .0% as large. The Brusselator sweep reaches the complementary spectral boundary. The branch margin collapses at h = 0 .8, where both the central fit and the independent reference lose a real principal logarithm. At shorter intervals, we use the same calculation to separate sampling-dominated and projection-dominated rate error. Independent evaluation datasets test the added observables when they must be estimated from trajectories. Median errors decrease in all four networks, with clear paired reductions for Michaelis–Menten, the Brusselator, and MAPK. For Lotka–Volterra, the median gap is smaller than the variation among datasets, and the paired test is unresolved. This result agrees with the finite-sample error budget. Reducing projection bias gives only a modest gain when sampling variation has comparable size. The observable formulation exchanges statistical efficiency for a calculation whose dimen-sion equals the number of selected functions. In the 13-state model, the ordinary estimator has a standard deviation 6 .05 times the Cram´ er–Rao limit attained asymptotically by the correctly specified full likelihood. In the MAPK calculation, a 15 × 15 observable matrix replaces a state-space matrix with about 1 .47e8 rows and columns. These examples show the intended trade-off. The method avoids enumeration of the molecular state space while retaining a quantitative account of its bias and sampling error. 

Future work. Extending the finite-state remainder bound to unbounded networks will re-quire generator-domain conditions and moment estimates. Finite-data applications would also benefit from automatic selection or regularization of the generated candidate functions in place of the present exploratory selection. Such extensions should account explicitly for the covariance of overlapping intervals. Long observation intervals require a separate treatment of logarithm branches and admissibility, closely related to the classical embedding problem [15, 14, 4, 1].
