Abstract 

We study Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method with its standard constant parameters for smooth strongly convex optimization. We characterize the exact worst-case asymptotic factor for the squared distance to the minimizer. To this end, we construct candidate worst-case trajectories by combining planar spirals with a common contraction factor that satisfy a self-similarity condition. Each component rotates by a fixed angle at every iteration. The exact factor is the optimal value of a linear optimization problem over probability measures on a fixed disk, with constraints derived from smooth strongly convex interpolation. Every optimal measure has a common contraction factor, which is also the largest factor for which the angular interpolation conditions are feasible. We also show that this worst-case asymptotic rate can be realized by a single function in a finite-dimensional space whose iterates attain this factor. A matching upper bound holds uniformly over all functions in the class and all finite dimensions. Consequently, allowing the function, dimension, and initial point to vary with the iteration horizon does not increase the worst-case asymptotic factor. We also show that the exact factor is 

1 − 4/(3 √κ) + o(κ−1/2) as κ → ∞ , where κ = L/μ is the ratio of the gradient Lipschitz constant to the strong convexity parameter. 

# 1 Introduction 

We study Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method (NAG) with constant parameters for smooth strongly convex optimization. Our goal is to determine its exact worst-case asymptotic convergence rate, measured by the squared distance to the minimizer. Let κ = L/μ denote the condition number. The classical convergence guarantee gives an asymptotic squared-distance factor of at most 1 − 1/√κ (Nesterov, 2018, Theorem 2.2.1, specialized to scheme (2.2.22); Lessard et al., 2016, Proposition 12). The exact worst-case factor among quadratic objectives is (1 − 1/√κ)2 (Lessard et al., 2016, Proposition 1 and Appendix A). These bounds leave open how slowly the method can converge on a nonquadratic objective. A function that nearly attains the worst-case error after t iterations need not remain worst-case as the method continues. We therefore distinguish between keeping the function and initial point

1.1 Contribution 

Our main contributions are: 

An optimization characterization of the exact rate. We characterize the exact worst-case asymptotic factor by a linear optimization problem over probability measures on a disk whose radius depends only on the condition number. Every optimal measure is supported on one circle, so its components have a common contraction factor. The value is also the largest factor for which the angular interpolation conditions are feasible; these conditions ensure consistency of the prescribed points, gradients, and function values with one function in the class. 

Matching lower and upper bounds. We construct one function and initial point attaining the optimal asymptotic factor. A matching upper bound holds uniformly over all functions in the class, all finite dimensions, and all nonoptimal initial points. Thus, allowing the function, dimension, and initial point to vary with the iteration horizon does not increase the worst-case asymptotic factor. 

Exact and asymptotic descriptions of the rate. We exhibit a condition number for which the exact asymptotic factor cannot be expressed by radicals. For large condition numbers, we obtain the explicit expansion 1 − 4/(3 √κ) + o(κ−1/2) as κ → ∞ , proving that the leading constant 4/3 in Gannot’s upper bound is sharp. 

Explicit worst-case examples and interactive visualizations. At κ = 10 , we construct a worst-case trajectory consisting of one planar spiral. At κ = 100 , our construction combines two planar spirals with different rotation angles. We provide interactive visualizations of these exact examples at https://nagsc.iol.berlin . For other user-selected values of κ, the website computes candidate worst-case trajectories numerically on demand. 

1.2 Related work 

The classical convergence guarantee for NAG can be improved without changing the algorithm. Lessard et al. (2016, Section 4.5) use integral quadratic constraints (IQCs) to compute tighter upper bounds through semidefinite programming, and Safavi et al. (2018) derive explicit bounds by solving the associated inequalities analytically. Gannot (2022, Proposition 2.13) obtains an analytic upper bound using frequency-domain inequalities. For squared distance, his bound already has the leading term 1 − 4/(3 √κ) as κ → ∞ ; we prove that the constant 4/3 is sharp. We extend this IQC and frequency-domain approach by constructing a finite separator for every contraction factor above the interpolation optimum. The resulting upper bound matches the rate attained by one fixed objective. Lyapunov functions provide a related approach to convergence analysis. Sanz-Serna and Zygalakis (2021) construct such functions for Nesterov methods and connect them to Lyapunov functions for differential equations. More generally, searches over prescribed families of quadratic Lyapunov inequalities can be formulated as semidefinite programs (Taylor et al., 2018; Upadhyaya et al., 2025), 2an approach implemented in AutoLyap (Upadhyaya et al., 2026). The exactness guarantees for these searches concern the chosen family of Lyapunov inequalities. Establishing sharpness requires a matching lower bound. Performance estimation formulates worst-case analysis after a fixed number of iterations as an optimization problem (Drori and Teboulle, 2014). The interpolation conditions of Taylor et al. (2017) make this formulation exact for smooth strongly convex functions and provide a way to construct matching examples. For the smooth convex version of Nesterov’s method, Kim and Fessler (2018, Theorem 5.2) give an O(t−3) upper bound on the smallest squared gradient norm evaluated through iteration t, under an initial distance bound. Du (2026, Theorem 7.2) constructs matching examples for t ≥ 7 in dimension t − 4. We impose these interpolation conditions for every pair of iteration indices along an infinite trajectory. One strongly convex function realizes this trajectory and attains the worst-case asymptotic squared-distance factor from a fixed initial point. Interpolation can also construct trajectories that demonstrate failure to converge. Goujaud et al. (2023) use it to find periodic trajectories and identify parameter choices for which first-order methods, including Nesterov acceleration, fail to converge. Regis (2026) studies related behavior by characterizing fixed points, two-point cycles, and their stability for heavy-ball and Nesterov momentum. For the Nesterov parameters considered here, convergence is already guaranteed. The trajectories we construct contract while rotating, and their decay determines the worst-case asymptotic factor. The behavior of these examples depends on the function class. For quadratic objectives, Hage-dorn and Jarre (2024) derive iteration-complexity bounds for Nesterov’s and Polyak’s methods by analyzing their linear recurrences. For twice continuously differentiable objectives, once convergence is established, linearization near the minimizer connects asymptotic convergence to a quadratic model (Van Scoy and Lessard, 2025, Lemma 3). Feng and Yuan (2026) establish local linear conver-gence of Nesterov’s method from sufficiently close initial points under a local Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition and twice continuous differentiability. They obtain an optimal local rate by tuning the parameters. Our function class assumes a Lipschitz continuous gradient but does not require twice continuous differentiability. The matching nonquadratic examples show that restricting this class to quadratic objectives can miss the worst-case asymptotic factor. The choice of algorithm parameters matters as well. For the smooth convex version of Nesterov’s method, which uses a varying momentum coefficient, Jang and Ryu (2025) prove that the iterates converge to a minimizer even when the minimizer is not unique. He and Fang (2026) study parameter sequences that combine accelerated behavior in the early iterations with a strongly convex asymptotic guarantee. Improved guarantees can also come from modifying the update itself. Park et al. (2023) analyze the optimized gradient method using Lyapunov functions and linear coupling, and introduce a strongly convex variant. This variant changes the momentum coefficient and adds a gradient correction, yielding a better function-value guarantee than the classical Nesterov bound. For strongly convex objectives, Lan et al. (2026, Section 5) develop regularization and restart methods with improved iteration-complexity guarantees for finding a small gradient. We keep the prescribed Nesterov step size and momentum coefficient fixed and determine the exact worst-case asymptotic rate of that iteration. Corollary 4.4 gives the corresponding exact factor for the gradient norm at the current iterate. 

Paper organization. Section 2 defines the function class, algorithm, and two asymptotic rates. Section 3 gives the angular interpolation conditions, defines the rate through a disk LP, and proves 3the common-circle reduction. Section 4 states the main theorem and outlines the matching lower and upper bounds. Section 5 presents a worst-case example at κ = 10 . The appendices provide the full proofs, additional examples, numerical results, and an algebraic obstruction to formulas by radicals.

# 3 Interpolation conditions for contracting trajectories 

We construct candidate worst-case trajectories whose components contract and rotate by fixed amounts at each iteration. We first state the conditions under which prescribed query points and gradients come from one function in F1,κ . We then express these conditions in terms of the contraction factor, rotation angles, and component weights. We use these conditions to define the feasible factors, then give a disk LP whose value is their largest element. Section 4 identifies this value with the exact worst-case rate.

# A Proof roadmap and dependencies 

The proof of Theorem 4.1 has four steps. First, an explicit finite cycle shows that cyclic monotonicity allows the interpolation values to be chosen as φi = qiφ0. For a fixed factor q and angular measure, determining these values therefore reduces to finding one scalar φ0 satisfying (10). Second, if the interpolation conditions have no solution at a given q, compactness gives a finite collection of cycle 14 inequalities that no angular measure satisfies. Convex separation then gives a strict cycle separator :a nonnegative weighted sum of their costs that is strictly negative at every angle. Third, suppose 

q⋆(s) > q quad (s) and choose an optimal angular measure, whose existence follows from Lemma B.1. For every q ∈ (q⋆(s), 1) , the interpolation conditions have no solution. We take a limit of the corresponding normalized separators as q decreases to q⋆(s). This limit is nonpositive and has average zero under the chosen optimal measure. The measure is therefore supported on the zeros of the limiting function. We show that this function has only finitely many zeros. We use the resulting measure to construct one finite-dimensional objective with the standard initialization and asymptotic factor q⋆(s). The quadratic example handles q⋆(s) = qquad (s). Fourth, every strict separator gives a uniform bound ∥xt − x⋆∥2 ≤ b(s, q )qt∥x0 − x⋆∥2. Taking tth roots before letting q ↓ q⋆(s) proves equality of both asymptotic factors. The lower-bound construction uses two limiting arguments. Compactness of probability measures handles all cycle constraints without assuming a bound on the number of support angles. Explicit bounds on the derivatives justify Taylor expansions of the limiting separator. Together with Rolle’s theorem and the fact that the limit is nonzero, these expansions show that it has only finitely many zeros. Appendix C.3 combines finite sums with the NAG recurrence to bound the sum of squared rescaled iterates. A finite induction establishes that this sum is finite for every trajectory. The resulting constant b(s, q ) is independent of the objective, dimension, initialization, and iteration count.

# E Quadratic forms and the KYP lemma 

The Kalman–Yakubovich–Popov (KYP) lemma expresses a frequency-domain inequality as a linear matrix inequality in a real symmetric matrix P . We apply it to the finite combination of interpolation inequalities used in Proposition C.4. This representation is not needed for Theorem 4.1. After removing the geometric contraction, its value remains constant along the trajectory. In suitable coordinates, the resulting motion is a rotation.

# I Rate values and their numerical evaluation 

While expressing the optimal contraction factor as a formula with radicals might not be possible in general as we have seen in Section H, we can still use our characterization to compute contraction values for varying values of κ in Table 2. To this end, we evaluate q⋆(√κ) for selected condition numbers and compare it with Gannot’s upper bound denoted by qG(√κ). The values are obtained by optimizing over mode weights and angles in the equivalent angular formulation. As can be seen, Gannot’s upper bound is quite tight with largest gaps for moderate κ.

Obtaining numerical rates. Basically the numerical rates are obtained by means of bisection. More precisely, for a given κ > 1, we range over test squared factors q. For each such q we want to assess numerically whether it is above or below q⋆(√κ) and to this end we choose a finite grid of angles in [0 , π ]. The unknowns are nonnegative mode weights summing to one and a nonnegative common potential value. We impose the two interpolation inequalities (10) for t = 1 , . . . , M , using the equivalent transformed-gradient normalization of Appendix C.3. With q and the angles fixed, these constraints are linear in the unknowns. The linear program maximizes a common slack in the inequalities: a nonnegative optimal slack indicates feasibility of this finite system, subject to numerical accuracy. If we considered all angles and all interpolation inequalities and solved the system exactly, then, within our search range, infeasibility would imply q > q ⋆(√κ). We are, however, dealing with three approximations: floating-point arithmetic, a finite angular grid, and a finite subset of the interpolation inequalities. We assess numerical accuracy through angular refinement and checks at additional iteration indices, nonetheless we stress that the obtained rates are only approximations. The bisection in q starts with the initial interval [(1 − 1/√κ)2, q G], whose endpoints are the quadratic factor and Gannot’s upper bound qG defined below. If the computed optimal slack is negative, we take q as the new upper endpoint of the numerical search; otherwise, we take it as the new lower endpoint. These updates do not constitute certified bounds on q⋆(√κ).
