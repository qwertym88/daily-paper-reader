A B S T R A C T 

Nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC) can be applied to large nonlinear systems with complex objectives while enforcing constraints, but the cost of solving the underlying optimization problems limits real-time application for systems with fast dynamics or many states. Neural networks can reduce this online cost by approximating the control law, but may violate constraints and typically require many precomputed optimizer solutions for training. The learning-based iterative solver for constrained optimization (LISCO) instead learns to predict primal-dual steps toward satisfaction of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions through self-supervised training without precomputed solutions, but its dense, monolithic architecture grows with the prediction horizon. We remove this horizon dependence by exploiting the temporal structure of the problem in the solver architecture, which makes the network size independent of the horizon while leaving the training loss and the solver iteration unchanged. A closed-loop resampling strategy further aligns the training distribution with closed-loop operation at no additional sampling cost. On an economic NMPC benchmark of a CSTR cascade, the proposed solver scales to larger problems than monolithic LISCO and achieves median speedups of up to a factor of four over IPOPT. In closed loop, it achieves comparable control performance to IPOPT with no observed constraint violations. 

## 1. Introduction 

Nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC) is highly relevant in process control, as it optimizes performance directly while enforcing input and state constraints [1, 2]. Its online application requires solving a nonlinear optimization problem at every sampling instant. The computational cost of this problem grows with the prediction horizon, the model size and the number of constraints [3]. Real-time application is therefore limited for systems with fast dynamics, for large systems with many states and inputs, and for robust NMPC formulations [4]. The online cost of NMPC has been addressed with efficient formulations such as the real-time iteration scheme [5] and fast solvers which exploit the structure of the NMPC problem [6, 7, 8, 9]. These methods lower the online cost substantially, but still require solving structured linear systems at each iteration, with a computational cost that grows with the problem size. For small linear problems, explicit MPC precomputes the control law offline [10], which does not extend to large nonlinear problems. This motivates learning-based methods, which are fast to evaluate and easy to deploy on control hardware. Existing learning-based methods can be broadly catego-rized into two areas, the direct approximation of the solution and the learning of the solver itself. In the first area, a neural network directly predicts the optimization solution or the control input. Approximate MPC follows this approach

by approximating the MPC control law, which maps the current state to the first control input of the optimal input sequence [11, 12, 13, 14]. Evaluating the network is often orders of magnitude faster than solving the problem online, but approximation errors can cause constraint violations and suboptimal closed-loop behavior [15, 16], and super-vised training typically requires sampling large numbers of optimizer solutions, which is computationally expensive. For constrained optimization more generally, the framework DC3 [17] adds a completion and correction step that reduces constraint violations, but incurs a computational overhead and leaves a residual optimality gap. Primal-dual learning and related self-supervised optimization proxies [18, 19, 20, 21] remove the need for sampled optimizer solutions. However, their direct predictions can still be infeasible or suboptimal. A further line of work learns an initial guess that warm-starts a classical solver [22, 23, 24], which accelerates the solution but still requires an online solver. In the second area, an update map or algorithm is learned and applied iteratively, so that the residual can be recom-puted and monitored online. In the learning-to-optimize (L2O) literature, the iteration map itself is learned, mostly for unconstrained problems such as neural network training [25, 26]. Related approaches tune or optimize the hyperpa-rameters of a given algorithm [27, 28] or unroll it and fit the parameters of the unrolled scheme [29, 30]. In these cases, learning builds on a prescribed optimization algorithm to accelerate the solution process. In our previous work, we introduced the learning-based iterative solver for constrained optimization (LISCO), tailored to constrained nonlinear pro-grams (NLP) and applied to NMPC [31, 32]. It learns a primal-dual update map for a modified Karush-Kuhn-Tucker

Each itera-tion is a single network evaluation and therefore requires no linear system solve. This approach has since been applied to mixed-integer and robust NMPC [33]. However, LISCO remains monolithic, as one dense net-work maps the full primal-dual residual to the full step. The input and output layers of such a network grow with the primal-dual dimension and therefore with the horizon, so the network size and the training effort have to grow with the problem [31]. Consistent with this, in our previous work we observed decreasing speedups over IPOPT [34] as the horizon length increased, with larger networks and more training epochs required to retain convergence [31]. Classical solvers such as IPOPT exploit the block-banded KKT structure induced by the dynamics and stage-wise costs and constraints using sparse linear algebra, e.g. MA27 [35], whereas the learned solver has so far not made use of this temporal structure. In this work, we close this gap by incorporating the temporal structure of the NMPC problem into the learned update map. Classical decomposition methods, such as the alternat-ing direction method of multipliers [36] and overlapping Schwarz schemes [37, 38, 39], also exploit the temporal locality of optimal control problems, but rely on local sub-problem solves, multiplier updates or explicit coordination at solve time. The proposed solver handles this coupling through overlapping residual windows and overlap averag-ing within the learned update map, so the solver iteration and the self-supervised training loss remain unchanged. The contributions of this paper are as follows. 1. We introduce a learned primal-dual solver for NMPC based on temporal decomposition. A shared neural network computes local updates in parallel on overlap-ping segments of the prediction horizon. Combining these local updates yields a full primal-dual step, while sharing the network across segments makes its size independent of the prediction horizon. 2. We extend the self-supervised training by a closed-loop resampling strategy, in which the initial state of a converged training sample is replaced by the predicted next state instead of being resampled uniformly. Since this state is already contained in the converged primal-dual iterate, the training distribution is aligned with closed-loop operation at no additional sampling cost. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We introduce the NMPC problem, its formulation as a parametric NLP with the modified KKT residual, the mono-lithic LISCO solver and the block-banded structure of the NMPC KKT system in Section 2. Next, we present the proposed architecture and its training in Section 3, describe the CSTR-cascade benchmark and the computational studies in Section 4, report the results in Section 5, and conclude in Section 6.

3.3. Self-supervised Training 

We train the shared network using the self-supervised loss from our previous work [31], summarized in Sec-tion 2.4. The loss (12) measures the residual of the full problem after the decomposed update and is averaged over a batch of primal-dual iterates. The residual and stopping cri-terion retain their interpretation from the monolithic solver, since a zero residual still certifies a modified KKT point. Training iterates are generated by the forward sampling strategy of Section 2.4, which uses the solver’s own iterates to cover a broad range of residual magnitudes. 

3.4. Closed-Loop Resampling 

The forward sampling strategy of Section 2.4 generates training iterates by applying the learned solver to sampled problems. In the original training procedure, the parameters defining these problems are resampled uniformly. For the NMPC formulation considered here, the problem parameter is the initial state, 𝐩 = 𝐱 init . During closed-loop operation, these initial states are the states visited by the controller. In economic NMPC in particular, the controller may drive the system to a steady state on the boundary of the feasible set, and such states with several simultaneously active con-straints are rarely produced by uniform sampling over the state space. A solver trained on uniformly sampled initial states therefore encounters a distribution shift in closed-loop operation. To align the training distribution, we modify the train-ing resampling strategy by introducing a reset probability 

𝑞 reset ∈ (0 , 1] , as summarized in Algorithm 2, which extends the self-supervised training procedure of our previous work [31]. Whenever a sample reaches the training tolerance 𝛿 train ,the initial state of the next training problem is set to the predicted next state 𝐱 1 of the converged solution with prob-ability 1 − 𝑞 reset . With probability 𝑞 reset , a new initial state is sampled uniformly instead. Since 𝐱 1 is a component of the primal solution ̂ 𝐰 , this state is available in the converged iterate at no additional cost, and the expected trajectory length is 1∕ 𝑞 reset steps. For 𝑞 reset = 1, every converged sample is replaced by a uniformly resampled parameter, which recovers the uniform training distribution from our previous work [31]. Two safeguards bound the effort spent on a single sam-ple, the iteration cap 𝑁 max on the number of steps and the stall cap 𝑁 stall on the number of steps without improvement of the residual. A sample that reaches either cap is treated as non-converged and resampled uniformly.

4.3. Computational Studies 

The decomposed solver is evaluated in four computa-tional studies, which investigate the scaling with the horizon and the number of reactors, the effect of the smoothing parameter 𝜖 , the influence of the window length and stride, and the closed-loop control performance. Throughout these studies, four methods for solving the NMPC problem (1) are compared. Monolithic LISCO is the architecture pre-sented in our previous work [31]. The decomposed solver of Section 3 is the proposed method and we use IPOPT [34] as the NLP-solver baseline. Approximate MPC maps the initial state directly to the first control action, is trained by imitation learning on precomputed optimizer solutions, and is considered only in the closed-loop comparison. The code for reproducing all studies is openly available. 1

All controllers are based on the same NMPC formu-lation and are evaluated on the same parameter samples, so speedups are compared per problem instance. The two learned solvers use the same hidden architecture, consisting of two hidden layers of 1024 units with GELU activation [46], and share the same training budget, optimizer set-tings, loss (12), resampling strategy, and evaluation settings. Their input and output layers necessarily differ, since by (10) the monolithic network maps the full residual to the full step, so these layers scale with 𝑁 𝑛 𝑠 𝑧 , whereas by (24) the decomposed network processes a single window and is independent of 𝑁 . The monolithic network therefore carries more trainable parameters than the decomposed one, with the gap widening with 𝑁 and 𝑛 𝑟 . Parameter counts and training times are reported per configuration in Section 5.1. 

> 1https://github.com/lukaslueken/lisco-decompose

IPOPT is configured with the sparse symmetric-indefinite linear solver MA27 from the HSL library [35], which exploits the block-banded structure of (15), at a tolerance of 10 −5 . Comparing solve times between the learned solvers and IPOPT further requires that both target a comparable accuracy. The learned solver runs at a fixed smoothing 𝜖 ,which relaxes the complementarity condition by up to 𝜖 2∕2 ,and the interior-point barrier parameter plays the same role for IPOPT, so the barrier target is set to 𝜇 target = 𝜖 2∕2 . With 

𝜖 = 10 −1 this gives 𝜇 target = 5 ⋅ 10 −3 , which is used in the horizon-scaling and closed-loop studies. In all studies, the solvers are cold-started to directly measure the quality of the predicted steps rather than the quality of an initial guess. The learned solvers initialize the primal variables at the midpoint of the scaling ranges and the multipliers at zero, in training and at inference, and IPOPT starts from its default initialization. The horizon-scaling study evaluates how solver perfor-mance scales with the horizon length 𝑁 and the number of reactors 𝑛 𝑟 . Monolithic LISCO and the decomposed solver are trained and then evaluated alongside IPOPT for the horizon lengths 𝑁 ∈ {10 , 20 , 50 , 100} and the reactor counts 𝑛 𝑟 ∈ {2 , 3, 5} , with all remaining settings fixed by Table 3. Training effort, parameter counts, convergence and solve times are reported in Section 5.1. The choice of the smoothing parameter 𝜖 involves a trade-off, as larger values improve the conditioning of the residual but relax the complementarity condition further. This trade-off is quantified in the smoothing study, in which the decomposed solver is trained and evaluated for a sweep of 𝜖 at a fixed configuration with 𝑛 𝑟 = 2 and 𝑁 = 50 . The resulting optimality gap is evaluated in Section 5.2 against IPOPT, which serves here as the objective reference and is therefore run at a tolerance of 10 −8 without an increased barrier parameter. In the overlap study, we vary the window length 𝑚 

and the stride 𝑠 of the decomposition, which determine the locality of the update map and the number of predictions per stage. This study considers only the decomposed solver, with all remaining settings taken from Table 3, and the results are reported in Section 5.3. In the closed-loop study, we apply the decomposed solver as an NMPC controller and compare it to IPOPT and approximate MPC. The three controllers start from the same set of 50 uniformly sampled initial states at a fixed configuration with 𝑛 𝑟 = 5 and 𝑁 = 50 . Each trajectory is simulated for 50 steps, and the closed-loop cost and constraint violations are compared in Section 5.4. In this study, the decomposed solver is trained with the closed-loop resampling of Section 3.4 at 𝑞 reset = 0 .02 , so the expected trajectory length of 50 steps during training matches the simulation length. All remaining studies use uniform resampling with 𝑞 reset = 1 . To isolate the effect of the training distribution, a second solver trained with uniform resampling and otherwise identical settings is eval-uated in the same closed-loop setting. Approximate MPC, as introduced in Section 2.1, is trained on closed-loop data.

We collect 150000 optimizer solutions from 3000 closed-loop trajectories with uniformly sampled initial states and 50 steps each. 4.4. Training, Evaluation and Implementation 

All four studies share one configuration, which is listed in Table 3. The overlap and closed-loop studies use 𝑛 𝑟 =5 reactors, while the smoothing study uses 𝑛 𝑟 = 2. The horizon-scaling study varies the number of reactors. Fol-lowing Algorithm 2, the training loop evaluates the loss after applying one solver step, then applies these steps to the current iterates and resamples the parameters of samples that reach the tolerance 𝛿 train , the iteration cap 𝑁 max or the stall cap 𝑁 stall . The number of epochs scales with the horizon as 𝑁 epochs = 6000 𝑁 , so that for longer horizons, training takes proportionally longer, with the same rule for both learned architectures. The default window length and stride, 

𝑚 = 5 and 𝑠 = 1 , are used in all studies except the overlap study, which examines the influence of this choice. The NLP and its residuals are evaluated in double precision, while the network operates in bfloat16. This halves the network memory relative to single precision without a meaningful loss in accuracy, since the network output is scaled relative to the residual magnitude and the residual itself is evaluated in double precision. All runs use Δ𝑡 = 1 s , so the horizon length and the predicted time interval scale together. While the objective function (33) is convex, the nonlin-ear dynamics (32) cause the NLP to be nonconvex. There-fore, the Hessian of the Lagrangian can be indefinite, and Assumption 5 of [31], which underlies the well-posedness result of Section 2.4, may not hold. To keep the training problem well-posed, we form the training gradient with the convexification and regularization of [31]. We replace the Hessian of the Lagrangian with the objective Hessian, which is diagonal with entries 2 𝑤 𝑢 𝐴 on the feed rates of A and zero elsewhere, and add to its diagonal 𝜌 reg 𝐈 with the regularization factor 𝜌 reg > 0 so that it is positive definite. We further apply the same dual regularization as in [31] with factor 𝜔 > 0 to improve the conditioning of the KKT matrix during training when the multipliers grow large. Both modifications act only on the gradient used to update the network during training and leave the residual (8) unchanged, so convergence at inference and all reported results are those of the original problem. ## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced a learned primal-dual solver for NMPC based on temporal decomposition. A shared neural network computes local updates in parallel on overlapping horizon segments. This makes the network size independent of the prediction horizon while retaining self-supervised training without precomputed optimizer solutions. We also intro-duced a closed-loop resampling strategy that uses predicted next states to align the training distribution with closed-loop operation at no additional sampling cost. Future work will investigate decomposed prediction of initial iterates for warm-started operation and extend the architecture to scenario trees in robust NMPC and to weakly coupled process networks.
