Abstract — Generating dynamically feasible motions for legged and manipulating robots requires solving a large, nonlin-ear, non-convex optimal control problem (OCP) in which con-tact wrenches must satisfy friction-cone constraints throughout the motion, not only at isolated instants. Existing solvers are sensitive to the initial guess and scale poorly with the number of joints. This paper introduces LEAP, which transcribes the OCP with pseudospectral collocation and, instead of a Newton or SQP solve, integrates a primal–dual gradient flow of a Powell– Hestenes–Rockafellar augmented Lagrangian to compute asolution. Because the flow is stiff, this paper designs a linearly implicit integrator with a Gauss–Newton metric, momentum, and a merit-based step controller that keeps the augmented Lagrangian monotone. Across locomotion benchmarks on five robot platforms, from planar hoppers to 32-joint humanoids, LEAP is the only method to solve every tested problem, achieves substantial speedups over Crocoddyl and Aligator on sev-eral benchmarks, and reaches trackable references at smaller compute budgets. The resulting trajectories are validated by tracking them in a high-fidelity simulator and on hardware. We will make the code public upon final acceptance of this paper.

Planning such motions means choosing joint torques and contact forces that respect the robot’s dynamics, its actuation limits, and the physics of contact, which requires the contact wrench to stay inside a friction cone at every instant. Trajectory optimization formalizes this as an Optimal Control Problem (OCP) over hybrid dynamics: minimize a cost, subject to the equations of motion in each contact mode, and the constraints above. Solving this OCP is difficult for three reasons. The dynam-ics are nonlinear and change discontinuously across contact modes. The constraints, in particular the friction cones and the dynamics themselves, are nonlinear and non-convex, and any solver must decide how to enforce them and where in time. And the resulting problem is non-convex in the decision variables, so the outcome depends on the initial guess, and a good one is often unavailable for a new behavior or a new robot. We refer to these as the dynamics, constraint, and initialization challenges. State-of-the-art solvers exploit the temporal structure of the dynamics to make each iteration cheap. Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP) methods such as Crocoddyl [18] and Aligator [12] combine quadratic approximations of the value function with multiple shooting, and direct methods such as acados [24, 25] transcribe the OCP into a nonlinear program (NLP) with banded structure. In both families each iteration is a Newton-type step, and this is what makes the constraint and initialization challenges persist. A Newton step is only meaningful near a solution, so these arXiv:2610.20936v1 [cs.RO] 14 Oct 2026 solvers rely on a good warm start and on line-search or trust-region globalization whose parameters must be tuned per problem. Constraints are handled by penalties, by projection onto a box, or by an augmented Lagrangian inside the Riccati recursion, and they are enforced only at discrete knots, so the friction cone can be violated between them. In Section V illustrates that, on the contact-constrained problems, these solvers scale poorly with the number of joints and their performance depends strongly on hyper-parameters. Gradient flows (GF) offer a different way to handle the initialization challenge. Instead of taking Newton steps, the trajectory is deformed continuously in a pseudo-time until it becomes stationary for an action functional. Every step of the flow is a descent step that is well defined anywhere, not only near a solution, so the method tolerates poor initial guesses and admits continuation naturally. [16] and [15] introduced the Affine Geometric Heat Flow (AGHF), whose steady state solves the OCP, and [7], [17] and [8] extended it to free boundary conditions and to low-dimensional legged systems. [2] and [3] improved the numerics to reach higher-dimensional systems, and [14]extends the idea to extended Lagrangians. What these methods gain on initialization they give back on the other two challenges. Constraints enter as quadratic penalties, so they are never satisfied exactly, and raising the penalty to reduce the violation makes the flow stiffer. The stiff flow is then integrated with general-purpose solvers whose error control is unaware of the optimization structure, so the dynamics challenge reappears as a numerical one. This paper introduces LEAP, a gradient-flow trajectory optimizer for robots in contact under a prescribed contact sequence, together with a numerical scheme designed for that flow. It keeps the robustness to initialization of gradient flows while resolving the aforementioned constraint and stiffness problems. Our contributions are four-fold: First, we utilize a Powell–Hestenes–Rockafellar (PHR) augmented Lagrangian formulation and derive a two-time-scale primal–dual flow to enable exact constraint handling (Section III). Second, we describe how to extend this approach to hybrid dynamical systems and illustrate how to ensure that wrench feasibility is satisfied for all timein Lemma 2. Third, we develop a linearly implicit solver to ensure that the augmented Lagrangian monotonically decreases along the numerical solution to the primal-dual flow (Section IV). Finally, we compare LEAP against Crocoddyl and Aligator on solve time and terminal tracking accuracy under a time budget across two tasks and five robots with two to thirty-two joints with solution quality judged by a whole-body tracking controller in a high-fidelity simulator (Section V). Section III reduced trajectory optimization to integrating the flow (14). Doing so with an off-the-shelf integrator fails for three reasons. First, the flow is stiff when the penalties are large [10]. Second, its Jacobian jumps whenever an in-equality constraint enters or leaves the active set A(z, γ ) := 

Ed ∪ { i ∈ I d : wi(z, γ ) > 0}, which violates the Lipschitz assumption behind the error control of implicit integrators. Third, the point of the flow is the monotonicity of Proposition 6(ii), and a discretization that does not preserve it forfeits the robustness that motivated the approach. Section IV-A builds a step that handles stiffness and the jumps, Section IV-B controls that step so that monotonicity survives discretiza-tion, Section IV-C discretizes the outer time scale of the multipliers, Section IV-D decides when the discrete solution is good enough, and Section IV-E handles initialization and mesh adaptation. A. Experimental setup 

To fairly compare the different methods, we define a set of benchmark problems and criteria to evaluate the solution. All experiments are run in a machine with a 2x AMD EPYC 7742 64-Core Processor and for each experiment each method is allocated 12 physical cores. 

1) Benchmark Problems: The results presented here com-prise ten benchmark problems across two tasks: walking over uneven terrain and jumping over a fence (Figure 2). Each task is evaluated on five robot platforms: a planar hopper( nj = 2 ), Unitree’s Go2 quadruped ( nj = 12 ), H1 biped ( nj = 19 ), G1 humanoid ( nj = 29 ), and PAL Robotics’ Talos humanoid (nj = 32 ). All methods solve the same optimization problem to the extend their formulations allow: same cost function, constraint and initial guess. 

2) Solution quality criteria: Each optimization method produces optimal trajectories for the states and torques at the collocation nodes. Small constraint residuals at the colloca-tion nodes do not ensure that a tracking controller can exe-cute the resulting motion and reach the prescribed terminal state. We therefore evaluate the references through closed-loop simulation and assess terminal goal accuracy sepa-rately from optimization feasibility. A whole-body inverse-dynamics controller tracks each reference in MuJoCo, using the same controller configuration for all optimization meth-ods on a given task and robot. We refer to the state obtained by numerically integrating the robot dynamics under the controller’s torques and the simulator’s contact forces as the simulated rollout. For the solve-time comparisons, success is determined by the rollout’s error relative to the problem’s prescribed terminal rest pose and foot placements. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented LEAP, a gradient-flow method for trajectory optimization of robots with prescribed contact sequences. The method combines pseudospectral colloca-tion with a two-time-scale PHR augmented-Lagrangian flow whose equilibria satisfy the KKT conditions of the tran-scribed problem. B´ ezier wrench parameterizations certify convex contact-wrench constraints throughout each interval when their control points are feasible. A linearly implicit Gauss–Newton integrator preserves descent at fixed multipli-ers and penalties, while feasibility initialization and adaptive mesh refinement address initial infeasibility and violations between collocation nodes. Across ten benchmarks on five robot platforms, LEAP meets the terminal tracking criteria in all ten simulated rollouts, compared with seven for Crocoddyl and eight for Aligator. It achieves substantial solve-time reductions on several benchmarks and lower normalized terminal error over a range of compute budgets. A hopscotch execution on the Unitree Go2 further demonstrates hardware tracking of an optimized reference, with a joint-position RMS error of 0.0682 rad and centimeter-scale RMS position errors.
