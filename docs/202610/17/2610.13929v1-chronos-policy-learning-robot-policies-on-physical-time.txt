# ABSTRACT 

Robot policies often convert sensing and control into fixed-rate observation–action sequences, leaving physical time implicit in sequence position. This assumption breaks when observation rates or delays vary, or when inference latency changes. We introduce Chronos Policy 1, which makes physical time explicit by condition-ing on observation capture times and desired action execution times. We study two ways to incorporate time into a diffusion transformer: adding timestamp embeddings to token features, or encoding physical time in positional encod-ing through time-aware rotary positional encodings (time-aware RoPE). We find that time-aware RoPE is more robust, allowing attention to depend directly on physical time while keeping token content unchanged. During training, we tem-porally randomize which observations are sampled and when the action chunk begins, exposing the policy to a wider range of sensing and execution sched-ules that may occur during deployments. This also enables learning from asyn-chronous, multi-rate data without forcing all measurements onto a fixed temporal grid. Across simulation and real-world experiments, Chronos Policy improves performance on dynamic tasks, where observation-to-action timing is critical, and on multisensory tasks, where the relative timing between observations mat-ters. On standard quasi-static tasks, it preserves nominal performance while im-proving robustness to changes in sensing and inference timing. Project website:

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Real robot systems rarely run on a fixed clock. Consider a robot grasping a moving object from a conveyor belt: the same displacement between two images can correspond to very different object velocities depending on whether the images were captured 20 ms or 200 ms apart. Likewise, the appropriate grasp depends on when the predicted action will actually be executed. Successful control therefore depends not only on what the robot observes, but also on when observations are captured 

and when actions execute .More generally, robot learning systems are exposed to timing variation at every stage of sensing and control. Sensors operate at different rates and latencies, inference takes time, controllers may execute at different frequencies. Yet many imitation learning methods convert demonstrations into fixed-rate observation–action sequences, using sequence position as a proxy for physical time. This assumes a consistent relationship between sequence position and elapsed time; when that relationship changes, the policy cannot tell how much time has passed between observations or how far in the future an action will execute. Prior work has addressed specific deployment-time effects, such as latency matching (Chi et al., 2024) and asynchronous action-chunk execution (Black et al., 2026). We take a different approach: instead of treating timing variation as an external mismatch to correct, we represent it explicitly as part of the policy input. Concretely, we formulate the policy as:

Chronos Policy therefore replaces index-based positional encoding with time-aware rotary positional encodings (time-aware RoPE): observation tokens use their capture times to, while noisy action tokens use their queried execution times ta as rotary coordinates. Timestamps also allow us to vary the timing of training examples. We randomly change which observations are selected and when the action chunk starts. Because the policy sees these timestamps, it can correctly interpret each sampled sequence even when its timing differs from the nominal training setup. This exposes the policy to timing variations that may occur at deployment and lets it use measurements that fixed-rate downsampling would otherwise discard. We evaluate Chronos Policy across three types of tasks: dynamic tasks, where observation-to-action timing affects the correct control; multisensory tasks, where the relative timing between observations matters; and standard quasi-static tasks, where precise timing is less critical. # 2 RELATED WORK 

Asynchronous multisensory learning. Multisensory observations provide feedback that enables imitation learning policies to succeed on contact-rich tasks where vision alone is insufficient (Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; 2023; Hou et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026; Clark et al., 2026). However, heterogeneous sensors often operate at different sampling rates, motivating methods that explicitly account for these differing temporal resolutions. Reactive Diffusion Policy combines slow visual planning with fast tactile feedback using separate modules (Xue et al., 2025), while ImplicitRDP integrates both streams within a single network (Chen et al., 2026). ManipForce uses frequency-aware embeddings and cross-attention to fuse asynchronous RGB and force/torque streams (Lee et al., 2025). LAG-Fusion composes modality-specific diffusion policies at their native inference rates, rebasing delayed guidance before fusion (He et al., 2026), and FA-RDP adapts inference frequency to the manipulation phase (Zhuo et al., 2026). These approaches establish the importance of preserving high frequency feedback. 

Inference delay and chunk continuity. Inference delay creates a mismatch between when obser-vations are captured, when a policy predicts an action, and when that action is executed (Xiao et al., 2020; Chi et al., 2024). For action-chunk policies, this also raises a consistency problem between newly predicted actions and commands already committed for execution. Prior work addresses this through consistency-based decoding (Liu et al., 2025), overlapping inference and execution with prefix inpainting (Black et al., 2026), training-time conditioning on committed actions (Black et al., 2025), masked action generation (Wang et al., 2026b;a), or predicting the future execution-time state (Tang et al., 2025). Most closely related, Delay-Aware Diffusion Policy conditions on measured inference delay and constructs delay-compensated targets (Liao et al., 2025). Another line of work reduces inference latency by changing the diffusion process itself. Diffusion Forcing introduces independent noise levels for individual sequence positions, enabling flexible partial denoising sched-ules (Chen et al., 2024), while Streaming Diffusion Policy applies variable noise levels across the action horizon to generate near-term actions with fewer denoising steps and lower latency (Høeg et al., 2024). These approaches reduce the timing mismatch by making policy inference faster, while Chronos Policy makes the remaining sensing and execution timing explicit to the policy; the two directions are complementary. 

Explicit time in robot control. In addition to observation and inference delay, prior work has also made time an explicit variable in robot control. Locomotion policies have conditioned on the time available to reach a target, allowing behavior to adapt to temporal requirements (Rudin et al., 2022). More recently, manipulation policies have been conditioned on remaining task time and a time-ratio signal, enabling a single policy to vary its execution strategy across different temporal regimes (Jia & Chen, 2025). Other work makes control timing itself adaptive: CTCO selects continuous options with variable durations (Karimi et al., 2023), while TARC jointly predicts actions and their application durations to vary control frequency online (Sukhija et al., 2025). These works use time to control the speed or duration of behavior. Chronos Policy instead represents when observations are captured and when actions are executed. This gives a more general and natural formulation that covers observation rate, sensor delay, cross-modal timing, and action execution delay within a single policy. 

Continuous-time representations and attention. Explicit time representations are well established for irregular time series. mTAN learns continuous-time embeddings for attention over irregular obser-vations (Shukla & Marlin, 2021), and ContiFormer combines continuous dynamics with transformer 3Preprint

Most closely related, BulletTime conditions video generation on continuous world-time coordinates through time-aware rotary embeddings (Wang et al., 2026c). Chronos Policy brings this continuous-time view to robot imitation learning, jointly representing the timestamps of heterogeneous observations and the requested execution times of actions. 

# 3 METHOD 

Chronos Policy makes observation and action timing explicit in the policy. Each observation is associated with when it was captured, and each predicted action with when it should execute. We implement this interface with a diffusion transformer using time-aware RoPE , which introduces time through attention while leaving the token embeddings unchanged. During training, we also vary observation and action timing while preserving the corresponding timestamps, so the policy learns to interpret the timing changes it may encounter at deployment. 3.1 POLICY FORMULATION ON PHYSICAL TIME 

Let Oto denote observations captured at times to. At time t, we query an H-step action chunk at 

ta,j = t + dt + j∆ta, j = 0 , . . . , H − 1, (2) where dt is the delay before the first queried action and ∆ta is the action interval. The policy is therefore 

Ata ∼ πθ (· | Oto , to, ta) . (3) This formulation makes the timing information explicit. The spacing of to captures observation rate, the gap between observation and action times captures measurement delay and execution latency, and 

ta specifies the desired execution frequency. For multimodal sensing, each modality simply keeps its own capture times rather than being aligned to a common sampling grid. 3.2 ENCODING PHYSICAL TIME IN THE POLICY 

Given explicit timestamps in the policy inputs, we must decide how temporal information should enter the network. One option is to encode each timestamp and add it directly to the corresponding token embedding, allowing time to modify the token representation itself. An alternative is to keep the token embedding unchanged and use time only through the positional encoding in attention. We 4Preprint compare these two choices in Section 4.3; the latter is consistently more robust to temporal variation. We therefore adopt time-aware RoPE .Every token is assigned a physical timestamp relative to the policy request time t. Image patches inherit the capture time of their frame, proprioceptive and force/torque tokens use their measurement times, and action tokens use their queried execution times. Thus, observation timestamps describe when measurements were captured relative to the request, while action timestamps lie in the future according to the requested execution schedule. Modality and spatial embeddings continue to represent token content, while physical time enters separately through the rotary positional encoding. During training, we expose the policy to different sensing and execution timings through temporal randomization . For each demonstration, we randomly vary the observation rate and delivery delay of each modality. We then select the measurements that would be available at that request time, while keeping their original capture timestamps. We also randomize when the predicted action chunk should begin. Sampling the start delay dt gives the queried execution times in Eq. 2. Together, these two randomizations expose the policy to temporal settings that may occur at deploy-ment. They also allow different measurements from the recorded trajectory to be used across training examples, including measurements that a fixed-rate downsampling scheme would otherwise discard. The timestamps are what make this possible. After randomization, the same token positions can correspond to different physical intervals. Without explicit time, these examples would be ambiguous. At deployment, we use the same physical-time interface as during training. Each available observation retains its capture time to, while the noisy action tokens are assigned queried execution times ta.These action times enter time-aware RoPE during denoising, so when action tokens attend to observations or to one another, the attention depends on the corresponding observation-to-action and action-to-action time differences. Changing ta therefore changes the actions predicted by the policy. During deployment, we execute action chunks asynchronously: while the next chunk is being predicted, the robot continues executing the previous one. In simulation, the action start delay dt is set from the injected inference latency. On the real robot, the current inference time is not known in advance, so we estimate dt from previously completed requests. When a new prediction arrives, actions whose queried execution times have already passed are discarded, and the remaining actions are executed according to ta.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We structure our experiments around three questions. (1) when is time information useful? We study dynamic tasks, where the correct action depends on how the environment changes over time, and multisensory tasks, where the timing between different observations matters. (2) which parts of the time-aware policy matter? We ablate the time representation and temporal randomization used during training. (3) does adding time hurt tasks where timing is less important? We evaluate on standard quasi-static manipulation tasks to test whether Chronos Policy preserves nominal performance while improving robustness to timing changes at deployment. 4.1 TASKS AND EVALUATION SETUP 

We evaluate Chronos Policy in three types of tasks. Conveyor is a dynamic pick-and-place task with randomized belt speed, where the policy must account for object motion and the time between sensing and action execution. Pivoting is a contact-rich multisensory task that combines vision, proprioception, and force/torque measurements acquired at different rates and times. We also evaluate on six standard quasi-static manipulation tasks to test whether explicit time modeling preserves performance when timing is less critical. We evaluate each policy under different timing conditions. Nominal uses the timing seen during training. We then vary observation timing by changing sensing frequency and delay, and vary 

inference latency by changing the delay before predicted actions are executed. Finally, the combined 

setting varies both observation timing and inference latency, providing a stronger test of robustness to temporal mismatch. 4.2 WHEN IS TIME INFORMATION USEFUL ?

Time substantially improves dynamic manipulation. Figure 4a shows a clear advantage on Conveyor. Under nominal timing, Chronos Policy improves success from 73.1% to 82.0%. More importantly, the gap grows when deployment timing changes. With moderate observation variation, Diffusion Policy drops to 15.6% while Chronos Policy retains 77.6%; under inference delays up to 200 ms, Diffusion Policy falls to 1.8% while Chronos Policy achieves 76.4%. Even under the combined perturbation, Chronos Policy retains 32.7% success while Diffusion Policy fails entirely. This result shows that sequence order alone is not enough to determine object motion when observation intervals vary, nor to determine how far ahead to act when execution is delayed. Providing both observation and action times allows the policy to interpret these changes directly. 

Time improves robustness in multisensory manipulation. The Pivoting results show a different pattern (Fig. 4b). Under mild timing changes, Chronos Policy and Diffusion Policy perform similarly, while the advantage of explicit time becomes larger as temporal mismatch increases. Under inference delays up to 200 ms, success improves from 51.3% to 60.2%, and under the combined condition from 19.6% to 38.4%. 6Preprint

We then study what makes a time-aware policy effective by ablating two key design choices in Chronos Policy: temporal variation during training and the representation of physical time in the transformer (Fig. 3). 

Temporal randomization requires explicit time information. Temporal randomization has two effects: it exposes the policy to a wider range of sensing and execution schedules that may occur during deployment, and it allows training to use measurements that would otherwise be discarded when demonstrations are downsampled to a fixed temporal grid. This additional diversity can act as 7Preprint useful data augmentation. On Conveyor, temporal randomization alone increases Diffusion Policy’s average success across the five perturbed conditions from 6.3% to 26.9%. Thus, even without timestamps, exposing the policy to more temporal variation can improve robustness when the relevant timing structure is relatively simple. The same strategy does not generalize to multisensory Pivoting. There, temporal randomization reduces the perturbed-condition average from 48.4% to 21.6%. Without timestamps, the policy cannot tell what timing produced each sampled history: the same sequence positions may correspond to different physical intervals, and asynchronous measurements from different modalities become difficult to interpret. Once physical time is explicit, however, temporal randomization becomes consistently useful. It increases the perturbed-condition average from 48.8% to 63.1% on Conveyor and from 33.3% to 55.3% on Pivoting. Temporal randomization therefore broadens the training distribution, while timestamps make that variation interpretable. 

Time-aware RoPE provides a better representation of physical time. We compare 

Chronos-RoPE with Chronos-AddTime , which follows the common choice of encoding con-tinuous time with Fourier features (Tancik et al., 2020). Each timestamp, expressed relative to the policy request time, is mapped through Fourier features and an MLP, and the resulting time embed-ding is added directly to the corresponding token. Observation tokens use their capture times, while action tokens use their queried execution times; for action tokens, the time embedding is added to the noisy action representation at each diffusion step. In contrast, Chronos-RoPE keeps the token embeddings unchanged and introduces the same physical timestamps only through the positional encoding in attention. On Conveyor, Chronos-AddTime performs slightly better under nominal timing, but 

Chronos-RoPE performs better in every other setting, increasing the temporal condition aver-age from 51.5% to 63.1%. On Pivoting, Chronos-RoPE outperforms Chronos-AddTime in every condition and improves the perturbed-condition average from 33.5% to 55.3%. We hypothesize this is due to its simpler separation of content and time, and to the fact that pairwise elapsed time directly modulates attention rather than being inferred from additive token features. We therefore use 

Chronos-RoPE as Chronos Policy in the experiments. 4.4 DOES TIME HURT STANDARD QUASI -S TATIC MANIPULATION ?We next evaluate six standard quasi-static manipulation tasks, where precise timing is less critical to the task. Under nominal timing, Chronos Policy and Diffusion Policy achieve comparable performance as shown in Fig. 4c (full results are shown in the Appendix), showing that explicitly modeling time does not sacrifice performance. The advantage becomes clear when deployment timing differs from training. Averaged across the five perturbed conditions, success improves from 59.7% with Diffusion Policy to 73.4% with Chronos Policy, with the largest gains under wide sensing variation and combined perturbations. Overall, Chronos Policy preserves nominal performance while providing substantially greater robust-ness to temporal mismatch. When timing is close to the training setup, explicit time does not sacrifice any performance; when sensing or inference timing changes, it gives the policy information about the observation and action timing that is absent from the baseline. 4.5 DOES TIME AWARENESS IMPROVE REAL -W ORLD ROBUSTNESS ?We finally ask whether the benefits of explicit time carry over to real robot deployment, where sensing and inference timing vary naturally. As shown in Figure 5, we evaluate three tasks that mirror the two settings studied in simulation: Dynamic Cup is a dynamic manipulation task, similar to Conveyor, where the robot must place a cup onto a moving saucer; Peg Insertion and Pivoting are contact-rich multisensory tasks that combine vision, proprioception, and force/torque feedback. These results suggest that asynchronous execution and explicit time awareness address complementary aspects of temporal mismatch. 

# 5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We introduce Chronos Policy, which grounds robot policy learning in physical time by conditioning on when observations are captured and when actions should execute. This formulation provides a unified representation of sensing rate, measurement delay, and inference latency within a single policy. Across dynamic, multisensory, and standard quasi-static manipulation tasks in simulation and on real robots, Chronos Policy improves robustness to temporal mismatch while preserving nominal performance. A direction for future work is to extend Chronos policies beyond fixed action rates to variable control frequencies and temporal scaling, allowing robots to execute the same behavior faster or slower than demonstration.

# A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

A.1 POLICY ARCHITECTURE AND INPUT REPRESENTATIONS 

Our implementation of Diffusion Policy (DP) and Chronos Policy uses the same backbone: a 16-layer diffusion transformer with width 512 and eight attention heads. A pretrained SigLIP2 base encoder extracts 768-dimensional image-patch features at 256 × 256 resolution, which are projected into the transformer. Proprioceptive and action vectors are embedded as separate tokens; Pivoting and Insertion tasks additionally include force/torque tokens. Transformer blocks combine self-attention over non-image tokens with cross-attention to image patches (Fig. 6). 

Figure 6: Architecture of Chronos Policy. Proprioceptive, optional force/torque, and noisy action tokens interact through self-attention and attend to image features through cross-attention. Time-aware RoPE encodes observation capture times and queried action times in attention. The transformer iteratively denoises the action chunk. Conveyor and the six standard tasks use two image observations per camera and two proprioceptive observations. Simulated Pivoting uses two images, three pose observations, and 32 six-dimensional force/torque samples. We represent a pose by its position and 6D rotation. Pivoting predicts a 19-dimensional adaptive-compliance action containing a nominal pose, a virtual-target pose, and stiffness following (Hou et al., 2025); the same controller and action representation are used for all methods. Other simulation tasks use absolute pose and gripper or hand commands. Every policy predicts 16 actions at 20 Hz. A.2 BASELINES AND TIME -AWARE VARIANTS 

Diffusion Policy. The transformer-based baseline uses sequence position without observation or action timestamps. Its temporal-randomization variant uses the same randomized observation schedules and action-start delays as Chronos Policy, but does not receive their physical times. 

Chronos Policy. Time-aware RoPE uses physical timestamps in seconds, measured relative to the policy request time. Observation tokens use their capture times, while action tokens use their queried execution times, so inference latency shifts the action timestamps into the future. We rotate 16 query–key coordinate pairs using logarithmically spaced periods between 0.1 and 2 s. Images, proprioception, force/torque, and actions retain their respective capture or query times. 

Chronos AddTime. The AddTime variant replaces time-aware RoPE with 16 sine and 16 cosine features of the same timestamps. A shared 32 → 512 → 512 MLP with GELU maps these features into an embedding added to each timed token. For action tokens, this embedding is added at each denoising step. AddTime and RoPE share the same data, token layout, diffusion conditioning, and temporal randomization. 

Real-Time Chunking. For the real-world baseline, we adapt RTC (Black et al., 2026) to the Diffu-sion Policy DDIM sampler using action-prefix inpainting. Previously committed actions constrain the next chunk while inference overlaps execution. RTC and Chronos Policy estimate inference 13 Preprint latency from completed requests; DP and RTC do not receive observation capture timestamps as policy inputs. A.3 TRAINING DETAILS 

We use ℓ1 loss for action prediction and a cosine diffusion-noise schedule. We optimize with AdamW, learning rate 3 × 10 −4, betas (0 .95 , 0.999) , and weight decay 10 −6; the pretrained image encoder uses one tenth of the learning rate. Training uses BF16 and a cosine learning-rate schedule. All policies are trained for 50 epochs, where one epoch is one complete pass through the training dataset. Table 1 lists the task-specific settings. Non-rotational action and proprioceptive dimensions use range normalization; 6D rotation coordinates are left unchanged. Image augmentation applies random cropping and color jitter, each with probability 0.3, followed by antialiased resizing to the encoder resolution. Temporal randomization alone can improve robustness, but does not resolve temporal ambiguity. 

On Conveyor, adding temporal randomization to Diffusion Policy substantially improves several perturbation settings. Success increases from 15.6% to 60.9% under Small observation variation and from 14.2% to 47.3% under delays up to 100 ms. This suggests that exposing the policy to a broader range of observation and execution schedules can itself act as useful data augmentation. However, the benefit does not extend cleanly to more severe shifts: under Wide sensing, success only improves from 0.0% to 6.4%, and both variants remain near zero under the Combined condition. Randomization also lowers nominal Conveyor performance from 73.1% to 53.6%, indicating a trade-off when temporal variation is introduced without telling the policy what timing generated each sample. This limitation is more pronounced on Pivoting. Adding temporal randomization to Diffusion Policy reduces performance in every condition, including nominal success from 61.6% to 29.8%. Unlike Conveyor, Pivoting requires relating vision, proprioception, and force/torque measurements sampled at different times. When these schedules are randomized but their timestamps are hidden, the same token positions can correspond to different cross-modal time offsets. The additional diversity therefore also introduces ambiguity that the policy cannot resolve. 

Timestamps and temporal randomization are complementary. Explicit time without temporal randomization already provides substantial robustness to execution delay. On Conveyor, RoPE without randomization reaches 76.4% and 69.3% under delays up to 100 and 200 ms, respectively. However, it is much less robust when observation timing changes, dropping to 20.2% under Wide sensing and 10.7% under the Combined condition. Adding temporal randomization raises these results to 45.6% and 32.7%, respectively. A similar pattern appears on Pivoting: RoPE without randomization drops to 9.1% under Wide sensing and 4.2% under Combined, whereas the full model reaches 48.0% and 38.4%. These results clarify the roles of the two components. Timestamps make a particular sensing and execution schedule interpretable, while temporal randomization exposes the policy to a broader set of such schedules during training. Neither component alone provides the same robustness as their combination. 

The representation of time also matters. AddTime and RoPE receive the same timestamps and temporal randomization, isolating how physical time enters the transformer. AddTime achieves slightly higher nominal performance on Conveyor (85.8% versus 82.0%), but RoPE performs better in every perturbed condition. The difference is especially large under Wide sensing (45.6% versus 24.0%) and Combined perturbations (32.7% versus 7.8%). On Pivoting, RoPE has higher mean success than AddTime in every condition, including 48.0% versus 23.6% under Wide sensing and 38.4% versus 16.7% under Combined. Since the two variants differ only in how the same physical timestamps are represented, these results are consistent with directly encoding pairwise elapsed time in attention being more robust than adding time to token content.
