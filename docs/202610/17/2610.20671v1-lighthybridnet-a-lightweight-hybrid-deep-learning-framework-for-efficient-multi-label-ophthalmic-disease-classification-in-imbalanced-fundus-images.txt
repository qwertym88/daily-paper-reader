(Dated: October 16, 2026) Eye diseases are a major global cause of blindness and vision impairment, highlighting the vital need for accurate and early diagnosis to prevent further deterioration. Despite advances in medical imaging, classifying retinal diseases from fundus images remains challenging due to the complexity and subtlety of visual features. This study aims to develop and validate LightHybridNet, a novel lightweight hybrid deep learning framework, to improve the classification of eye diseases from fun-dus images. The model incorporates advanced architectural features, such as depthwise separable convolutions and compound scaling mechanisms, to enhance diagnostic accuracy and robustness. To validate the proposed model, the ODIR-5K dataset, comprising 8 disease categories, has been used. To enhance image quality, several pre-processing approaches were applied, including histogram equalisation, colour space conversion, and contrast adjustment, resulting in high-resolution final im-ages. Furthermore, image data augmentation was employed to increase the dataset size, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to generalise during training. The hybrid architecture helps the model to concentrate on the most relevant regions of the images, while capturing and integrating details at different scales, significantly improving classification performance. To evaluate its effectiveness, the model was compared with various transfer-learning models. The results show that LightHy-bridNet consistently outperforms the baselines, achieving a weighted F1-score of 91.51%, a macro AUC of 98.26%, and a Cohen’s Kappa of 90.14% on the validation set. These results demonstrate superior accuracy, balanced discrimination across classes, and strong agreement despite severe class imbalance. Furthermore, model interpretability was enhanced by employing various explainable AI methods, such as Grad-CAM, to identify and emphasise key features influencing the decision-making process. LightHybridNet represents a promising advancement in automated ocular disease detection, offering improved diagnostic reliability for clinical use. 

Keywords: Multi-label classification, Lightweight Deep Learning, Fundus Imaging, Class Imbalance, Mo-bileNetV3, EfficientNet, Explainable AI. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Vision impairment and blindness represent one of the most significant and persistent global health challenges of the modern era. Sight plays a fundamental role in ed-ucation, employment, independence, and overall quality of life. When vision is impaired or lost, individuals of-ten become less productive, rely more on caregivers, and participate less socially. According to the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness (IAPB) Vision Atlas, more than 1.1 billion people worldwide live with vision loss, primarily due to lack of access to basic eye care services [2]. Alarmingly, over 90% of people with vi-sion impairment reside in low- and middle-income coun-tries, where healthcare infrastructure and specialist avail-ability remain limited [1]. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, have introduced new opportunities for automated analysis of medical images. AI-based systems can reduce clinician work-load, standardise diagnosis, and enable large-scale popu-lation screening [12]. Many high-performing models rely on complex architectures, such as deep CNNs, multi-branch networks, and transform-ers, that contain tens or hundreds of millions of parame-ters and require high-end GPUs for inference [5, 6, 8]. This ”heavyweight bias” in medical AI highlights the gap between academic innovation and clinical applica-bility, rendering such models impractical for resource-limited settings such as rural clinics or mobile screening units. This research is motivated by the need for eq-uitable access to healthcare technologies. For AI-driven ophthalmic screening to have a meaningful global impact, systems must balance diagnostic accuracy with efficiency, portability, and robustness. Lightweight models capable of on-device inference can reduce reliance on cloud in-frastructure, preserve patient privacy, and operate in ar-eas with inconsistent internet connectivity, particularly with emerging smartphone-based fundus cameras and portable devices. Despite promising advances, a signifi-cant scalability bottleneck limits the translation of oph-thalmic AI to clinical tools. Existing methods typically achieve high accuracy with computationally heavy mod-els unsuitable for resource-constrained environments, or prioritise efficiency but underperform on severely imbal-anced multi-label datasets such as ODIR-5K [9]. The multi-label nature of retinal diagnosis—where a single image may exhibit overlapping conditions—further com-pounds challenges, with public datasets showing bias to-ward common diseases and poor minority class perfor-mance. In response, this study addresses the central re-search question: Can a lightweight hybrid deep learn-ing architecture achieve reliable multi-label retinal dis-ease classification while maintaining low computational cost, enabling deployment in resource-constrained clini-cal environments? To this end, we introduce LightHy-bridNet, a novel lightweight hybrid deep learning frame-work that enhances eye disease classification from fun-dus images. Unlike prior methods, LightHybridNet in-corporates depthwise separable convolutions to focus on diagnostically relevant regions and compound scaling mechanisms to capture fine- and coarse-grained features, handling the subtlety and complexity of ocular pathol-ogy [16, 17]. These enhancements improve the model’s ability to handle high variability in fundus images, en-abling accurate and robust classification. To address in-terpretability, we integrate explainable AI methods such as Grad-CAM, which provide visual interpretations of decisions by highlighting key features [20]. Such trans-parency fosters trust in clinicians and yields actionable insights for diagnosis and treatment. This study examines eight primary ocular disease classes, along with a normal condition, from the ODIR-5K dataset, analysing their effects on vision and high-lighting the vital importance of early diagnosis and treat-ment, as illustrated in Figure 1 (representative examples, including frequent comorbid cases).

> II. RELATED WORK

Eye diseases are a major concern in healthcare due to their potential to cause significant visual impairment and impact overall quality of life. The optimal treat-ment and management of these conditions necessitated their expeditious identification and precise diagnosis. In this context, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models emerged as promising approaches to assist healthcare practitioners in accurately diagnosing ocular illnesses [39]. This section evaluated and analysed sev-eral studies on the subject, critically synthesising existing research to provide significant insights and context for future advances in automated eye disease identification. Recent studies have made impressive strides in using ML and DL for detecting eye diseases from images such as fundus images and optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans. Chowdhury et al. proposed OCCT, an optimised transformer-based model for classifying eye diseases us-ing optical coherence tomography images [33]. To ad-dress class imbalance, the study used GAN-based data augmentation, increasing the dataset to 130,649 images. OCCT, refined through an ablation study, outperformed Vision Transformer (ViT), Swin Transformer, and CNN models, including DenseNet, ResNet, and MobileNetV2, achieving 97.09% accuracy. Extensive image preprocess-ing (morphological erosion, median filtering, alpha-beta correction) enhanced quality. The model exhibited high accuracy despite limited training data, making it a ro-bust and scalable AI tool for retinal disease detection. Shoaib et al. presented the DiaGAN-CNN model, an advanced deep learning framework for diabetic retinopa-thy (DR) detection [34]. This model combined GAN-based data augmentation with transfer learning, utilis-ing InceptionResNet-v2 and Inception-v3 for feature ex-traction, to greatly improve classification accuracy. The study validated its method on the Ocular Disease Intel-ligent Recognition (ODIR) dataset, achieving 98% ac-curacy and precision in identifying DR and other eye disorders. The DiaGAN-CNN model surpassed existing approaches by accurately capturing subtle retinal prop-erties, enabling high-speed, high-precision diagnoses. Ma et al. [35] presented the Intelligent Eye Multi-modal Interactive Diagnostic System (IOMIDS), an AI-powered chatbot that used textual and image data for eye illness diagnosis and self-triage. In a two-stage cross-sectional research conducted across three medical centres, IOMIDS analysed 50 disorders using 15,640 data entries. Among its multimodal models, the text + smartphone model achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy (inter-nal: 79.6%, external: 81.1%), outperforming the others. Multimodal integration enhanced answer quality and re-duced disinformation, highlighting the use of AI Chat-bots in automated ophthalmic care. Malik et al. [36] introduced dual-branch semi-supervised learning for multi-class retinal disease classi-fication using Colour Fundus Photography (CFP). This approach integrated labelled and unlabelled data through self-training-based semi-supervised learning, improving diagnostic accuracy, especially in data-scarce environ-ments. The authors evaluated six CNN models on the OIH dataset (4217 images); ResNet50 achieved the highest accuracy (93.14%), with 93% sensitivity, 98.37% specificity, and an AUC of 97.75%. The study provided insight into the potential of semi-supervised learning in clinical decision support systems (CDSS), making AI-powered ophthalmic diagnostics more accessible and ef-fective. Kansal et al. [37] presented a DL-based system for automated ocular illness categorisation using the ODIR dataset (5,000 fundus images, 8 disease categories). The study employed transfer-learning models (DenseNet201, EfficientNetB3, and InceptionResNetV2) and proposed a two-level feature-selection method that combined Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) with neural-network clas-sifiers (DNN, LSTM, and BiLSTM). The BiLSTM clas-sifier achieved 100% accuracy on training data and over 98% accuracy on validation data, demonstrating high ef-ficiency and minimal processing complexity. The model provided a scalable, automated approach to clinical deci-sion support, reducing ophthalmologists’ workloads and enhancing early disease detection in resource-constrained environments. Multi-label fundus classification remained challenging due to overlapping disease features and severe class im-balance. Bhati et al. [13] proposed DKCNet, which em-ployed an Inception-ResNetV2 backbone augmented with dilated convolutions and squeeze-and-excitation (SE) blocks, achieving strong performance on ODIR-5K with a macro AUC of 0.9608. However, its high parameter count ( 87.7M) limited deployment feasibility. Alfahdawi et al. [15] introduced Fundus-DeepNet, which incorporated bilateral image fusion, thereby repli-cating clinical practice by combining information from the left and right eyes. Similarly, He et al. [14] pro-posed the Dense Correlation Network (DCNet), which used a backbone CNN to extract features from paired left and right eye colour fundus photographs, followed by a spatial correlation module to capture pixel-wise inter-eye correlations and refine features for patient-level fusion. Evaluated on the ODIR-5K dataset, DCNet achieved a mean AUC of 0.93 and a mean F1-score of 0.913, with significant improvements over baselines that lacked bi-lateral correlation modelling. This highlighted the value of exploiting inter-eye relationships for multi-label tasks, though DCNet’s ResNet-based backbone incurred higher computational costs compared to lightweight alterna-tives. 4Multimodal approaches further extended this paradigm by integrating clinical metadata with im-age features, demonstrating improved discrimination in complex cases. Recent studies have also explored ultra-wide-field imaging for multi-disease classification, though the computational demands remain high. Beyond detection, disease staging was critical for prognosis and treatment planning. Grassmann et al. [38] demonstrated that CNN ensembles could achieve human-level performance in AMD severity prediction on AREDS. Wu et al. [18] showed that ConvNeXT, a mod-ern CNN inspired by transformer design principles, out-performed EfficientNetB7 in AMD staging while main-taining superior efficiency. Attention-based Efficient-Net variants were also applied to glaucoma staging with promising results. Recent trends focused on attention mechanisms, hy-brid designs, and data-centric optimisation. ConvNeXT utilised large-kernel convolutions and depthwise separa-bility to capture global context efficiently [18]. Efficient-NetV2 incorporated compound scaling and channel at-tention to enhance feature learning. Data-centric strate-gies such as CLAHE preprocessing, aggressive data aug-mentation, and cost-sensitive learning proved essential for handling dataset imbalance in ODIR-5K. Heavyweight models were characterised by deep, com-plex architectures with tens of millions of parameters and high requirements for floating-point operations. These models often employed multi-branch designs, dense con-nectivity, attention mechanisms, or ensemble-style fea-ture fusion to maximise representational capacity. Ex-amples included Inception-ResNet variants, DenseNet-based hybrids, and large EfficientNet or transformer-based networks. While heavyweight architectures fre-quently achieved high performance in controlled exper-imental settings, they demanded substantial computa-tional resources, extended training time, and high mem-ory consumption, limiting their deployment in clinical environments with constrained hardware [13]. In contrast, lightweight models were designed with efficiency as a primary objective. They typically em-ployed depthwise separable convolutions, inverted resid-ual blocks, parameter sharing, and streamlined feature-extraction pipelines to minimise model size and compu-tation while incurring minimal performance loss. Archi-tectures such as MobileNetV3, EfficientNet-B0/B1, and MobileViT exemplified this category, achieving competi-tive accuracy with an order-of-magnitude fewer parame-ters and FLOPs [16][17]. This distinction was crucial in ophthalmic screening applications. Fundus-based diagnostic systems were in-creasingly deployed on portable cameras, mobile devices, and edge-computing platforms, particularly in low- and middle-income regions where access to ophthalmologists was limited [1]. In such contexts, heavyweight models were often impractical due to latency, energy consump-tion, and infrastructure requirements. Lightweight archi-tectures, by contrast, enabled real-time inference, lower power usage, and scalable tele-ophthalmology solutions. However, purely lightweight models sometimes strug-gled to capture the complex, multi-scale pathological patterns present in multi-label datasets such as ODIR-5K, especially under severe class imbalance. This mo-tivated recent research into lightweight hybrid archi-tectures, which combined complementary efficient back-bones to enhance representational capacity while main-taining deployability [18]. Despite their high accuracy, DL models were often crit-icised as “black boxes,” which limited clinical trust and adoption. XAI techniques such as Grad-CAM generated heatmaps that highlighted diagnostically relevant reti-nal regions, aligning predictions with ophthalmological landmarks [20][13]. Model-agnostic methods, including LIME and SHAP, further quantified feature importance and decision contributions. Interpretability was particularly critical for lightweight models deployed in real-world screening programs, where transparency, accountability, and clinician trust were es-sential. Heavyweight models achieved high diagnostic perfor-mance but were impractical for real-world deployment due to computational demands [13]. Lightweight mod-els were efficient but often underperformed on complex, imbalanced, multi-label datasets [16]. Key gaps included the lack of unified frameworks that simultaneously addressed multi-disease classifica-tion, severity staging, and model interpretability. Fur-thermore, XAI remained underutilised in lightweight ar-chitectures.

> IV. METHODOLOGY A. ODIR-5K Dataset

The ODIR-5K dataset used in this work is available online through a grand challenge hosted by Peking Uni-versity [9]. The dataset contains around 5000 organised fundus images of the left and right eyes of patients. These fundus images were captured using various fundus cam-eras, such as Kowa, Zeiss, and Canon, at different res-olutions. Disease diagnostic keywords were assigned to these images by eye specialists. Based on these diagnostic keywords, the disease classification labels are assigned to each pair of fundus images. This visual pathology dataset is unique among publicly accessible datasets, as it con-tains colour fundus images of both the left and right eyes of a patient, with single or multiple abnormalities per im-age. The dataset contains a common target label for the pair of eye images. The images are grouped into eight disease classes: Normal, Diabetes, Glaucoma, Cataract, AMD, Hypertension, Myopia, and Others. Patients’ age and gender are also included. Another challenging as-pect of the ODIR-5K dataset is that images from the other class contain lesions associated with 12 different 6ophthalmic diseases. It is not easy to learn appropriate features in such a case. Also, the dataset is highly im-balanced, given the number of images per class. These issues negatively affect accuracy and reduce the perfor-mance of the trained classification models. Although the ODIR-5K dataset is more applicable to real-life clinical situations, as the images are captured with different cam-eras under varying illumination conditions, this poses a significant challenge for any disease identification model. To ensure that only diagnostically reliable images were used, a quality-filtering step was applied after loading the metadata and mapping image paths. The filtering criteria were explicitly defined as follows: 

• Missing or corrupted image files (path does not ex-ist or file cannot be opened). 

• Presence of non-diagnostic quality-issue keywords in the original dataset metadata, specifically: “low-quality image”, “lens dust”, “optic disk photo-graphically invisible”, or “image offset”. A total of 324 images (≈3.2% of the initial set) were re-moved due to these conditions, resulting in a final dataset of 9,676 valid fundus images (approximately 4,838 left-eye and 4,838 right-eye images after pairing verifica-tion). This strict filtering minimises the risk of the model learning from imaging artefacts or noise. Regarding label assignment: The ODIR-5K dataset provides patient-level multi-label annotations (one 8-dimensional binary vector per patient), derived from di-agnostic keywords associated with both left and right eyes. In this work, left and right fundus images are treated as separate, independent inputs to the CNN. To enable per-image training while remaining consistent with the dataset’s design, the full patient-level label vec-tor is assigned identically to both the left-eye and right-eye images of the same patient. This approach is widely adopted in ODIR-5K literature because diseases can be bilateral (affecting both eyes) or unilateral (affecting one eye), and the patient-level label effectively represents the logical OR of any eye-specific findings. Thus, if a patient has diabetic retinopathy in one or both eyes, both im-ages receive a positive label for Diabetes (D=1). This per-image label propagation allows straightforward con-volutional processing while preserving approximate clin-ical consistency.  

> B. Pre-processing and Image Enhancement

The heterogeneous nature of the ODIR-5K dataset, stemming from various camera models and illumination conditions, necessitates rigorous pre-processing. Initially, a field-of-view (FOV) aware cropping operation is per-formed by identifying the first non-black pixels to define the image mask borders, effectively removing redundant background information. Images are then resized to a uniform 224 × 224 pixel resolution, a standard input di-mension for the MobileNetV3 and EfficientNet backbones [16, 17]. A critical step in our pipeline is the application of Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE) [30, 31]. Unlike global histogram equalisa-tion, which can over-amplify noise in homogeneous re-gions of fundus images, CLAHE operates on localised 8 × 8 tiles. To prevent the “noise-over-enhancement” common in medical imaging, we implement a clip limit (CL = 2 .0) that truncates the histogram bins. The max-imum height of any histogram bin M is defined as: 

M = CL × Npixels 

L (1) where Npixels represents the number of pixels per tile and L denotes the number of grey levels (typically 256). Any values exceeding M are clipped and uniformly redis-tributed across all bins before calculating the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) for intensity mapping. This technique is particularly effective for enhancing the visi-bility of fine vascular structures and subtle lesions such as microaneurysms and drusen while maintaining anatomi-cal fidelity. Finally, all images undergo ImageNet normalisa-tion (mean [0 .485 , 0.456 , 0.406] and standard deviation [0 .229 , 0.224 , 0.225]) to standardise the input distribution for the pre-trained backbones.  

> C. Data Augmentation Strategy

To improve model generalisation, simulate real-world acquisition variability, and mitigate overfit-ting—particularly to replicated minority-class sam-ples—we applied an extensive on-the-fly augmentation pipeline exclusively during training, using the Albumen-tations library [21]. The training augmentation pipeline consists of the fol-lowing stochastic transformations: 

• Geometric invariance: Horizontal flip ( p = 0 .5), vertical flip ( p = 0 .3), and random rotation up to 

±30 ◦ (p = 0 .5) to account for variations in camera orientation and patient positioning. 

• Illumination variability: Random brightness and contrast adjustments ( ±0.2, p = 0 .5) to simulate differences across imaging devices and clinical en-vironments. 

• Contrast enhancement: CLAHE with clip limit 

CL = 2 .0 ( p = 0 .5) to further improve vessel and lesion visibility without excessive noise amplifica-tion. 

• Mild geometric distortions: Shift-scale-rotate (shift limit = 0 .1, scale limit = 0 .1, rotate limit = 15 ◦,

p = 0 .6) to mimic varying patient-to-camera dis-tances. 7

• Regularisation: Coarse dropout (max holes = 8, max height/width = 16 pixels, p = 0 .5) to ran-domly mask contiguous regions, encouraging the model to rely on global context rather than lo-calised artefacts [32]. As illustrated in Figure 2, these transformations generate clinically plausible variations while preserving anatomi-cal integrity. Validation and test images undergo only deterministic pre-processing (cropping, resizing, and nor-malisation) to ensure unbiased evaluation under realistic conditions. Prior studies on retinal benchmarks, includ-ing ODIR-5K, have shown that aggressive augmentation strategies of this nature can yield 5–10% improvements in AUC.

> D. Class Balancing

The left and right fundus images are treated individu-ally as input to the CNN architecture in this work. Based on the disease description for a particular eye part, a dis-ease label is assigned to each image. A fundus image having multiple diseases is treated separately with indi-vidual disease class labels. The images with artefacts like “low-quality image”, “optical disk photographically invisible,” “lens dust,” and “image offset” are removed from the final dataset to reduce the false recognition rate. After separating left and right eye images with their cor-responding ground truth, the class-wise distribution is shown in Table I. It is observed that the normal, dia-betes, and other classes have significantly more images than the other disease classes. Replication-based oversampling is used in this work to address the class-balance issue without introducing syn-thetic artefacts [19]. Minority-class samples are dupli-cated to approximately match the majority-class count, thereby expanding the training set while preserving anatomical fidelity. Aggressive on-the-fly augmentation (flips, rotations, brightness/contrast, CLAHE, coarse dropout) prevents overfitting to duplicates. We chose replication-based oversampling over synthetic methods such as SMOTE or GANs to avoid generating anatom-ically unrealistic samples that could mislead the model in medical imaging contexts. > E. Overview of the Proposed LightHybridNet Architecture

As shown in Fig. 3, LightHybridNet comprises par-allel MobileNetV3-Large and EfficientNet-B1 backbones [16, 17], global average pooling, feature concatenation, dropout regularisation, and a lightweight fully connected sigmoid head. The backbone networks extract comple-mentary features: MobileNetV3 excels at local texture and fine-grained lesion detection via depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residuals, while EfficientNet-B1 captures multi-scale global context through com-pound scaling and MBConv blocks. The resulting 1280-dimensional features from each backbone are concate-nated to form a 2560-dimensional representation, fol-lowed by dropout (p=0.5) and mapping to 8 sigmoid outputs for multi-label classification. This late fusion strategy preserves independent hierarchies while achiev-ing only 10.74M parameters and 0.853 GFLOPs. Late feature fusion was selected over early fusion to allow each backbone to independently develop specialised represen-tations before integration, reducing computational over-head during training and inference. Attention mecha-nisms were excluded to maintain low parameter count and FLOPs, prioritising edge deployment feasibility over marginal accuracy gains.

> 2. EfficientNet-B1 (efficientnet b1)

The EfficientNet-B1 backbone is created via timm.create model(’efficientnet b1’, pretrained=True, num classes=0) . EfficientNet-B1 is part of the EfficientNet family, which employs compound scaling to balance network depth, width, and resolution for optimal performance-efficiency trade-off. The ’B1’ variant is a baseline model with moderate size, pretrained on ImageNet-1k (’in1k’ implied). Setting 

num classes=0 configures it as a feature extractor, producing 1280-dimensional features. EfficientNet-B1 excels at multi-scale feature capture through its MB-Conv blocks (mobile inverted bottleneck convolutions) with squeeze-and-excitation optimisation and swish activations. This makes it adept at modelling global pathological structures in retinal images, such as optic disc cupping in glaucoma or macular changes in AMD [17]. The fine-tuning from the original TensorFlow weights ensures compatibility with PyTorch while preserving high transfer-learning efficacy.

> V. RESULTS A. Experimental Setup

All experiments are conducted on the preprocessed ODIR-5K dataset with a patient-level stratified split (80% training, 20% validation), ensuring that left- and right-eye images from the same patient are not sepa-rated between training and validation sets to prevent data leakage cite odir2019. After quality filtering and left/right eye separation, the training set contains approximately 9,968 images. Replication-based over-sampling expands minority classes to match the ma-jority class count ( ∼3,300), resulting in ∼24,933 train-ing samples. Extensive on-the-fly augmentation (hori-zontal/vertical flips, rotations ±30 ◦, brightness/contrast 

±0.2, CLAHE clip limit 2.0, coarse dropout) is applied to prevent overfitting to duplicated samples [19, 32]. An 80/20 patient-level stratified split was chosen to balance computational efficiency, stable evaluation of the minority class, and sufficient data for model development on the moderately sized, severely imbalanced ODIR-5K dataset. While 5-fold cross-validation or a separate held-out test set could provide even more robust estimates, the current configuration ensures no leakage between fellow eyes and allocates adequate validation samples ( ∼20%, 

∼2,500 images) for hyperparameter tuning and perfor-mance monitoring. This split is widely adopted in prior ODIR-5K studies due to the dataset’s size and imbal-ance, where smaller folds risk unstable metrics for rare classes. All final reported results (weighted F1-score, macro AUC, Cohen’s Kappa) are computed on the held-out validation portion to provide a realistic estimate of generalisation performance. Future work may explore full k-fold CV or external test sets for additional valida-tion. LightHybridNet is trained for 25 epochs using Bi-nary Cross-Entropy with Logits loss, AdamW optimiser (learning rate 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 1 × 10 −2) [27], and OneCycleLR scheduler [28]. Mixed-precision train-ing (FP16) is used on NVIDIA GPUs [22]. Evaluation metrics include weighted F1-score, macro AUC, Cohen’s Kappa [25, 26], per-class precision/recall/F1, and ef-ficiency measures (parameters, GFLOPs, inference la-tency). Ablation studies compare imbalance strategies and backbone combinations. The proposed model is bench-marked against heavyweight baselines (DKCNet [13], 10 DCNet [14]) and lightweight alternatives.

> G. Efficiency and Inference Speed

Inference latency: 12 ms (high-end GPU), 45 ms (CPU), 25 ms (mobile emulation via TensorFlow Lite). These speeds enable real-time screening on portable de-vices, unlike heavyweight models requiring seconds on non-GPU hardware.  

> H. Efficiency and Edge Deployment Feasibility

LightHybridNet is explicitly designed for edge deploy-ment on resource-constrained devices such as portable fundus cameras and mid-range smartphones. Key effi-ciency metrics are as follows: 

• Model size (memory footprint) : 10.74 million parameters, corresponding to approximately 41.0 MB in FP32 precision (single-precision floating-point format, unquantized). 

• CPU inference speed : ∼81 ms per image (mea-sured on a standard CPU environment equivalent to Intel i7-class processors in virtualised settings; real edge hardware may achieve faster times with optimisations). 

• Power/energy estimates : On mid-range mo-bile/embedded hardware (e.g., Snapdragon 8-series or similar with INT8 quantisation via TensorFlow Lite or ONNX Runtime), inference is expected to consume < 0.8–1.0 W per image (based on typical mobile CNN benchmarks for models of similar size and FLOPs). Full FP32 inference on a CPU may briefly reach 2–4 W. These characteristics enable real-time or near-real-time screening ( ∼12–15 images per minute on CPU-only de-vices) without requiring cloud connectivity or high-end GPUs. Post-training quantization (INT8) and model pruning can further reduce size to ∼10–15 MB and in-ference time to ∼20–40 ms on mobile hardware, making LightHybridNet highly suitable for tele-ophthalmology in low-resource settings.

> VI. CONCLUSION

This work presents LightHybridNet, a lightweight hybrid deep learning framework that effectively ad-dresses the challenges of multi-label ophthalmic dis-ease classification on the severely imbalanced ODIR-5K dataset [9]. By fusing complementary efficient backbones (MobileNetV3-Large and EfficientNet-B1) with late fea-ture concatenation [16, 17], the model achieves clinical-grade performance (weighted F1-score 0.9151, macro AUC 0.9826, Cohen’s Kappa 0.9014) using only 10.74M parameters and 0.853 GFLOPs—representing dramatic efficiency gains over heavyweight baselines while match-ing or exceeding balanced diagnostic accuracy [13, 14]. Replication-based oversampling with aggressive augmen-tation proved superior for handling severe class imbal-ance without synthetic artefacts or information loss [19]. This data-centric approach substantially improved re-call for clinically critical minority conditions (e.g., Hy-pertension recall 0.8293 despite only 138 original sam-ples), ensuring equitable performance across disease cat-egories. Grad-CAM visualisations confirmed clinically aligned attention on relevant anatomical regions (e.g., op-tic disc for glaucoma, macula for AMD, vasculature for diabetes/hypertension), enhancing interpretability and clinician trust [20]. Compared to established benchmarks, LightHybridNet demonstrates competitive or superior balanced metrics at a fraction of the computational cost. While DKCNet achieves a slightly higher weighted F1-score ( 0.943), it uses 87.7M parameters and incurs higher FLOPs [13]. DCNet reports comparable F1 ( 0.913) but relies on a heavier ResNet backbone [14]. LightHybridNet’s supe-rior macro AUC and Kappa highlight better minority-class discrimination, a critical advantage in imbalanced multi-label settings [25]. Misclassification analysis revealed errors primarily in subtle co-morbidities or early-stage changes, where dom-inant pathology overshadowed minority labels—a com-mon challenge in ODIR-5K [13]. Zero-shot evaluation on the independent MESSIDOR-2 dataset [29] yielded an encouraging AUC of 0.8564 for referable diabetic retinopathy without fine-tuning, indicating transferable feature learning despite domain shift (demographics, cameras, label granularity). These findings have substantial clinical and practical im-plications. With inference latencies of 12 ms (GPU), 45 ms (CPU), and 25 ms (mobile emulation), LightHy-bridNet enables real-time screening on portable fun-dus cameras and smartphones, particularly in resource-constrained settings [1]. On-device processing reduces la-tency, preserves privacy, and supports offline operation, contributing to equitable global eye health. Despite these strengths, limitations include reliance on a single primary dataset (ODIR-5K), potential demo-graphic/imaging biases, and challenges with ambigu-ous “Others” labels or overlapping pathologies. A no-table limitation of this study is the potential for demo-graphic bias and limited generalizability arising from the ODIR-5K dataset. The dataset was collected exclusively from patients at hospitals in China, predominantly rep-resenting East Asian populations. Retinal appearance (e.g., pigmentation, vessel visibility, drusen character-istics) and disease prevalence patterns can vary signifi-cantly across ethnic groups due to differences in skin tone, iris pigmentation, genetic predispositions, and environ-mental factors [1, 10]. Consequently, model performance observed on ODIR-5K may not fully translate to other populations, such as Caucasian, African, South Asian, or Hispanic cohorts, where fundus features and comorbid-14 ity distributions differ. This introduces a risk of reduced sensitivity or specificity for underrepresented groups, po-tentially exacerbating health inequities in global deploy-ment. While the model’s lightweight design and strong test-set performance on the source domain are encourag-ing, rigorous external validation on diverse, multi-ethnic datasets (e.g., EyePACS, APTOS, or RFMiD) is essential before broad clinical use. Future work should incorporate fairness-aware training techniques, such as domain adap-tation, adversarial debiasing, or balanced multi-source pretraining, to improve robustness across demographic groups. Severity grading and physical device testing were beyond scope. This study makes several original contributions: the development of a novel lightweight hybrid architec-ture achieving strong efficiency-adjusted performance on multi-label ODIR-5K; empirical validation that replication-based oversampling outperforms alternatives for imbalanced ophthalmic classification; integration of clinically aligned Grad-CAM for enhanced interpretabil-ity; and a deployment-oriented design bridging bench-mark research and real-world screening. Future work should prioritise rigorous external vali-dation on diverse benchmarks (e.g., EyePACS, AP-TOS, RFMiD), extension to disease severity grading via multi-task/ordinal learning, multimodal fusion in-corporating patient metadata, OCT, or visual fields, quantisation-aware training and pruning for further compression on microcontrollers, federated learning for privacy-preserving generalisation across institutions, and prospective clinical trials measuring diagnostic impact, clinician acceptance, and patient outcomes in under-served communities. In conclusion, LightHybridNet demonstrates that pur-posefully designed lightweight hybrid architectures, com-bined with data-centric optimisation, can deliver reliable, interpretable, and deployable multi-label ocular disease recognition. This advances equitable ophthalmic AI, of-fering a scalable solution to preventable vision loss world-wide [1, 2].
