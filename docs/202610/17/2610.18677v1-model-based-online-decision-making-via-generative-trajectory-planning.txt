## Abstract 

Online decision-making faces a tension between efficient inference and long-horizon reasoning. Value-based methods rely on short-horizon bootstrapping that limits the propagation of long-term information, while trajectory optimization methods plan explicitly but must solve a high-dimensional optimization problem at every decision step. We propose Generative Trajectory Planning (GTP), a model-based framework that addresses this tension by learning a generative model as a proposal distribution over action sequences. Instead of optimizing sequences from scratch, GTP samples candidate sequences from a diffusion prior and refines them using learned dynamics, reward, and value models, enabling efficient trajectory-level reasoning. Across continuous control and long-horizon manipulation tasks, GTP matches or exceeds state-of-the-art model-free, diffusion-policy, and model-based baselines, and remains stable in regimes where strong baselines degrade. Our results suggest that generative models are best understood not as policies, but as structured proposal distributions for planning, offering a new perspective on how to integrate generative modeling with model-based decision-making. 

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as the dominant framework for online decision-making [33, 35], with strong empirical success across domains such as robotics and continuous control [14, 19– 21]. At its core, RL compresses future outcomes into a scalar value function and optimizes single-step policies against this objective [34]. While effective when reward signals are informative, this formulation exhibits structural limitations in long-horizon settings: reliance on short-horizon bootstrapping slows the propagation of long-term information and accumulates estimation errors [24, 32], while exploration driven by stochasticity at the level of individual actions [14, 21] limits the discovery of coherent behaviors. Model-based RL aims to mitigate these issues by incorporating learned predictive models into policy and value learning [9, 15, 17, 31]. In practice, however, these models are typically used indirectly, to shape value targets or generate synthetic data, rather than to enable explicit reasoning over a long-horizon. As a result, decision making remains driven by local updates, and the core limitations of short-horizon bootstrapping and action-level exploration persist. In contrast, trajectory optimization (TO) explicitly optimizes action sequences, allowing long-horizon planning without relying on bootstrapped value functions [30, 40]. However, optimizing high-dimensional, non-convex objectives at every step is computationally expensive and susceptible to local minima. Moreover, in online settings, planning relies on learned dynamics [8, 11], where prediction errors accumulate over long horizons and can be exploited by the optimizer [17, 36]. As a result, TO occupies the opposite end of the design spectrum from RL, as it enables explicit trajectory-level reasoning but is computationally intensive and degrades under model error.

A promising direction from generative modeling reframes planning as conditional trajectory gen-eration [1, 3, 6, 18]: models learn distributions over high-return trajectories and sample candidates directly, achieving strong offline performance with large datasets [1, 18]. In online settings, however, progress is limited as prior work largely treats generative models as policy replacements trained with standard RL objectives [23, 26, 38, 41], inheriting their shortcomings. As a result, the long-horizon and expressive benefits of generative planning remain underutilized online. Taken together, these observations suggest that online decision making lacks a framework that combines efficient inference with explicit trajectory-level reasoning. We address this gap by casting decision making as generative inference, where a learned generative model defines a proposal distribution over plausible action sequences. At each timestep, candidates are sampled from this distribution rather than optimized from scratch. These candidates are then refined using learned value and dynamics models, amortizing long-horizon reasoning into the generative model. To enable effective exploration, we characterize trajectory-level uncertainty using ensemble dynamics and act optimistically upon these estimates. The resulting framework GTP offers, a unified approach to integrate generative modeling with model-based decision making. In summary, our contributions are (1) we propose Generative Trajectory Planning (GTP), a model-based online decision-making framework that uses a generative model as a proposal distribution over action sequences, enabling trajectory-level reasoning without per-step optimization; (2) we develop an iterative planning procedure that interleaves beam search with diffusion-based refinement, progressively improving sampled action sequences under the learned prior and dynamics; (3) we show that GTP outperforms classical RL, diffusion-policy, and model-based RL baselines on loco-motion benchmarks, improves sample efficiency on long-horizon manipulation, and scales to visual manipulation through latent representations, where standard RL methods fail to make progress.

Even with accurate models, the planner’s proposal remains a bottleneck: simple parametric families (e.g., Gaussians) cannot capture the multimodal, temporally correlated structure of high-return action sequences. We replace this with a learned generative model that concentrates on high-return trajectories and is reused across steps, amortizing planning. At decision time, candidates are sampled from this prior and refined using fϕ, rξ , and Vψ . We instantiate the prior with a diffusion model, enabling flexible modeling of high-dimensional, multimodal action distributions [6, 18].

Section 3.1 described planning with fixed components. We now consider the online setting, where they are learned from data collected by the planner. We outline resulting challenges and the design choices that address them; the full loop is summarized in Algorithm 2. 

Mode collapse during prior training. Training our action prior in a sample-and-imitate loop is prone to collapse: as pθ sharpens, the elite set drawn from its own samples differs little from what it already produces, so retraining reinforces the existing distribution rather than improving it. To inject novel high-scoring candidates, we perform a few steps of Langevin ascent on JH initialized from the planner’s elites, perturbing them along the planning objective (cf. Appendix C). 

Exploration under learned dynamics. Unlike uncoordinated exploration through action noise, GTP leverages its dynamics ensemble to guide exploration over entire trajectories. Each ensemble member fϕi independently evaluates candidate sequences, with disagreement among predicted returns 

{J(i) 

> h

}Ni=1 capturing trajectory-level uncertainty. When acting, we use max aggregation in Eq. 4, favoring trajectories predicted to achieve high returns under at least one ensemble member. This induces optimism under uncertainty and encourages structured, temporally coherent exploration. 

Slow value propagation in long horizons. Standard temporal-difference learning propagates rewards slowly over long horizons. Instead, we leverage multi-step return estimates from beam search, weighting different horizons by inverse ensemble variance to balance short-horizon bias against long-horizon uncertainty. We use median aggregation across ensemble members for robust value targets, in contrast to the optimistic max aggregation used for exploration. This allows GTP to learn from reliable trajectory-level estimates while encouraging exploration through optimistic planning (Sec. 3.3). 

Computational efficiency. Although GTP incurs additional computational overhead from planning, this cost can be substantially reduced during both training and inference (cf. Appendix I). During training, reusing planner-generated elite trajectories across multiple gradient updates reduces update costs without sacrificing performance (cf. Appendix J). At inference, the learned generative prior can generate actions without explicit planning, while executing multiple actions between replanning steps further reduces computational overhead (cf. Appendix K). These strategies enable more efficient training and deployment with minimal performance degradation.

## 4 Experiments 

In this section, we present experiments designed to answer three questions. (Q1) Does combining a diffusion action prior with sample-based planning yield competitive performance on standard continuous-control benchmarks? (Q2) Does the proposed approach scale to long-horizon manipula-tion and high-dimensional image observations? (Q3) Which design choices drive GTP’s performance, and how does its exploration behavior differ from existing methods? We address (Q1) on MuJoCo locomotion (Section 4.1); (Q2) on three state-based manipulation tasks (Fetch) and a vision-based Push-T task (Section 4.2), where the latter demonstrates scalability to high-dimensional inputs; and (Q3) through component ablations, a study of the planning horizon, and an analysis of exploration behavior (Section 4.3). Additionally, we discuss a failure case and its remedy in Appendix D, provide a sensitivity analysis of beam search parameters in Appendix E, and analyze computational costs, efficien training, and efficient inference in Appendices I, J, and K, respectively. All methods are implemented in JAX, with hyperparameters detailed in Appendix B.1. 

Baselines. We compare GTP against baselines spanning four families. Model-free RL: SAC [14] and TD3 [13], two strong off-policy actor-critic methods. Diffusion policy RL: QSM [26] and DPMD [23], which train diffusion policies with RL objectives but act without planning at inference. Trajectory optimization: CEM-MPC and its value-augmented variant CEM-MPC-V, which adds a learned terminal value to the planning objective. Rather than training separate models, both variants reuse GTP’s learned dynamics and (for CEM-MPC-V) value function, and replace the diffusion prior with Gaussian CEM sampling, in similar spirit to [8]. This isolates the contribution of the diffusion prior while holding the rest of the planning stack fixed. Model-based RL: DreamerV3 [15] and TD-MPC2 [16], the current state of the art in model-based RL. All baselines are run under the same evaluation protocol; we detail each implementation, its source, and any modifications in Appendix G.

4.3 Analysis 

Having shown that GTP outperforms strong baselines across our benchmarks, we now examine what drives these gains, ablating its main components, studying the effect of the planning horizon, and analyzing how long-horizon planning shapes exploration during training. 

Candidate-set augmentation. Removing the Langevin augmentation ( No Aug. ) causes mode collapse: with no mechanism to inject novel candidates into the elite set, the prior concentrates on its current mode and the regression target degenerates into a fixed point. Replacing Langevin with the isotropic Gaussian perturbation used in prior online diffusion RL methods [23, 38] ( Gauss. Aug. ) recovers much of the lost performance but still trails GTP, consistent with our arguments in Appendix C that uninformed per-timestep noise scales poorly with horizon. 

Planning and value learning. Replacing trajectory-level value targets with single-step TD boot-strapping ( TD-1 ) yields lower final returns due to having slow long-horizon information propagation that motivated trajectory-level targets in the first place. Removing iterative beam search in favor of a best-of-BD filter over a single batch of samples ( No Plan. ) drops performance substantially, showing that filtering is not a substitute for our refinement procedure in Alg. 1. 

Ensemble and aggregation. Collapsing the dynamics ensemble to a single model ( No Ensemble ) is catastrophic, removing the uncertainty signal the rest of the planner relies on. Switching the update aggregation away from the conservative scheme ( No cons. update ) destabilizes value learning: without down-weighting ensemble disagreement, optimistic predictions from individual members leak into the value targets and amplify model bias. Switching the rollout aggregation away from the optimistic scheme ( No opt. rollout ) is more subtle: final return is competitive with GTP, but the training curve in Fig. 5 sits below GTP throughout most of training, indicating that the conservative rollout sacrifices sample efficiency by suppressing exploration of high-uncertainty, potentially high-return sequences. 

Planning horizon. We sweep H ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 , 16 } on HalfCheetah and FetchSlide, training a separate model at each horizon (Fig. 7). Increasing the horizon up to H = 4 yields clear gains on both tasks, confirming the benefit of trajectory-level planning. Beyond H = 4 , behavior diverges: 9on HalfCheetah, performance degrades as the horizon grows, which we attribute to the difficulty of accurately rolling out learned dynamics in a contact-rich locomotion task; on FetchSlide, performance plateaus rather than degrades, indicating that longer horizons are tolerable when dynamics are simpler. GTP trains stably even at H = 16 on manipulation tasks, suggesting that the limiting factor at long horizons is dynamics-model accuracy rather than training stability. We hypothesize that an additional benefit of longer horizons is improved exploration. By optimizing entire future trajectories, the planner can reason about delayed consequences of actions and guide data collection toward states that are informative for eventual task completion. 

Exploration analysis. To visualize the exploration behavior induced by different learning algorithms, we analyze the distribution of visited states throughout training on PushT . At several checkpoints, we collect 2000 evaluation rollouts and record the agent end-effector positions. To isolate exploration relative to the object rather than its absolute location, we transform all states into an object-centric coordinate frame by translating the T-shaped block to the center of the workspace. Figure 6 then shows a heatmap of the resulting end-effector visitation distribution over the course of training. The visitation patterns highlight the importance of temporally coherent exploration. SAC and DPMD rarely interact with the object, while TD-MPC2 explores broadly but indiscriminately despite using model-based planning. This suggests that planning over policy rollouts with Gaussian perturbations is insufficient to discover coordinated behaviors, as independently perturbed actions fail to capture temporal dependencies. In contrast, GTP jointly models action sequences, enabling structured exploration that initially discovers useful interactions and progressively concentrates around the object’s contour and key contact regions. This demonstrates how generative trajectory priors can guide exploration toward task-relevant behaviors rather than merely increasing state coverage. These findings answer (Q3): each design choice contributes to GTP’s performance, with the ex-ploration analysis highlighting its ability to discover directed, task-relevant behaviors rather than diffuse state-space coverage. More broadly, our experiments demonstrate that combining expressive generative trajectory priors with model-based planning yields strong sample efficiency and final performance across diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks. Together, these results support our central hypothesis that generative models can serve as effective proposal distributions for planning, enabling structured exploration and long-horizon reasoning while improving online decision making beyond reactive policies and conventional trajectory optimization. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced GTP, a model-based framework for online decision making that uses a learned generative prior to propose trajectories for planning. By combining diffusion-based trajectory generation with model-based evaluation and iterative refinement, GTP enables explicit trajectory-level reasoning without optimizing action sequences from scratch at every step. Crucially, planning provides a learning signal, as trajectories discovered through search are distilled into the generative prior, allowing the agent to improve its proposals through interaction. Across continuous control and manipulation benchmarks, GTP achieves strong sample efficiency and performance. Our ablations highlight the importance of iterative refinement, trajectory-level value learning, and structured exploration. Together, these findings highlight the importance of combining expressive generative priors with model-based planning to enable effective long-horizon reasoning and online learning. Our results suggest several directions for future work. First, reducing reliance on terminal value functions could enable more flexible long-horizon planning, particularly in settings with sparse rewards or unreliable value estimates. Developing alternative approaches to evaluating long-term outcomes could allow GTP to plan over longer horizons without depending heavily on accurate value predictions. Second, extending GTP to more complex tasks with rich visual observations presents an interesting direction for future research. Finally, developing more efficient and effective planning algorithms could improve the quality and diversity of generated trajectories while reducing computational overhead, allowing greater use of inference-time computation to discover behaviors beyond those represented by the current generative prior.

## A Related Work 

Reinforcement learning. Reinforcement learning has achieved strong empirical success in con-tinuous control through both model-free and model-based approaches. Model-free methods such as SAC [14] and TD3 [13] learn reactive policies by optimizing value functions, but rely on short-horizon bootstrapping, which can limit long-horizon reasoning. Model-based methods aim to address this limitation by learning predictive models of the environment and incorporating them into decision making. Early approaches such as PETS [8] combine learned dynamics with sampling-based plan-ning, enabling explicit reasoning over action sequences but requiring optimization from scratch at each step. More recent methods integrate model learning with value estimation and policy optimiza-tion. DreamerV3 [15] learns a latent world model and trains policies and value functions entirely in imagination, using model rollouts to improve sample efficiency. TD-MPC2 [16] combines learned latent dynamics with short-horizon planning and value bootstrapping, achieving strong performance by tightly coupling model predictive control with reinforcement learning. Despite these advances, decision making in these methods remains largely policy-centric. Actions are selected step-by-step using learned value functions or short-horizon planning with bootstrapping, rather than reasoning over entire trajectories. Model-based rollouts interleave dynamics predictions with policy or value evaluations at each step, which limits the ability to capture coherent, trajectory-level structure. Explo-ration is likewise driven by local perturbations, typically Gaussian noise in action space, rather than structured variation over action sequences. Our approach addresses these limitations by shifting from step-wise decision making to trajectory-level reasoning through our learned generative prior. 

Diffusion models for online decision-making. A growing body of work uses diffusion models as the policy class for online RL. Psenka et al. [26] dispense with an explicit policy network entirely and sample actions by running Langevin dynamics on the gradient of a learned critic, treating the Q-function as an unnormalized log-density over actions. While elegant, this approach inherits the variance and conditioning issues of critic gradients and offers no learned proposal to amortize sampling cost. Wang et al. [38] instead train a dedicated diffusion policy and update it by backpropagating through the full denoising chain; this enables expressive multimodal action distributions but is computationally expensive and, in their reported results, fails to translate the added capacity into reliable performance gains. More recent methods [10, 23] sidestep this bottleneck by training the diffusion policy with denoising score-matching objectives derived from the critic, decoupling policy learning from the sampling chain and yielding strong results on standard continuous-control benchmarks. Despite their use of diffusion, these methods retain the structure of standard off-policy actor-critic algorithms: the policy is updated to follow the critic, and exploration is left to the environment-step noise injected using Gaussian perturbations. There is no explicit mechanism for proposing trajectories the current policy is unlikely to generate, no use of the model class to plan over multiple timesteps, and no objective-aware perturbation of the proposal distribution. As a consequence, the practical benefit of using a diffusion policy reduces to a more expressive single-step action distribution, leaving the broader generative capacity of diffusion largely unexploited. Our work targets exactly this gap by treating the diffusion model as a learned proposal over action sequences , refined online with planning through learned models. 14 Diffusion models for offline decision-making and imitation. A parallel line of work uses diffusion models to generate trajectories rather than single actions, exploiting the multi-step structure of control. Janner et al. [18] introduce Diffuser, which trains an unconditional diffusion model over state-action sequences offline and steers sampling at inference time with classifier-style guidance from a learned reward. Ajay et al. [1] refine this idea with classifier-free conditioning, showing that returns, goals, and constraints can be incorporated directly into the generative model and that the resulting planner is competitive with strong offline RL baselines. Chi et al. [6] demonstrate that diffusion policies trained over short action chunks dramatically outperform Gaussian and energy-based alternatives on real-world manipulation, and that the gains come precisely from the model’s ability to produce temporally coherent multi-step actions. Collectively, these results establish that diffusion is a strong fit for sequence-level decision making when supervision is plentiful, whether through offline datasets or expert demonstrations. Our setting differs in that supervision is generated online from the planner itself, but the same property, that diffusion models excel at producing coherent action sequences, is what motivates our use of a sequence-level prior over the single-action policies common in online decision-making.

## C Augmenting Planned Trajectories via Langevin Dynamics 

Motivation. Training the diffusion prior pθ follows a sample-and-imitate loop: at each update step, we draw candidate action sequences from the current model, score them under the planning objective 

JH , and regress pθ onto the elite set reweighted by their scores. This loop has a well-known failure mode. As pθ sharpens, its samples concentrate around a small region of trajectory space; the elite set drawn from these samples then differs little from what the model already produces, the regression target collapses onto the current mode, and learning stalls. Avoiding collapse requires a mechanism that periodically injects novel candidates into the elite set, i.e. trajectories the prior is unlikely to have generated on its own but that nonetheless score well under JH .A common remedy when training diffusion policies is to perturb sampled actions with isotropic Gaussian noise [10, 23, 26]. We find this unsatisfying for two reasons. First, it is unprincipled: the perturbation direction is uninformed by the objective, so most of the injected variance lands on low-value trajectories and are discarded, wasting sample budget. Second, our setting amplifies this weakness. We are learning a generator over action sequences rather than single actions, so independent Gaussian noise across timesteps produces temporally incoherent perturbations that bear little resemblance to plausible trajectories. Worse still, we explicitly want pθ to retain enough stochasticity to support beam search at deployment: a near-deterministic prior would defeat the purpose of using a sampling-based planner, so the perturbation mechanism should not encourage the prior to memorize a single mode. We therefore augment the sampled trajectories with a few Langevin steps [29] obtained by ascending 

JH . Because the perturbation follows the gradient of the objective itself, novel candidates are drawn toward regions of trajectory space that are simultaneously distinct from the current prior and 

high-scoring under the model which are the two properties we desire.

## D Aggregation Sensitivity and Failure Modes 

In this section, we study the impact of ensemble aggregation in termination-sensitive environments and show that a more conservative, risk-sensitive scheme substantially improves performance. We observe that the choice of aggregation across dynamics models plays a critical role in environments with early termination, such as Ant-v4 . In these settings, our default configuration (median aggre-gation during training updates and max aggregation during rollout evaluation) leads to degraded performance. This effect arises from the interaction between model bias and termination sensitivity: small prediction errors compound over the planning horizon and quickly produce unstable states that trigger termination. Under max aggregation, the planner favors trajectories that appear high-return under at least one ensemble member, even when they are not consistently supported across the ensemble, yielding brittle plans that fail under slight model mismatch. To address this, we adopt a more pessimistic aggregation scheme, using minimum aggregation during updates and median aggregation during rollout evaluation. This reduces sensitivity to overestimated returns from individual models and biases planning toward trajectories that are consistently predicted to perform well across the ensemble, leading to significantly improved stability and return. We report wall-clock timing for action selection and gradient updates to characterize the computational profile of GTP relative to representative baselines. Two costs are relevant in online decision making: the per-step inference cost incurred during environment interaction and the per-update cost incurred during training. GTP introduces additional computation through diffusion-based trajectory generation and beam search, which we quantify below. We subsequently investigate strategies for reducing these costs in Appendices J and K. 

Setup. All measurements were taken on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU (32 GB), using our JAX implementation. Times are averaged over 1,000 steps after a warm-up phase to exclude compilation overhead. Each baseline was run under a matched batch size of 256 and identical hardware. 

Action selection. ## J Efficient Training via Trajectory Reuse 

A significant computational overhead in GTP arises from generating elite trajectories through beam search at every gradient update (cf. Alg. 2). However, generating fresh elites at every iteration is not necessary, as previously optimized trajectories can be reused across multiple updates. To reduce this overhead, we introduce a trajectory reuse variant that caches planner-generated elite trajectories and reuses them for subsequent updates, following a similar approach to Wang et al. [39]. Specifically, we refresh the cached elites once every 10 gradient updates instead of regenerating them at every iteration. This reduces the frequency of elite trajectory generation by a factor of 10 while preserving the same training objective. 

Learning performance. Figure 10 compares the learning curves and final success rates of the original online training procedure and its trajectory reuse variant. Trajectory reuse achieves compa-rable performance across all four manipulation tasks, suggesting that frequent regeneration of elite trajectories is not essential for effective learning. 

Computational savings. Trajectory reuse reduces the amortized update time of GTP ( H = 8 )from 157.15 ms to 19.12 ms, corresponding to an 8.2× speedup. These results demonstrate that reusing planner-generated trajectories substantially reduces the computational cost of GTP without compromising its learning performance. 

## K Efficient Inference 

Although GTP uses iterative beam search during training, the learned generative prior can support efficient action selection at deployment. We investigate two approaches for reducing inference-time computation: removing explicit planning and executing multiple actions between replanning steps. 

Planning-free inference. Since GTP trains its generative prior on planner-discovered trajectories, the learned prior can generate high-quality actions without explicit trajectory optimization. To evaluate this property, we vary the number of candidate trajectories at inference on FetchPick&Place, including a single-candidate variant without beam search. As shown in Table 7, removing inference-time planning reduces action selection time from 34.25 ms to 8.61 ms, a 4.0× speedup, while maintaining comparable success rates. This demonstrates that planning during training can be amortized into the generative prior, enabling efficient deployment without repeated trajectory optimization. 

Multi-step execution. In our default implementation, GTP follows receding-horizon MPC and replans after every environment step. However, the generated action sequences can also be executed for multiple steps before replanning, reducing the frequency of expensive planning operations. These results suggest that GTP’s generated trajectories remain effective even when executed partially open-loop. L.3 Relation to GTP 

The preceding analysis considers an idealized self-imitation process, whereas GTP incorporates several additional components. 

Beam search. The convergence theorem assumes that trajectories are sampled directly from the current prior. In practice, GTP instead generates improved trajectory proposals through beam search. Let e denote the distribution of trajectories produced by the planner. Under exact distillation, the updated prior satisfies 

p+(a) ∝ e(a) exp( J(a)/λ ). (28) By the same variational argument, 

Ep+ [J] ≥ Ee[J] + λD KL (p+∥e). (29) Thus, the distilled prior achieves an expected planning objective at least as high as that of the proposal distribution. If beam search produces proposals satisfying Ee[J] ≥ Epn [J], the resulting update is 23 also monotonic. This provides an interpretation of beam search as an improvement mechanism whose discoveries are distilled into the generative prior. 

Temperature adaptation. GTP adapts the temperature to maintain a target effective sample size (ESS) over planner-generated trajectories. In the population limit, the normalized ESS is related to the divergence between the updated prior and its proposal distribution: 

ESS( λ) = 11 + χ2(p+∥e) . (30) Consequently, targeting a fixed ESS corresponds to controlling the size of the prior update in terms of 

χ2 divergence. This prevents excessively concentrated importance weights while allowing the prior to progressively shift toward higher-return trajectories. 

Langevin augmentation. A limitation of the idealized update is that it cannot introduce probability mass outside the support of the current prior: 

supp( pn+1 ) ⊆ supp( pn). (31) Consequently, if the prior loses support around optimal trajectories, subsequent updates cannot recover these regions, violating the positivity assumption required for convergence. GTP addresses this issue by augmenting planner-generated trajectories with Langevin perturbations (App. C). The stochastic component introduces additional support, while the objective gradient directs perturbations toward high-scoring regions. This helps mitigate mode collapse and maintains opportunities to discover improved solutions. 

Scope and limitations. Our theoretical results characterize the idealized prior update under a fixed planning objective, infinitely many samples, and exact diffusion model fitting. They establish monotonic improvement and asymptotic convergence in this setting, but do not provide convergence guarantees for finite-budget beam search, imperfect function approximation, or jointly learned dynamics and value models. Nevertheless, the analysis provides a theoretical foundation for iterative trajectory improvement and prior distillation, while clarifying the roles of GTP’s key components in promoting effective learning. 24 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
