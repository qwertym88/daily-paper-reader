Abstract 

Planning under uncertain control execution noise requires reliable feedback policies across diverse en-vironments. We introduce Planning Under Noise (PUN) , a neural operator that learns noise-aware value fields for planning in unseen geometries. Building on an established screened-Poisson formulation of stochastic optimal control, we address two challenges: specifying boundary conditions that support navigation across varying geometries and efficiently computing fields with reliable gradients. Zero-Dirichlet obstacle boundaries terminate sampled paths at walls, weakening the goal signal along long routes and making it harder to estimate reliably. We instead impose reflecting Neumann conditions on obstacles and a Dirichlet condition at the goal, allowing sampled paths to continue exploring free space until they reach the target. To solve the resulting PDE without a configuration-space mesh, we extend a Monte Carlo (MC) solver, Walk on Stars (WoSt) , to higher dimensions. However, local MC fluctuations can reverse estimated gradient directions and cause planning failures, making direct planning require costly sampling. PUN amortizes this computation by learning geometry-to-field mappings from noisy, partially converged Monte Carlo labels, avoiding expensive convergence for every training example. At inference, test-time scaling uses bounded local search guided by the predicted global field. We evaluate PUN on planning tasks across dimensions. Across four noise levels, PUN matches or outperforms all compared planners, achieving 95–100% success on the 4D replanning benchmark while maintaining the lowest runtime. At the highest tested noise level in 4D ( λ = 4), PUN achieves 10.7–60.6% relative gains in success rate over other methods while running 3.29–32.15 × faster. 

# 1 Introduction 

Planning (Latombe, 1991) is one of the most fundamental problems in robotics. Given a robot’s configuration space (LaValle, 2006), planning computes a path from the current configuration to a goal that is optimal under a given cost function. Real robots do not execute a commanded motion exactly (Thrun, 2002). These errors accumulate along a motion, so a route planned with a few centimetres of clearance can be executed into a wall. Sampling-based planners such as PRM (Kavraki et al., 1996) and RRT (LaValle & Kuffner, 2001), and graph search such as A ⋆ (Hart et al., 1968), plan for a nominal system that executes commands exactly and leave the noise to a tracking controller. Sampling-based methods such as MPPI (Williams et al., 2017a) and PI 2-CMA (Stulp & Sigaud, 2012) optimize control sequences or policy parameters using sampled trajectory costs. Optimal feedback under execution noise can be derived from a value function, which gives the minimum expected cost of reaching the goal from each configuration and satisfies the nonlinear Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation (Fleming & Soner, 2006). Solving this equation on a grid scales exponentially with the dimension of the configuration space (Bellman, 1957; Han et al., 2018). While the problem can be converted into a linear screened Poisson equation (Theodorou et al., 2010) under control–noise matching (Assumption 1), a central difficulty in Poisson-based planning is specifying boundary conditions that produce a reliable value field across varying geometries. Conventional mesh-based numerical methods can solve the screened Poisson equation efficiently in two and three dimensions. However, a robot with d degrees of freedom (DoF) induces a d-dimensional configuration space, making volumetric discretizations and numerical PDE solution increasingly expensive. Planning must also accommodate complex and previously unseen obstacle geometries, for which constructing a new mesh and solving the PDE from scratch can be costly. Mesh-free Monte Carlo methods (Muller, 1956; Sawhney & Crane, 2020; Sawhney et al., 2023; Jambon et al., 2026) support PDE solution on complex geometries, motivating their use for planning in higher-dimensional configuration spaces. Among these, Walk-on-Spheres (WoS) (Muller, 1956) estimates the solution at a point by jumping repeatedly to a uniformly sampled point on the largest ball fitting in free space, until it reaches a boundary of known value, which requires every reachable boundary to be Dirichlet. Moreover, directly planning with Monte Carlo estimates is unreliable: trajectories depend on local value gradients, and even modest estimator noise can corrupt or reverse the estimated direction, leading to planning failure. Neural operators (Kovachki et al., 2023) can amortize expensive PDE solves by learning mappings from input geometries directly to solution fields. Moreover, prior work has shown that fully converged labels are unnecessary when training from Monte Carlo simulations: neural operators can recover accurate predictions from noisy, partially converged estimates (Viswanath et al., 2026). This enables training of neural operators with inexpensive Monte Carlo supervision while providing fast, smooth value-field predictions at inference time. We propose Planning Under Noise (PUN), shown in Fig. 2, an operator-learning framework that maps environment geometry to value fields that account for control noise. Boundary conditions can be understood through a heat analogy. With unit desirability at the goal and zero desirability on obstacle boundaries, the goal acts like a hot source, while the obstacle walls act like cold sinks. The zero-Dirichlet condition fixes the field to zero along each obstacle surface, making the entire obstacle boundary a heat sink. In a long or narrow passage, heat spreading from the goal therefore leaks into the walls along the route. The farther the goal’s influence must travel, the more it is attenuated, leaving a weak signal in distant parts of free space that discretization, sampling, or learning errors can obscure. Narrow passages make this particularly pronounced because the absorbing walls remain close on both sides. This attenuation is visible in Fig. 1(c), where the weak goal signal provides insufficient directional guidance for starting points far from the goal, making it difficult to navigate toward the target. Moreover, the screened Poisson equation already includes decay throughout free space through its screening term; absorbing obstacle boundaries introduce an additional source of attenuation. We therefore keep a Dirichlet condition at the goal but impose homogeneous Neumann (zero-flux) conditions on obstacle boundaries. The walls now act like insulation: they prevent heat from escaping through the boundary without forcing the desirability itself to vanish there. Heat can spread along a corridor without continually draining into its walls, allowing the goal’s influence to reach farther into free space. Screening still causes decay, but the additional loss through obstacles is removed. This is precisely the effect illustrated in Fig. 1(d): compared with the absorbing Dirichlet field in Fig. 1(c), the mixed-boundary field extends the goal’s influence much farther through the environment, preserving a stronger signal for planning along long routes. The Neumann condition also makes the value gradient parallel to smooth obstacle boundaries; therefore, gradient-based planning directs motion along walls rather than into them. This permits guidance along walls in narrow passages without the strong repulsion induced by zero-Dirichlet conditions. Under our regularity assumptions, the value field and control remain bounded at obstacles, whereas zero-Dirichlet desirability makes both diverge there (Appendix C.4). We first derive dimension-general Poisson kernels and Monte Carlo weights, extending mesh-free supervision beyond three dimensions for scalability. This enables supervision generation without a volumetric configuration-space mesh. We then train PUN using noisy, partially converged Monte Carlo estimates in the weak supervision paradigm. By amortizing the PDE solution across geometries, PUN produces spatially coherent value fields that guide gradient following and local search without requiring a fully converged Monte Carlo solve for every environment. We evaluate PUN across 2D–4D environments and compare its planning success and runtime with nominal planners and stochastic controllers under execution noise. • We formulate Poisson-based path planning as a mixed boundary-value problem, imposing a Dirichlet condition at the goal and homogeneous Neumann conditions on obstacle boundaries. 

• We introduce an operator-learning framework that maps environment geometry to noise-aware value fields, enabling zero-shot planning in unseen complex geometries without per-instance retraining or numerical PDE solves.

# 3 Related Works 

Path Planning. Classical planners divide into two families. Sampling-based methods such as PRM (Kavraki et al., 1996), RRT (LaValle & Kuffner, 2001), and RRT ⋆ (Karaman & Frazzoli, 2011) build a graph of collision-free motions, while search-based methods such as A ⋆ (Hart et al., 1968), Dijkstra (Dijkstra, 1959), and fast marching (Sethian, 1996) compute a cost-to-go over a discretization; both plan for a deterministic system and defer execution noise to tracking and replanning. Sampling-based methods such as MPPI (Williams et al., 2017a;b) and PI 2-CMA (Stulp & Sigaud, 2012) optimize control sequences or policy parameters using sampled trajectory costs. Our implementations and their treatment of execution noise are described in Appendix D.1. We compare against these planners in Table 5. Planning Neural Operator (PNO) (Matada et al., 2025) learns the solution operator of the Eikonal equation from labels produced by a deterministic solver, but it is limited to deterministic systems. NTFields (Ni & Qureshi, 2023) and EikoNet (Smith et al., 2021) train a physics-informed network on the Eikonal residual, which fits a single geometry per model and must be retrained for each new environment. In contrast, PUN is trained over a family of geometry classes with approximate Monte Carlo supervision across diverse noise ranges to support planning with different noise spectra while achieving reliable stability. 

Monte Carlo Methods. Monte Carlo estimators for PDEs can be categorized depending on the type of PDE, include Walk-on-Spheres (Sawhney & Crane, 2020), Walk-on-Boundaries (Sugimoto et al., 2023), and Backward Stochastic Differential Equations (BSDE) (Zhang, 2017). Recent works combine Monte Carlo methods with neural networks to further reduce variance (Nam et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023a), which train a network by regressing on noisy Monte Carlo targets and thereby amortize repeated pointwise Monte Carlo estimation into a forward pass. Two of their ingredients we adopt directly: a deterministic cap on the number of walk steps, and evaluation of the partially trained model when a walk exceeds that cap. The diffusion loss (N¨ usken & Richter, 2021) truncates an SDE simulation and supplements it with a boundary physics loss. 

Neural Operator. Neural operators parameterize maps between function spaces and can be evaluated on different discretizations, with numerical accuracy depending on the representation (Kovachki et al., 2023). The architectures we compare differ in how they propagate information: FNO (Li et al., 2021), UNO (Rahman et al., 2023), LocalNO (Liu-Schiaffini et al., 2024), DISCO (Ocampo et al., 2023), GINO (Li et al., 2023b), UPT (Alkin et al., 2024) and Transolver (Wu et al., 2024). We include U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) as a fixed-resolution 5reference. Physics-informed operators such as PINO (Li et al., 2024) add a PDE residual to the objective; we use walk labels as the primary signal with regularization on gradient terms. 

# 4 Planning under Noise (PUN) with Neural Operator 

PUN learns a desirability field from Walk-on-Stars supervision and uses the predicted field to guide local search. Labels are constructed directly in Ψ-space using finite-horizon walks, with learned continuation for unfinished trajectories. The neural operator is trained online on these labels. Algorithms 1 and 2 describe a single walk and the training loop, respectively; walks and terminal evaluations are batched.

## 4.4 Grid-Based Local Search 

PUN supports test-time scaling by increasing the local search budget without modifying the learned global field. More depth lets the search evaluate more reachable endpoints, while using the field for guidance. The same endpoint search can be run on other fields; in PUN, it is run on a global noise-aware field that is reused across replans. Paths are extracted from Ψ θ on a lattice. # 6 Discussion 

Extensions. Several extensions are possible. A spatially varying cost c(x) is the most useful: with constant 

c, the state-cost term penalizes elapsed time and the objective also penalizes control effort. Reflection carries 9no additional contact cost; choosing c(x) as a function of obstacle clearance would add a clearance-dependent running cost. Transient problems replace the stationary equation by its time-dependent counterpart, giving a finite-horizon value function rather than a first-exit one, which suits tasks with deadlines or moving goals. Partial information is the most direct use of our amortization: since the operator maps geometry to a field in one forward pass, replanning as a robot discovers obstacles requires only a forward pass on the updated geometry instead of a new solve. The open question is how to condition the operator on a partially observed occupancy map. 

Limitations. The value field is derived for reflecting boundaries. Local search allows its use in collision-failure tasks, but nominal graph feasibility does not guarantee collision-free execution under noise. Deriving the exact value function for that execution model would require a corresponding boundary condition. Assumption 1 is a further restriction. The control–noise matching assumption enables exact HJB linearization and is standard in classical path-integral control (Kappen, 2005; Theodorou et al., 2010). Our experiments are also limited to settings where this assumption is reasonable: we test geometric planning problems in up to four dimensions and do not include full robot models with contact. Evaluating on humanoid robots with contact would test both the assumption and the capacity of the operator. Finally, adversarial geometries such as mazes remain challenging, since long corridors can lead to weak or poorly resolved learned gradients. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced Planning Under Noise (PUN), a neural operator framework that learns noise-aware planning fields from approximate Walk-on-Stars supervision. Dimension-general screening kernels support mesh-free supervision beyond three dimensions, and local search converts predicted fields into paths on unseen geometries. Experiments across 2D–4D environments demonstrate zero-shot planning and effective learning from stochastic labels. In replanning comparisons under execution noise, PUN achieves the lowest reported runtime among the tested methods, while matching or exceeding the success of every compared planner at each tested noise level across 2D–4D.

## C.4 Why We Use Reflection at Obstacles 

Reflection lets the process remain in the free domain and continue toward the goal after reaching an obstacle boundary. Its corresponding condition is ∂nΨ = 0, as in Eq. 4 and the reflecting walks of Sawhney et al. (2023). This choice also keeps log Ψ, and so the control, finite up to the boundary under Assumption 2. 

Zero Dirichlet data produces a logarithmic singularity. Suppose instead that obstacles are absorbing, with zero terminal desirability. Denote the resulting solution by Ψ abs , retaining positive goal data and reflection at the outer workspace boundary. Then Ψabs = 0 on ∂S, log Ψ abs (x) → −∞ , Vabs (x) = −λ log Ψ abs (x) → +∞

as x approaches an obstacle from the free domain. Thus zero desirability represents an infinite collision penalty. The singularity is in the logarithmic representation and value function; the linear PDE solution Ψ abs can remain smooth. The control also becomes singular at a smooth obstacle surface. ## D.1 Evaluation and Training Protocol 

Evaluation scope. The pretrained 2D application benchmark contains 592 maps (37,888 starts); the 2D generated and 4D manipulator test sets each contain 512 maps (32,768 starts). Every map uses eight goals and every goal uses eight starts, chosen at the centers of eight equal goal-distance quantile bins. Success is evaluated on the matched saved queries, including failed rollouts in the denominator. Field prediction or solve, controller preprocessing, and rollout/search all contribute to reported test-time cost. These costs are amortized over the corresponding starts; they include more than the neural forward time. FEM solutions provide the numerical ground-truth fields for reconstruction-error evaluation. 21 Execution model for replanning comparisons. Table 5 compares nominal planners, sampling-based controllers, and PUN at execution-noise levels λ ∈ { 0.25 , 1, 2, 4}. Before boundary handling, the proposed transition is exk+1 = xk + ak + √λ∆t z k, with independent zk ∼ N (0 , I d), ∆ t = 1 /65 ,536, and ∥ak∥2 ≤ 1/(8 n), where n is the largest grid dimension. Each control interval contains eight microsteps. Coordinates occupy a unit square in 2D, a 1 × 1 × 12 domain in 3D, and normalized joint coordinates in 4D. Collision checks cover the entire executed motion segment, and an obstacle hit is counted as a rollout failure. 1. Absorption and radius floor. A walk reaches Γ D only in the limit, so absorption is triggered on the shell {dD <ε} and the terminal value is g at the nearest point of Γ D ; ε is chosen below the tubular-neighborhood radius of Γ D and its distance to Γ N . The star radius also carries a floor, r(x) = min {dD(x), max( rmin , d sil (x)) },so that steps do not become arbitrarily short near a silhouette. The shell costs at most CΨε (Eq. 38); the floor can break star-shapedness, and the error it introduces has unknown sign (Sawhney et al., 2023). 2. Weighted survival. The solid-angle sampling law in Eq. 8 leaves the scalar screening factor Qκd as the per-step weight. We multiply these factors rather than sample killing events or apply Russian roulette. Conditioning out the killing randomness removes that source of variance and avoids zero replicas caused by stochastic killing. 3. Fixed-horizon continuation. Walks stop at the prescribed step cap or the goal. Capped paths contribute their accumulated weight times the detached EMA prediction at the endpoint, as in Section 4.2. Goal-reaching paths use the known boundary value. Every requested trajectory remains in the sample mean, including capped paths. 4. Log accumulation and bias correction. The trace records the sum of log step weights independently of their floating-point product, so product underflow does not erase the log evidence. The log-label variants use log-sum-exp and the sample-variance correction in Eq. 36. The direct-label variants use the weighted arithmetic mean without this correction. Direct and log targets are constructed from the same sampled paths. 5. Reuse of model-independent walks. Cached endpoints, weights, and completion states are independent of the neural parameters. The EMA continuation is reevaluated during training rather than frozen into the cache. 6. Stable screening kernels. Backbone compute policy. FNO uses 200 epochs. Each alternative backbone has a maximum of 200 epochs and a registered training-compute allowance of 66,856.484 seconds; an epoch is not started if it is not expected to fit within the remaining allowance. Each completed epoch uses 64 training maps and eight goals per map. The 200-epoch cosine schedule is retained when a run stops at the compute cap. The allowance was derived from 1.5 times an estimated individual FNO training cost; shared work in the reference run means it is not an exact equal-GPU-time comparison. Completed training epochs were 200 for FNO, 200 for U-NO, 185 for U-Net, 13 for GINO, 200 for LocalNO, 200 for DISCO, 146 for UPT, and 134 for Transolver. Our overall method is architecture-agnostic; we chose FNO because it gives the highest planning success among the tested implementations under this compute policy, but our overall method is compatible with other neural-operator architectures. Planning benefits from balancing sensitivity to local obstacle geometry with a global view of the environment.
