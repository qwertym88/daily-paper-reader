# One Policy, Many Styles: Where to Aggregate Preferences for Steerable Humanoid Control? 

Ziqian Huang Yuzhuo Chen Shimin Zhang Junyang He Xinyue Annie Yang Animesh Garg 

College of Computing Georgia Institute of Technology 

## Abstract 

Modern robotic agents are increasingly expected to support steerable control, where a single policy can adjust its behavioral style at runtime under changing user preferences. Preference-conditioned reinforcement learning provides this interface, but conditioning alone does not ensure that a requested behavior change is strong, does not leak into unrelated behaviors, and does not compromise task performance. Our evaluation combines Preference Reward Responsiveness (PRR) with coverage and utility metrics. We use these measures to compare reward-level, advantage-level, loss-level, and PCGrad-based aggregation across two humanoid-control frameworks. No aggregation level performs best across all environments and criteria. Experiments show that aggregating objectives early produces a wider range of behavioral responses but greater unintended changes in other objectives, while aggregating them later improves control precision at some cost to responsiveness. 

## 1 Introduction 

Robotic and embodied agents are increasingly expected to operate under changing user preferences and behavioral constraints. A household robot may need to prioritize speed in one setting, energy efficiency in another, or smooth and safe motion around humans. Rather than training separate policies for every trade-off, an ideal system should support steerable control, where behavior can be adjusted at runtime through a compact and interpretable preference interface. Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) provides a natural framework for this problem by representing behavior through multiple reward dimensions and conditioning a policy on a preference vector which specifies desired objective trade-offs [Basaklar et al., 2023a, Liu et al., 2025]. This enables one policy to expose a continuous control interface at test time, but conditioning alone does not guarantee steerability. Increasing one preference may produce little change in the intended behavior, produce unintended behaviors, or compromise the essential tasks that the robot must continue to perform. This distinction is not captured by aggregate return or Pareto-coverage measures alone [de las Heras Molins et al., 2026]. A useful evaluation should answer three questions: Does the policy respond strongly when a preference changes? Does the change unintentionally affect other behaviors? Does the policy still perform the task reliably without more failures? We therefore formulate preference-conditioned steerability as a multidimensional evaluation problem and introduce a unified suite based on the Preference Reward Responsiveness (PRR) matrix, Self-PRR, Cross-PRR, Selectivity, and Rank Accuracy. These metrics complement standard utility measures by exposing both intended responsiveness and cross-objective leakage [de las Heras Molins et al., 2026].

## 2 Background and Related work 

Preference-conditioned MORL trains a single policy over preference vectors so that it can represent multiple objective trade-offs at runtime [Basaklar et al., 2023b, Ambadkar et al., 2026]. Recent work applies this to humanoid control through a mixture-of-experts architecture [Li et al., 2026a], but fixes a single aggregation point and does not measure cross-objective leakage. We instead study how the aggregation point affects whether changing a policy’s preference input predictably changes its behavior. In the LLM literature, Constitutional AI shows how written principles can guide behavior through self-critique and AI feedback [Bai et al., 2022]. The constitution shapes training rather than serving as a continuous runtime control. SteerLM enables runtime steering by conditioning generation on desired attribute values, while multi-objective alignment methods combine rewards or model predictions to trade off properties such as helpfulness, safety, and coding ability [Dong et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2024]. Recent methods extend this idea to fine-grained value priorities and efficient adaptation across a Pareto frontier of user preferences [Guo et al., 2025, Li et al., 2026b]. Together, these works motivate an explicit interface for controlling behavior. The level at which objective signals are combined during training can affect how well each preference controls behavior. The level at which objective signals are combined during training can affect how well each preference controls behavior. Gradient-surgery methods such as PCGrad address objective conflicts by modifying per-objective gradients before they are combined [Yu et al., 2020]. Preference-conditioned MORL is usually evaluated with Pareto coverage and utility, which do not directly separate intended response from cross-objective leakage. Recent controllability work begins to expose this limitation [de las Heras Molins et al., 2026]. We extend this perspective to embodied control by comparing reward, advantage, and loss-level combination and evaluating responsiveness, monotonicity, selectivity, task preservation, and failure behavior across two robot-control settings.

5.1 ProtoMotions: G1 Humanoid Preference-Conditioned Locomotion Setup. We evaluate four aggregation strategies, reward-level, advantage-level, loss-level, and PCGrad (Section 4.1), on a G1 humanoid trained to walk under a K=5 -dimensional style preference (energy, arm swing, foot clearance, step width, smoothness) alongside a fixed-weight heading-tracking task reward. Preferences are sampled from Dirichlet(0 .5) during training. Each method is trained for 1.2 × 10 9 environment steps with three independent seeds; full task and reward definitions are given in Appendix C.1. 

Evaluation protocol. We measure steering ability (Section 4.2) along the five axes from Section 1: responsiveness (Hypervolume, Expected Utility, Self-PRR), monotonicity (Rank Accuracy), selectiv-ity (Selectivity, Cross-PRR), task preservation (mean task reward Jtask for tracking the fixed heading and speed target), and failure behavior (fall rate). These are computed from a per-dimension pref-erence axis sweep together with N =50 preferences sampled from the training-time Dirichlet(0 .5) 

distribution, under a fixed-horizon convention that zeroes reward after a fall. Full metric definitions and evaluation hyperparameters are given in Appendix C.2. 

Main results. Table 1 reports every metric as mean ± std over three training seeds. No single method dominates. Reward-level attains roughly 3× the hypervolume, 2× the self-responsiveness, and roughly 2× the Jtask of the other three methods, with error bars that do not overlap across seeds. It is, however, the weakest of the four on precision, with the lowest selectivity and the highest cross-dimension leakage. Advantage-level shows the opposite profile: the smallest controllable region but the cleanest separation between preference dimensions. Loss-level and PCGrad fall between the two on both axes. We hypothesize this pattern reflects how much per-objective structure survives before aggregation. Reward-level collapses all K objectives into a single scalar reward before the critic or the advantage estimator ever sees them, leaving no mechanism downstream that could prevent one objective’s gradient from dominating or leaking into the others. Advantage-level, loss-level, and PCGrad instead keep per-objective critics and advantages further into the update (Section 4.1), which appears to trade raw responsiveness for cleaner separation. Aggregating early thus favors a wide controllable range at the cost of precision, while aggregating late favors precise, disentangled control at the cost of range. ## 6 Limitations and Future Work 

Our study is limited to two simulated humanoid environments with a narrow range of tasks and a small set of hand-designed preference dimensions, and no strategy resolves the responsiveness-precision trade-off we observe. Future work should investigate adaptive objective-combination methods that explicitly balance these properties, other gradient-conflict-resolution methods beyond PCGrad, robustness to out-of-distribution and dynamically changing preferences, and transfer to real robotic systems. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We study when a preference-conditioned policy is truly steerable: whether changing its preference input changes the intended behavior selectively, without compromising the underlying task. Because standard MORL metrics such as hypervolume and expected utility do not capture these properties, we introduce an evaluation suite based on the Preference–Reward Responsiveness matrix, including Self-PRR, Cross-PRR, Selectivity, and Rank Accuracy alongside utility and failure metrics. Across two humanoid-control frameworks, we show that the level of objective-signal aggregation shapes the trade-off between responsiveness and cross-objective interference. In ProtoMotions, 6reward-level aggregation achieves the widest controllable range and highest utility but greater leakage, while later aggregation and PCGrad improve selectivity at the cost of range. In MimicKit, all aggre-gation levels steer reliably, with later aggregation improving coverage or precision without materially reducing task performance. Overall, no fixed strategy dominates, motivating both multidimensional steerability evaluation and adaptive aggregation methods. 7References

For each outer objective i ∈ { 0, . . . , K }, we traverse the other K objectives in an independently sampled random order ( torch.randperm ), rather than a single shared shuffle order for the entire update step. Each objective’s gradient gi is compared only against the original , pre-projection gradients gj of the objectives it is checked against, so a correction already applied to gi earlier in its own traversal does not affect how later projections are computed; i is explicitly excluded from its own inner loop to avoid a projected gradient later spuriously conflicting with its own untouched original direction. These choices follow the original PCGrad formulation [Yu et al., 2020].
