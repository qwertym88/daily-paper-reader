We develop quantum algorithms for optimal control over a prescribed finite time in-terval (finite horizon), focusing on continuous-time linear–quadratic regulation (LQR). This foundational control problem often requires solving a nonlinear matrix Riccati equation, which can be costly for large systems and long control durations on con-ventional classical solvers. We instead work directly with the state-costate optimality equations, reformulating the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problem as a multiple-shooting linear system. Importantly, we prove a condition-number bound for the system that is independent of the horizon, under stabilizability and detectability assumptions. By incorporating the initial and final conditions into a single linear system, we can solve the subsequent linear system more efficiently with a quantum linear systems subrou-tine. For a given initial state, our quantum algorithms returns classical estimates of the optimal cost and the optimal control inputs at a selected time, without constructing a Riccati solution operator. Given sparse-matrix block-encodings, an initial-state prepa-ration oracle and a fixed number of control inputs, the query and gate complexities scale polylogarithmically with system size and control duration, provided that sparsity, relevant norms, and stability parameters are bounded. The dependence on the target error is inverse-linear up to logarithmic factors. We establish the required bounds uni-formly in system size and duration for a family of damped mass–spring chains with two endpoint controls. Numerical experiments support the analysis. 

# 1 Introduction 

Optimal control theory studies how to choose control actions over time in order to steer a dynamical system toward a prescribed objective while optimizing a performance criterion [1]. It has found broad applications in electric power systems [2], aerospace engineering [3], process control [4] and mathematical finance [5] etc. A particularly important and analytically tractable class of optimal control problems is the LQR model [1,6–8], in which the dynamics are linear and the cost functional is quadratic. These connections make LQR both a classical benchmark problem and a practically relevant subroutine in modern optimal-control algorithms. LQR problems are commonly studied in two closely related but conceptually distinct settings: finite-horizon and infinite-horizon control. The finite-horizon formulation optimizes system per-formance over a prescribed time interval, often with a terminal objective, and therefore leads to a time-dependent optimal feedback law. This setting is particularly natural for transient control tasks, such as maneuvering, tracking, terminal-state regulation, and receding-horizon [1, 6, 7, 11]. By contrast, the infinite-horizon formulation concerns long-time regulation and, under standard stabilizability and detectability assumptions, yields a stationary stabilizing feedback law. From a computational perspective, these two formulations lead to differential Riccati equation (DRE): classical finite-horizon LQR methods typically require solving a DRE, whereas the infinite-horizon case is usually reduced to an algebraic Riccati equation (ARE) [1, 6, 7]. Since the finite-horizon problem involves a time-dependent matrix differential equation rather than a stationary algebraic equation, it is often computationally more involved, especially for large-scale systems or when high accuracy is required over long time intervals [8, 12]. Motivated by both its computational chal-lenges and its relevance to time-limited control tasks, this work focuses on the finite-horizon LQR problem. Recent advances in quantum computing have opened new possibilities for high-dimensional scientific computation. Foundational quantum algorithms such as Shor’s factoring algorithm [13] and Grover’s search algorithm [14] demonstrate that quantum computers can offer substantial complexity advantages for certain computational tasks. In scientific computing, quantum linear-systems algorithms (QLSAs) [15–20] and quantum algorithms for ordinary and partial differential equations [21–29] have emerged as important algorithmic primitives. These developments naturally raise the question of whether quantum algorithms can also accelerate large-scale optimal-control computations. Recently, Clayton et al. [30] developed a differentiable quantum-control framework that com-bines quantum Lyapunov-equation solvers with policy-gradient methods for infinite-horizon LQR problems. In parallel, recent quantum algorithms for Lyapunov, Sylvester, algebraic Riccati, and matrix-function problems provide potential building blocks for infinite-horizon LQR and related control applications [31–35]. Block-encoding and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) techniques have also been used to formulate quantum pipelines for discrete-time Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) control [36], and this discrete-time framework has subsequently been extended to more general discrete-time nonlinear constrained optimal-control problems [37]. These works illustrate the growing interest in quantum approaches to optimal control, while also highlighting that the literature remains at an early stage. Quantum algorithms are particularly well-developed for linear problems, most notably linear systems and linear differential equations. By contrast, quantum algorithms for genuinely nonlinear problems remain less mature, despite recent progress based on linearization or embedding tech-niques [38–40]. This limitation is especially relevant for the DRE, which is fact a nonlinear matrix differential equation. Recently, Krovi [41] proposed a quantum algorithm for the DRE based on a Möbius transformation that linearizes the Riccati dynamics. However, this approach requires solving a matrix-inversion problem whose condition number can grow exponentially with the time horizon T , leading to an exponential dependence on T in the overall complexity [42, 43]. This becomes prohibitive for long-horizon finite-time control. More recently, Wang et al. [44] developed a quantum method for Riccati equations based on weighted Riesz operators. 1 By treating stable and unstable components of the dynamics separately, their method avoids directly encoding exponentially growing forward propagation. It constructs quantum encodings of Riccati solution matrices and can estimate classical feedback controls given quantum access to the current state. This provides a complementary route to optimal control: this formulation works through the Riccati operator, whereas in our method, we directly use the state–costate boundary-value system. In this work, we develop quantum algorithms for continuous-time finite-horizon LQR with a fixed initial state. The outputs are classical estimates of the optimal cost or the optimal control

# 2 Overview of results and key ideas 

Optimal control seeks to minimize a cost function for a system whose n-dimensional state evolves under d time-dependent control inputs. In LQR, the dynamics is linear and the cost function is quadratic. We consider a fixed initial state and a finite time horizon T . Our algorithms estimate the optimal cost and the optimal control vector at one selected temporal grid point. Both outputs are classical, with a target error ε measured in absolute value for the cost and in Euclidean norm for the control vector. The formal problem is stated in Section 3.

We assume the sparse-matrix block-encodings and initial-state preparation oracle specified in Assumption 1, together with stabilizability and detectability as in Assumption 2. Here logarithmic factors in the inverse error and failure probability are suppressed. Theorems 2 and 3 give the full parameter dependence and distinguish query costs from additional gates. For fixed d, both bounds are polylogarithmic in n and T within this access model. 

Conditioning from the boundary conditions. Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle (PMP) [7, 46] introduces an auxiliary vector called the costate. Along an optimal trajectory, it describes the sensitivity of the minimum remaining cost to changes in the state. For LQR, the state of the dynamics system and costate together satisfy a linear differential equation, with conditions imposed at both the initial and terminal times. We collect the state and costate at the temporal grid points and impose the evolution equations between adjacent points, together with the endpoint conditions. This construction, known as multiple shooting, gives a block-structured linear system. Its condition number measures the sensitivity of its solution to perturbations and enters the cost of the quantum algorithm. Although the state–costate dynamics can contain growing modes, the boundary conditions allow these modes to be treated in the appropriate direction. A change of variables separates a part that decays under forward propagation from a part that decays under backward propagation. The initial and terminal conditions supply data at the corresponding ends. The influence of one grid point on another therefore decays with their separation, which allows us to bound the inverse of the assembled system. For a fixed grid spacing h, we obtain a condition-number bound independent of the time horizon 

T , with dependence on h and the underlying LQR problem. This dependence includes the problem-dependent constant M , defined in Section 4. The growth of M with the state dimension n depends on the problem family and need not be mild; see Section 6.4 for further discussion. This is why the 

3informal complexity bounds above require suitable bounds on the stability parameters. However, in this work we also provide a concrete example for which M admits an upper bound independent of the state dimension n.

Dependence on the state dimension. Our example is a coupled mass–spring chain with two endpoint controls. Each mass contributes a position and a velocity to the state, so n is twice the number of masses, while d = 2 .For fixed positive damping, on-site and coupling stiffnesses, and cost weights, with masses bounded above and away from zero, we prove that the stability parameters, including M , as well as the coefficient norms and matrix sparsity, admit bounds independent of the chain length. With a uniformly bounded initial-state norm, both estimation tasks therefore have polylogarithmic dependence on n and T in the stated input model. The formal statement is given in Section 4.

# 7 Conclusion 

We developed quantum algorithms for continuous-time finite-horizon LQR that return classical estimates of the optimal cost and the optimal control vector at a selected temporal node for a prescribed initial state. The state–costate boundary-value problem is formulated as a multiple-shooting linear system. Under stabilizability and detectability, a Riccati–Lyapunov decoupling yields a condition-number bound uniform in the horizon at a fixed time step. Gauss–Legendre discretization preserves this bound for sufficiently small propagator error, while increasing the stage number provides the required accuracy on a fixed admissible grid. Inverse block-encoding and amplitude estimation produce the requested outputs; the Riccati and Lyapunov solutions are used only in the analysis. Under the stated oracle model, with the required norm and stability bounds available, the query and additional gate complexities are polylogarithmic in the state dimension and horizon when the control dimension is fixed and the sparsity, coefficient norms, ∥R−1∥, ∥x0∥, and stability parameters 

Mc, M P , α −1 

> c

remain uniformly bounded. The dependence on the additive error tolerance is inverse-linear up to logarithmic factors. For the coupled mass–spring chain, we prove the required bounds on the system and stability parameters for fixed positive damping, on-site and coupling stiffnesses, and cost weights, with masses bounded above and away from zero. With two endpoint controls and uniformly bounded total initial mechanical energy, this family therefore satisfies the conditions for the stated complexity scaling. Numerical experiments support the discretization and conditioning estimates over the tested ranges. Future work includes preconditioning for weakly damped systems and fine spatial discretiza-tions, with the construction and block-encoding costs of the preconditioners included in the resource estimates. Another direction is to develop encodings tailored to prescribed control functionals and obtain sharper resource bounds that account for their access and normalization costs.
