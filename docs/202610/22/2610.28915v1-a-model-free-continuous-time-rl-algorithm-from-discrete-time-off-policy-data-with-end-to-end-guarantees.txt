# A Model-Free Continuous-Time RL Algorithm from Discrete-Time Off-Policy Data with End-to-End Guarantees 

Chaorui Yao 1 Yuming Paul Zhang 2 Lin Yang 1 Yuhua Zhu 3,* 

Abstract 

In this paper, we study continuous-time reinforcement learning, where the state follows an unknown stochastic differential equation but only a fixed off-policy batch of discrete-time transitions is available. Standard discrete-time Q-learning becomes problematic as the sampling interval shrinks, because differences between action values vanish. Continuous-time alternatives, such as Deep Advantage Updating and martingale q-learning, preserve the action signal but jointly learn a value function and an action-dependent critic. Building on the PhiBE-Q formulation [1], we develop a model-free PhiBE policy iteration (PhiBE-PI) framework that uses a single state-action critic while preserving the continuous-time action-ranking scale. At each iteration, the framework learns the critic directly from sampled increments and uses it to improve the policy. The same batch is reused throughout, without explicitly estimating the drift or diffusion. We further develop an implementation under linear function approximation and establish a local end-to-end suboptimality guarantee for the learned policy. Our analysis quantifies how errors from time discretization, function approximation, and finite-sample policy evaluation propagate through policy iteration. It accounts for the dependence of successive policies on the reused data, so the guarantee does not require a fresh batch at each iteration. Moreover, we identify a bias–variance trade-off: smaller sampling intervals improve the approximation to the continuous-time problem but can increase statistical error at a fixed sample size. Thus, finer time discretization does not necessarily lead to better policy performance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Many reinforcement-learning problems arise from systems that evolve in continuous time. Robotic and physical systems evolve between controller updates; physiological variables such as glucose levels change continuously while measurements and interventions arrive intermittently [2, 3, 4]; and portfolio and execution problems are often modeled through controlled diffusions [5, 6]. The natural objective in these settings is a continuous-time stochastic-control objective, but the data available to a learning algorithm are usually sampled. When the generator is known, policy iteration evaluates a value function by solving a linear PDE and improves the policy by maximizing the Hamiltonian, or equivalently the continuous-time action score induced by the value function [5, 7, 8, 9, 10]. This route is appealing not only because it preserves the continuous-time Bellman structure, but also because the resulting known-model PI has strong algorithmic convergence theory: it is closely related to Newton–Kantorovich iteration and admits quadratic, superlinear, or exponential-type convergence rates in various discrete, diffusion, and HJ/HJB settings [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. These guarantees, however, rely on an accurate continuous-time model. As discussed in [14], identifying continuous-time dynamics from discrete-time observations is generally ill-posed: different continuous-time dynamics may yield the same discrete-time transitions. Consequently, errors in the recovered model can propagate through policy evaluation and improvement, leading to a suboptimal policy despite the fast convergence of known-model PI. This motivates learning a finite-∆ t surrogate of the relevant state-action equation directly from sampled increments, without requiring explicit recovery of the continuous-time dynamics. A model-free alternative avoids estimating the dynamics and instead learns the policy directly from data. Its most direct form discretizes everything, treating each sampling interval as one step of a discrete-time MDP and applying standard off-policy Q-learning to the logged one-step transitions [15, 16]. The appeal is operational: the critic is a single state-action object, its Bellman equation is written directly in terms of the logged transition kernel, and off-policy one-step samples can be reused across policy-evaluation steps. But this reduction distorts the action-ranking signal in the continuous-time limit. The first action is applied only over [0 , ∆t], so Q∆tπ (s, a ) differs from V ∆tπ (s)by only O(∆ t). Consequently the advantage used for greedy improvement vanishes as ∆ t → 0[17, 18]. Discrete-time Q-learning fits the data interface, but loses the continuous-time action scale. Another model-free route seeks a continuous-time analogue of the Q-function that preserves the action-ranking signal as ∆ t → 0. This idea goes back to Baird’s advantage updating [19], which rescales the vanishing discrete-time advantage to retain the effect of an action per unit time. Tallec et al. [17] further developed this approach through Deep Advantage Updating (DAU). For controlled diffusions, Jia and Zhou [20] developed the little q-function and its martingale characterization. However, DAU and martingale q-learning jointly learn a value function and an action-dependent critic, rather than using a standalone state-action critic as in standard Q-learning. Related continuous-time RL work studies finite-horizon LQ learning, mean-field-game learning, entropy regularization, and the accuracy of discretely sampled stochastic policies [21, 22, 23, 24]. These works address important continuous-time learning regimes, but they do not provide the one-batch, model-free, Q-based policy-iteration mechanism targeted here. The preceding routes fall short in complementary ways. The model-based route has the right continuous-time action ranking and, once the generator is known, can benefit from fast policy-iteration convergence. Its bottleneck is upstream: the generator must be known or accurately recovered from finite-∆ t transitions, and this recovery error can dominate the subsequent PI iteration. Discrete-time Q-learning has the opposite strength: it gives a standalone off-policy state-action equation tied directly to one-step samples, but its advantage signal collapses as ∆ t → 0. DAU and martingale q-learning preserve an O(1) action signal, but jointly learn a value function and an action-dependent critic. These complementary limitations motivate learning the relevant state-action equation directly from sampled increments. This leaves a simple question: can sampled one-step data support policy iteration for continuous-time control without first recovering the model? We seek a Q-based policy iteration method that keeps the operational simplicity of discrete-time Q-learning: for each policy, learn a single 2state-action critic from a fixed off-policy batch and improve the policy greedily. At the same time, the critic should preserve the continuous-time action-ranking scale, so that greedy improvement remains meaningful as ∆ t → 0. The method should learn directly from finite-∆ t increments, without fitting the drift and diffusion or learning a separate value critic. The central question is whether such a sampled-increment Q-style object can be propagated through policy iteration with a local end-to-end guarantee for the original continuous-time control objective. Recent methods based on PhiBE, a PDE-based Bellman equation, provide the ingredients for such a construction. PhiBE uses finite-step moment information from the sampled kernel ρ∆t to build continuous-time PDE-based Bellman equations for policy evaluation, and Optimal-PhiBE extends this idea to value-level optimal control [14, 25]. Separately, PhiBE-Q identifies a nondegenerate continuous-time state-action evaluator whose policy average recovers the value function and whose greedy action agrees with the Hamiltonian ranking rule [1]. Together, these results provide the two structural ingredients needed here: finite-∆ t moment surrogates identified from one-step samples, and a continuous-time-scale state-action ranking. This paper combines these ingredients in the linear function approximation setting. We construct a finite-∆ t PhiBE-Q surrogate by replacing the infinitesimal generator with one-step moment quantities identified from the logged transition kernel ρ∆t. The resulting policy-evaluation equation is estimated directly from the observed increments ∆ si/∆t and ∆ si∆s⊤

Combined with greedy improvement, this yields a one-batch off-policy policy-iteration algorithm: the same batch is reused at every evaluation step, even though later greedy policies are data-dependent. Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate a one-batch off-policy PhiBE policy iteration (PhiBE-PI) framework and give an implementation with linear function approximation from one-step samples. Second, we prove a uniform finite-sample policy-evaluation bound over the feature-induced greedy policy class. This uniformity is essential because the same batch is reused across policy iteration and later greedy policies depend on that batch. Third, we establish a local end-to-end continuous-time suboptimality guarantee for the output policy. The bound separates surrogate-transfer error, linear function approximation error, one-batch finite-sample evaluation error, and policy-iteration propagation error. The guarantee also exposes the central continuous-time tradeoff. Decreasing ∆ t improves the population surrogate by making the finite-step moments closer to the infinitesimal generator, but it worsens the finite-sample radius because generator-scale increment estimators become more variable. Thus the analysis quantifies the same bias–variance tension that makes the sampled-data continuous-time regime nontrivial. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the learning setting and the small-step action-ranking gap. Section 3 introduces the PhiBE-Q surrogate and the one-batch linear policy-iteration algorithm. Section 4 proves the local end-to-end finite-sample guarantee. Section 5 presents numerical evidence, and Section 6 discusses limitations and future directions.

# 5 Experiments 

We study three examples. First, a smooth stochastic control problem with an analytic optimal value provides an instance satisfying A1–A3 and allows us to measure the control error of hard-greedy PhiBE-PI. Second, scalar LQR illustrates the effects of the discount parameter and sampling interval on statistical and discretization errors. Third, CartPole examines performance when the training and deployment frequencies differ. In the LQR and CartPole comparisons, PhiBE-PI and the Bellman policy iteration (BE-PI) baseline use the same off-policy data and features. The LQR experiment uses unbounded costs, uniform sampling, and a drift-only evaluator; CartPole uses deterministic 16 rollout data, discrete actions, and softmax improvement. These two experiments explore settings beyond the assumptions of the hard-greedy guarantee.

5.3 CartPole with different training and deployment frequencies 

We consider CartPole control when only coarsely sampled training data are available, but actions can be updated more frequently at deployment. We compare PhiBE-PI and BE-PI using the same fixed data while varying the deployment control interval. 

Dynamics and data. The state is s = ( x, v, θ, ω ), where x is cart position, v = ˙ x, θ = 0 is upright, and ω = ˙θ. The available forces are Fa ∈ {− 50 , 0, 50 } N. We use cart mass M = 8, pole mass m = 2, half-length ℓ = 0 .5, and gravity g = 9 .8. Writing ca = ( Fa + mℓω 2 sin θ)/(M + m), the dynamics are ˙ x = v, ˙θ = ω, ˙ ω = g sin θ−ca cos θℓ(4/3− mM +m cos 2 θ) , and ˙ v = ca − mℓ M +m ˙ω cos θ.For each of 30 training seeds (9000–9029), both methods use the same dataset of n = 8000 action-labeled transition pairs. To collect these pairs, we draw an initial state with independent coordinates uniform on [ −0.2, 0.2], then repeatedly choose a force uniformly and hold it for ∆ tr = 0 .1215 s. We integrate the dynamics using fourth-order Runge–Kutta with internal step h = 0 .001215 s and no added force noise; deployment uses the same integrator and internal step. A rollout terminates when |x| ≥ 2.4 m or |θ| ≥ π/ 15 (12 ◦), checked after every internal step, and restarts from the same initial-state distribution. We record ( s, a, r (s), s ′, τ, d ) for each transition, where τ ≤ ∆tr is the elapsed time up to the next action update or failure, and d indicates failure. Each pair is assigned the smooth instantaneous reward r(s) = 0 .01 + 0 .99 exp[ −3( θ/ (π/ 15)) 2 − 3( x/ 2.4) 2], with zero value after termination. 

Implementation. Both methods use 246 state–action features: an intercept and 81 Gaussian state features, tensored with three action indicators. The Gaussians have width one and centers in {− 1, 0, 1}4 after scaling the state coordinates by (2 .4, 2, π/ 15 , 2). We use a common β = 5 s −1,selected from a preliminary stability scan, giving BE-PI discount γ = e−β∆tr ≈ 0.5447. Softmax logits are Q/T for BE-PI and βQ/T for PhiBE-PI, with T = 200; deployment is deterministic greedy. We add (0 .1/n )I to each normalized evaluation matrix. We initialize the weights at zero and stop when their Euclidean change is at most 10 −5, with a maximum of 200 iterations. 19 Failure can shorten a transition, so policy evaluation uses its recorded duration τ . We weight each transition’s contribution by τ / ∆tr . BE-PI discounts the next-state value by e−βτ and sets it to zero after failure. Its reward target is r(s)(1 − e−βτ )/(1 − e−β∆tr ), which equals r(s) for a full hold interval. For nonterminal transitions, PhiBE-PI approximates the rate of change of the policy-averaged value by ∇ ¯Qπ(s) · (s′ − s)/τ , differentiating both the features and the softmax probabilities. For failed transitions, it instead approximates this rate by [0 − ¯Qπ(s)] /τ , using zero continuation value after failure. Both methods use the recorded r(s) without intermediate reward samples.

Reward and absolute angle are averaged over observations at action updates before termination, first within each rollout and then equally across rollouts and training seeds. We also report survival time, capped at H, and the fraction of rollouts that fail by H; conditional reward alone can conceal early failure. # 6 Conclusion 

We developed a model-free PhiBE-PI framework based on PhiBE-Q [1] for continuous-time control from a fixed off-policy batch. The framework learns a single state-action critic directly from sampled increments and reuses the same data for successive policy updates. For its linear-function-approximation implementation, our analysis connects finite-sample policy evaluation to a local end-to-end control guarantee. It also makes explicit the trade-off between reducing discretization error and controlling statistical error at a fixed sample size. The experiments illustrate both the benefits and limitations of this approach. The smooth-control example measures policy performance against an analytic optimum, while the LQR example separates discretization and statistical errors. Both exhibit a bias–variance trade-off in the sampling interval. In CartPole, PhiBE-PI benefits from updating actions more frequently at deployment than in the training data and outperforms the BE-PI baseline at the finer control intervals tested. The BE-PI baseline, however, performs better when the training and deployment intervals coincide. The theoretical guarantee remains local and applies to linear function approximation with hard-greedy improvement under the stated regularity and coverage assumptions. The softmax implementation used in CartPole lies beyond this guarantee. Extending the analysis to soft policy updates and nonlinear function approximation, and obtaining sharper finite-sample bounds for repeated policy improvement, are important directions for future work.
