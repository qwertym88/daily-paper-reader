Neural network architectures embody choices about how information is represented, accumulated, and evaluated. Recurrent neural networks process sequential inputs by repeatedly updating a hidden state, allowing information from earlier inputs to influence later computations [SMH11]. Attention mechanisms instead provide a way to retrieve and combine information according to its relevance to a query [BCB15, VSP +17]. These mechanisms are usually presented through explicit computational formulas, involving matrix multiplication, nonlinear activation, normalization, and aggregation. Such formulas specify how a model operates, but they also invite a structural question: what mathematical objects and operations underlie these computations, and how might alternative choices lead to different architectures? The use of geometry to understand and design neural networks has an established foundation. Geo-metric deep learning emphasizes how the structure and symmetries of a learning problem can guide the construction of architectures, bringing together methods for grids, groups, graphs, and manifolds within a common perspective [BBCV21]. The study of permutation-invariant learning provides a particularly concrete example: the requirement that a function be insensitive to input order leads to structured archi-tectures for processing sets [ZKR +17]. These developments illustrate the value of treating mathematical structure as a source of architectural constraints and design principles. Complementing this perspective, we focus on the relationship between the space in which information is stored, the operations by which it is accumulated, and the pairings through which it is evaluated. In this paper, we introduce a geometric framework organized around the notions of a knowledge world 

and an intelligence . A character space A represents elementary units of encoded information, while a sequence of spaces ( An)n≥0 describes the possible knowledge states after successive inputs. Update maps specify how a new character is incorporated into an existing state. An intelligence assigns to each state a function that evaluates characters in a stage-dependent evaluation space Vn. In a concrete setting, these

structures are expressed by maps 

μn : A × An → An+1 , Bn : An × A → Vn,

The spaces Vn may have several coordinates, corresponding to different evaluation criteria, and their dimensions may vary with the stage. We call Bn an attention pairing , using the term “pairing” for a structured two-input evaluation map without requiring it to be bilinear. An important feature of the framework is the distinction between evaluating information and using that evaluation in a computation. A pairing may produce compatibility scores, a proposed memory increment, or a candidate hidden representation. Additional operations determine how these evalua-tions affect the model: they may be normalized, used to rescale and aggregate stored information, or incorporated into a subsequent knowledge state. For example, scaled dot-product attention combines a query–key pairing with softmax normalization and weighted aggregation of values [VSP +17]. In our formulation, these are separate choices rather than inseparable parts of the definition of intelligence. Likewise, learned encoders may determine how input data enter the character space, while the pairing on that space can be fixed by its geometric structure. This separation makes it possible to vary the representation, evaluation, and update mechanisms independently, subject to their compatibility. We illustrate the framework by realizing the central computations of several established architectures. Sequence-valued knowledge states equipped with inner-product pairings recover dot-product attention. Restricting evaluation and normalization to prefix states gives causal attention without changing the underlying pairing. Additive accumulation of rank-one contributions yields a knowledge world whose normalized readout recovers positive-feature linear attention, consistent with its recurrent formulation [KVPF20]. Low-rank tensor-valued characters, together with contractions that retain the separate atten-tion heads, provide a geometric realization of Tensor Product Attention [ZLY +25]. Finally, state spaces that stabilize in dimension support recurrent architectures: bilinear evaluations can generate accumu-lated interaction memories, while nonlinear evaluations can recover the affine-plus-activation recurrence of a vanilla RNN [SMH11]. These examples concern the core computational mechanisms; their incorpo-ration into complete models requires further choices of parametrization, layer composition, and output maps. The framework also makes several structural distinctions explicit. We call a knowledge world abelian 

when incorporating two characters in either order gives the same state. Starting from a fixed initial state, this condition makes the final accumulated knowledge insensitive to permutations of the input characters. We separately consider a placeholder-free condition, which requires insertion of a distinguished null character to commute with other updates. This condition does not, by itself, require null insertion to act as the identity. Causality is different from both properties: it concerns which information is available when an output is computed. An abelian accumulation rule can therefore support causal computations when each output is evaluated on the corresponding prefix. Distinguishing properties of storage, evaluation, and information availability helps clarify which architectural choices are responsible for particular computational behaviors. More generally, topology, differential geometry, and algebraic geometry offer spaces and operations that can be investigated within this framework. The aim is not merely to translate existing architectures into geometric terminology, but to make geometric constructions available as inputs to the design process. This perspective separates mathematical specification from computational and empirical validation. A geometric system satisfying the proposed axioms does not automatically determine a unique architecture, nor does it guarantee efficient implementation or effective learning. Parametrization, numerical stability, computational cost, and the interaction between layers remain essential design questions. Nevertheless, making the underlying structures explicit provides a way to formulate these questions and to compare candidate designs. We begin with an accessible description of the framework and its categorical formal-ization, then examine attention and recurrent architectures as examples, and finally explore geometric constructions that may lead to new computational attention layers.

2.1. An intuitive abstraction of the axioms. We begin by introducing a mathematical framework for describing how information is accumulated, processed, and evaluated. The basic objects are characters ,

knowledge states , processes , and intelligence . We first explain these notions in the language of Euclidean spaces, so that the framework can be understood without any background in abstract mathematics. Let A ⊆ Rd be a space of characters , together with a distinguished element 0 ∈ A, called the null character . A character represents an elementary piece of information. For example, in a language model, a character may represent a token embedding; in computer vision, it may represent a feature vector extracted from an image patch. Thus, the word “character” should be understood as a general unit of information, rather than necessarily a letter or symbol. For each nonnegative integer n, let An ⊆ Rdn be a space of knowledge states . Intuitively, An rep-resents all possible internal states of a system after receiving n characters. These spaces need not be Euclidean spaces themselves: they may be submanifolds or other geometric subsets of Rdn . Moreover, the dimensions dn are allowed to vary with n. Of course, we would like to let A1 = A so that the first stage of knowledge is just our character space. Now, to connect those stages, there should be a family of maps 

μn : A × An → An+1 ,

which describe how knowledge is updated upon receiving a new character. More precisely, given a current knowledge state x ∈ An and a character t ∈ A, the new state is 

x′ = μn(t, x ) ∈ An+1 .

Starting from an initial state x0 ∈ A0, a sequence of characters t1, . . . , t N therefore generates a sequence of knowledge states recursively: 

xn+1 = μn(tn+1 , x n).

This is a formalization of how information unit establish the knowledge. We are particularly interested in two properties of these updates. First, we call the system placeholder-free if 

μn+1 (0 , μ n(t, x )) = μn+1 (t, μ n(0 , x )) for every x ∈ An and t ∈ A. In other words, inserting a null character before or after another character leads to the same knowledge state. Notice that this condition does not require the null character to have no effect; it only requires its update to commute with other character updates. Second, we call the system abelian if 

μn+1 (s, μ n(t, x )) = μn+1 (t, μ n(s, x )) for every x ∈ An and s, t ∈ A. This means that the order in which two characters are incorporated does not affect the resulting knowledge state. Consequently, the final state obtained from a fixed initial state depends only on the multiset of input characters, rather than their order. A simple example is obtained by taking A = An = Rd×d for all n, with 

μn(t, x ) = x + t. 

Since matrix addition is commutative, this system is abelian. More generally, the maps μn may involve nonlinear transformations or geometric constraints, and need not satisfy the abelian property. Abelian systems are natural candidates for tasks involving unordered information, such as learning from sets of feature vectors. The RNN formulas describe the stabilized stages n ≥ 2, with initial stages as defined in the preceding constructions. An inspiring aspect of this correspondence is the possibility of designing new neural network architec-tures by constructing suitable geometric spaces. Topology, differential geometry, and algebraic geometry offer a wealth of structures to explore. This framework therefore provides a potential route for applying the objects and tools of modern mathematics to neural network design. A possible design strategy within this framework proceeds as follows. Given a learning task involving data such as text, music, images, or motion, we first identify the relevant structural features of the data and choose geometric models that reflect them. For example, when the task does not depend on input order, abelian knowledge worlds provide a natural starting point. We then equip the chosen knowledge worlds with suitable update maps and intelligence, expressed through attention pairings, and use these structures to construct and test individual layers. Finally, we assemble promising layers into a model, specifying how information is passed between them. The process can thus be summarized as Real-world data Geometric knowledge spaces and intelligence Layer designs Model design For mathematicians, a natural problem is to construct geometric systems ( An, μ n, w n) satisfying the relevant axioms. Each such system may suggest a candidate for a new attention-based architecture, whose computational feasibility and effectiveness can then be investigated. We now present several geometric constructions satisfying our axioms. The design of attention layers based on these constructions is left for future work. 4.1. Further geometric knowledge worlds.
