Abstract 

Long-horizon tasks are important for modern AI and robotics, yet pose a challenge for re-inforcement learning. Historically, a popular approach has been to decompose a task into subgoals, and learn a subgoal-conditioned policy to execute each subtask in sequence. We introduce an alternative called Value Disaggregation (V ADAR ), which trains a single subgoal-independent policy to maximize a linear combination of per-subgoal critic values. Because subgoal information is needed only at training-time , V ADAR removes the need for possi-bly costly or cumbersome subgoal-selection during policy deployment. Moreover, through careful experiments, we show that V ADAR (1) is always competitive with, and frequently 

outperforms , natural baselines like goal-conditioning and (2) succeeds on tasks with non-sequential goal structure and multiple optimization objectives. In particular, V ADAR is the 

first algorithm to solve challenging, long-horizon tasks in the R OBOCASA 365 suite, even when starting from a policy pretrained from behavior cloning with near-zero initial task suc-cess. Taken together, V ADAR ’s successes suggest that subgoal information benefits actor-critic reinforcement learning by enhancing the efficacy of critic learning , whereas policy goal-conditioning is often unnecessary. 

1 Introduction 

Learning to complete long-horizon, multi-step tasks is a fundamental challenge across a number of core AI domains, from robotics (Gupta et al., 2019), to language modeling (Wei et al., 2022), to chemical reaction design (Liu et al., 2023b). A popular approach, often referred to as hierarchical goal conditioning (HGC) in reinforcement learning literature, decomposes these complex, long-horizon tasks into a set of subgoals, arXiv:2610.26629v1 [cs.LG] 19 Oct 2026 each of which can be executed in sequence by a dedicated learned, goal-conditioned policy (Gupta et al., 2019; Nasiriany et al., 2019; Levy et al., 2019; Nachum et al., 2018). However, attempting to learn per-sub-task policies introduces a new set of challenges. Myopic completion of a given sub-task might compromise performance on future sub-tasks. Moreover, explicit decompositions often require sub-tasks to be sequential in nature, and necessitate a dedicated sub-task planner to decide the current subtask to be attempted during policy deployment. In this paper, we propose an alternative to HGC that we call Value-Disaggregation (V ADAR ), a framework for sample-efficient reinforcement learning on long-horizon multi-step tasks. We focus on continuous control robotic manipulation tasks that can be decomposed into sub-tasks g1, . . . , g |G|. These subgoal evaluations are rapidly becoming highly reliable with today’s VLMs (Du et al., 2023). While HGC learns policies π(s, g )

that take sub-goals g as explicit input, V ADAR instead learns a sub-goal agnostic policy π(s), with a critic that estimates the values of all sub-tasks simultaneously. The policy is then trained to maximize a linear combination of critic values, optimizing for an aggregate performance on current-sub-goals as well as future tasks. We stress that V ADAR is a generic algorithmic template, being compatible with any preferred choice of critic learning algorithm or policy extraction method; we depict V ADAR in Figure 1. VADAR removes the need for subgoal-evaluation at inference by design . Thus, compute-intensive VLM calls used to delineate subgoals, need only be made at training time , and not during policy rollouts. Moreover, VADAR is flexible across both task structure and reward constraints, enabling activations both to safety-constrained reinforcement learning (Appendix K.1), and to non-sequential task orders which could admit a directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure. (Appendix G). Across a variety of benchmarks for continuous control and policy extraction methods, we find that V ADAR , (1) 

Consistently outperforms HGC (Section 4), hindsight experience replay (HER) (Andrychowicz et al., 2018), and dense preference-based rewards (Brown et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2026); (2) Enables the first non-VLA BC-to-online RL method to converge on the evaluated long-horizon R OBOCASA 365 tasks (Nasiriany et al., 2026) under fixed initial-state conditions; (3) Enables multi-objective reinforcement learning by incorporating safety constraints (Appendix K.1), and enables test-time steering (Section 6.3) by combining critic learning with best-of-N sampling (Li et al., 2026); and (4) Works seamlessly with VLM supervision (Appendix I) and reaches convergence when goals are specified by reasoning Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This allows sample efficient robot learning from high-dimensional visual observations. To understand these gains, our ablations examine dense subgoal supervision and future-aware policy extraction (Section 6.2). We find that future-aware policy extraction reduces myopic behavior, while intermediate sub-goal supervision improves learning even when policy extraction uses only final-subgoal values. This suggests that subgoals can support long-horizon RL through critic learning without explicit policy goal conditioning. Taken together, our findings invert the traditional perspectives on overcoming the challenges of long horizon, valued-based reinforcement learning. In short, we find: Rather than myopically conditioning policies via de-composed long-horizon sub-goals, predicting temporally rich subgoal successes can provide critics with dense supervision to enhance the accuracy of long-reaching measurements of full-task success. 

2 Related Work 

Long-horizon reinforcement learning via temporal abstraction. Long-horizon reinforcement learning is commonly addressed by introducing temporal abstraction: instead of reasoning over primitive actions, the agent reasons over extended behaviors, skills, or subgoals. Classical formulations include the options frame-work, where temporally extended actions are specified by initiation sets, intra-option policies, and termination conditions (Sutton et al., 1999; Bacon et al., 2016); hierarchical abstract machines (Parr & Russell, 1997); feudal reinforcement learning (Dayan & Hinton, 1992; Vezhnevets et al., 2017); and MAXQ value decom-position (Dietterich, 1999). Subsequent work studies how such skills and hierarchies can be discovered from data (Fox et al., 2017; Evans & Özgür ¸ Sim¸ sek, 2024; Cannon & Özgür Simsek, 2025; Peng et al., 2022). While these methods differ in how subtasks are specified or learned, they use subgoal structure to organize policy execution: the learned policy must either act through a prescribed hierarchy of subtasks(Dietterich, 1999; Parr & Russell, 1997) or utilize temporally extended controllers (Bacon et al., 2016; Fox et al., 2017). In contrast, VADAR assumes that subgoals are part of the MDP design, but does not learn or execute a hierarchy over them; subgoals are used only to construct training-time value targets for a single flat policy. 

Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning. Goal conditioned RL provide a natural design for conditioning on desired outcomes/sub-goals. Instead of learning a separate controller for each task, goal-conditioned methods learn policies or value functions indexed by a goal, enabling transfer across related goal-reaching problems. Classical dynamic-goal learning first studied transfer across changing goal-reaching tasks (Kaelbling, 1993). This idea was later extended through universal value function approximators, which learn goal-indexed value 2functions with function approximation (Schaul et al., 2015), and hindsight relabeling, which reuses failed roll-outs as successful examples for alternative achieved goals (Andrychowicz et al., 2018). However, conditioning a flat policy on the final task goal does not by itself resolve long-horizon credit assignment. For this reason, hi-erarchical goal-conditioned RL (HGC) uses goal conditioning as a local control primitive: a high-level policy, planner, or task specification provides intermediate subgoals, and a low-level goal-conditioned policy learns to reach them (Nachum et al., 2018; Levy et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2020; Nair et al., 2018). This reduces the effective horizon, but introduce additional dependencies: they often assume a sequential subgoal structure, treat only one subgoal as active at a time, and require the deployed policy to be supplied with the correct current goal. These issues are costly and difficult when subgoal progress and completion must be evaluated by expert rules or large vision-language models during inference. V ADAR avoids this test-time dependency by learning a subgoal-agnostic policy; goal information enters only through the critic during training. 

Value decomposition and reward-factorized critics. MAXQ decomposes the value of a hierarchical policy into child-task values and completion functions, reflecting the procedural structure of a subroutine hierar-chy (Dietterich, 1999). Successor representations and successor features instead factor value prediction into predictive components and reward-dependent weights (Dayan, 1993; Barreto et al., 2017). VADAR follows this latter spirit of reward-factorized value estimation, but uses subgoal-completion signals to learn a vector-valued critic in an offline-to-online actor-critic pipeline. Closer to robotics, task-and-motion planning (TAMP) couples high-level symbolic task planners with low-level continuous motion planners over a fixed subtask vo-cabulary (Kaelbling & Lozano-Pérez, 2011; Garrett et al., 2021). V ADAR shares the assumption that the task admits a subgoal-level decomposition, but does not plan over symbolic actions or commit to a fixed sequencing at execution time: the decomposition enters only through the critic, and the deployed policy is a single flat, subgoal-agnostic controller. 

Representation learning for value-based RL. Recent work has shown that auxiliary prediction objectives can improve value learning by shaping the representations used by the critic. MR.Q (Fujimoto et al., 2025) learns state and state-action representations that predict rewards, termination, and future latent states, using model-based objectives to improve a model-free actor–critic without planning through the learned dynam-ics. DR.Q (Lyu et al., 2026) builds on this approach by encouraging the learned representations to retain information about future states through a mutual-information objective, together with a recency-aware replay scheme. VADAR is complementary to these approaches: rather than introducing an auxiliary dynamics or representation-learning objective, it obtains additional supervision directly from the RL problem by fitting sub-goal value functions at multiple temporal resolutions . Thus, the shared critic representation is trained simulta-neously by intermediate and long-horizon TD targets, which we find can improve final-task value estimation even when the intermediate values are not used for policy extraction.

Sparse-Reward RL. In many tasks, such as those encountered in robot manipulation, defining reward that densely measure progress towards goal completion is challenging (Amodei et al., 2016; Krakovna et al., 2020). Thus, we consider a sparse-reward defined via the done indicator done ∈ { 0, 1} indicating task completion. Standard practice is to set r ← rsparse (done) , where 

rsparse (done ) = −1 · I{done = 0 } + rdone I{done = 1 }, (3.2) and where rdone is a small positive constant (e.g. rdone = 5 ). In words, rsparse penalizes each time-step until the task is not completed, and provides a small positive bonus for task success. 

Offline-to-Online RL. In many robotics settings, environment interaction is expensive and a fixed dataset of demonstrations Doff is available. We follow the now-standard offline-to-online recipe (Nair et al., 2021): pretrain a behavior-cloning policy πBC on Doff , then initialize the online policy from πBC and fine-tune online, optionally mixing Doff into the off-policy updates at a fixed ratio (Ball et al., 2023). All algorithms in our experiments share the same BC-pretraining and warm-start protocol, so comparisons isolate the online fine-tuning method. 

Dense Preference Reward. An alternative approach to long-horizon RL is to learn dense preference-based rewards. We describe the formulation and implementation in Appendix E and compare against this approach in Section 6 and Appendix F.2.

Limitation 1 : Accurate goals must be supplied to the policy at test-time, which is prohibitive if sub-goal completions requires expert engineering or larger models to evaluate. Limitation 2 : Optimizing for the current goal can be overly myopic if future actions depend subtly on how the current goal is completed, such as in robotic pick and place tasks. As a point of comparison, we introduce implicit hierarchical goal conditioning (iHGC), which retains goal-conditioned critics but uses a subgoal-independent policy π(a | s),removing the need for test-time goal information (Limitation 1). Although iHGC can outperform traditional HGC as shown in Fig. 10, both optimize the policy against the current-subgoal critic alone and remain sus-ceptible to myopic behavior (Limitation 2). This motivates V ADAR , which incorporates future-subgoal values during policy optimization.

We now introduce Value Disaggregation (V ADAR , Algorithm 2), a framework for subgoal-driven long-horizon RL that addresses the limitations of goal conditioning described in Section 4. VADAR combines three key insights : (1) Like implicit hierarchical goal conditioning (iHGC), goal-conditioned critics can train a single, subgoal-independent policy. (2) Leveraging weighted combinations of critics can balance current and future goal completion, thereby reducing the myopic overexploitation described in Limitation 2. Finally, (3) The extraction from multiple critics is naturally compatible with general goal topologies, addressing Limitation 3 by allowing more than one subgoal to be active at any given time.

6 Experiments 

We evaluate V ADAR on a suite of long-horizon manipulation benchmarks. Each method is pretrained via behavior cloning on offline data, stopped early at a low success rate so all methods begin fine-tuning from a comparable starting point, and then fine-tuned online for a fixed number of environment steps. Per-task descriptions and hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix H.

(plotted in a solid line) and V ADAR -CF, which use current-future (plotted in a dotted line). We compare against 3 alternatives, each using the same base-algorithm per plot: (1) Hierarchical Goal Conditioning (HGC), where both the policy and critic are conditioned on the current subgoal g, where g is set to the next incomplete subgoal at every step (Algorithm 1). (2) Implicit Hierarchical Goal Conditioning (iHGC), which retains the goal conditioned critic of HGC but does not condition the policy on the subgoal. (3) Dense Preference Reward , denoted Dense, where we adopt best-practices for a learning dense reward model via preferences (described in Appendix E ), where the reward models are pretrained with additional rollouts on a richer dataset of successful and failed runs in order to steelman their performance. (4) The original base-algorithm that using sparse reward with no goal information, denoted as “Sparse”.

Dense Subgoal Supervision Improves Critic Learning . To further test whether dense supervision across multiple temporal resolutions improves critic learning, we ask a stricter question: can intermediate subgoal supervision improve the final-task critic even when those intermediate values are never used for policy extrac-tion? We introduce V ADAR LAST -Q, which trains the same multi-goal critic as V ADAR (Section 5) but extracts the policy using only the final-subgoal value Qϕ(s, a, g |G|). Thus, V ADAR LAST -Q uses the same policy ob-jective as the Sparse baseline, while additionally supervising the critic on intermediate subgoals. Figure 7 shows that V ADAR LAST -Q approaches V ADAR -NF on several tasks and substantially outperforms Sparse on the R OBOCASA 365 benchmarks. Because the policy objective is unchanged, these gains isolate the effect of denser critic supervision. Full results across all benchmarks and policy-extraction methods are provided in Appendix M. This suggests that intermediate subgoal targets improve the critic even when their values are not used directly for policy extraction. 

Effect of the policy extraction method. While V ADAR LAST -Q sometimes matches V ADAR -NF, the size of this gap depends on the policy-extraction method. V ADAR LAST -Q is generally closer to V ADAR -NF under 

OGPO , where the critic-derived signal supervises every denoising step of the generative policy. In FQL , where the critic provides less dense supervision than OGPO , V ADAR LAST -Q alone is not sufficient, and Next-future weighting provided additional gains. Takeaway: Intermediate supervision improves final-task learning VADAR LAST -Q, which uses V ADAR for critic supervision, but only supervises on the final subgoal , im-proves over Sparse on the long-horizon R OBOCASA 365 tasks even though intermediate subgoal values never enter its policy objective directly. This supports our hypothesis that dense subgoal supervision im-proves critic learning. Next-future weighting provides additional gains where final-subgoal extraction alone remains insufficient.

7 Limitations and Conclusion 

This work introduces V ADAR , an approach to critic learning that utilizes subtask decomposition, which pro-vides significant improvements across a wide range of policy extraction algorithms as well as challenging environment specifications such as long horizon or safety filtering. Still, a limitation of V ADAR is that it defin-ing a fixed DAG structure of subtasks, whereas other methods enable conditioning on a continuum of goal information (Black et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2026). We look forward to generalizing the design principles of V ADAR to this continuous or combinatorially large goal spaces, such as those described by images or text, in future work. Moreover, while recent literature has focused on improved methods for policy extrac-tion, particularly with modern expressive policy parameterizations (Ren et al., 2024; Patil et al., 2026; Park et al., 2025b; Dong et al., 2025), V ADAR demonstrates that there remains significant untapped potential for algorithmic improvements to critic learning, particularly in long-horizon tasks. Moreover, in light of the recent popularity of scaling test-time computation (Snell et al., 2024), our study suggests a complimentary scaling direction: scaling the density of training signals used to supervise learning, which not only improves learning efficacy, but also reduces the need for complex test-time computation (e.g. VLM queries for goal-selection) at deployment.

Here we give a short hands-on reference for applying V ADAR to a new task or base algorithm. Subgoal decomposition. Most of the long horizon tasks can be decomposed naturally into subtasks which should be taked as the subgoals for V ADAR . For robotics tasks, we believe that the subgoals defined by us for our environments (Appendix H) can serve as good examples for subgoal selections for a new task. 

Weighting variant. From our experiments, we found V ADAR -NF to be the most performative variant for robotic manipulation tasks, and recommend it as the default. V ADAR -CF can train slightly faster early on when the first subgoal is hard to reach from BC initialization, at the cost of greater susceptibility to over-exploiting the current goal. The myopic variants are useful as ablations but not as defaults. 

Adapting to a new base algorithm. VADAR is a wrapper over any Value Based RL Algorithm. Given any value-based RL algorithm A, the required modifications are: 1. Change critic output dim from 1 to |G| (backbone unchanged); ensemble aggregation becomes entry-wise. 2. Store per-step completion vectors dt and per-head rewards rt in the replay buffer. 3. Replace the critic loss with its summed-over-heads version; broadcast any Lreg across heads (see Appendix C.1 for more details). 4. Apply the entrywise reward clip in the TD target. 5. In the policy loss, replace Qϕ(s, a ) with Qagg (s, a ) = ⟨w(d(s)) , ⟨Qϕ(s, a, g ), g ∈ G⟩⟩ .The policy is not conditioned on subgoals. We have applied this recipe unchanged to SAC, FQL, and OGPO (Appendix C), and these serve as good references for applying V ADAR to any new Value Based RL Algorithm. 

B Critic Parameterizations, Ensembling, and Target Networks 

This section expands the brief mention in Section 3 of critic ensembling and target exponential moving averages (EMAs), which are standard practices in modern off-policy value-based RL and which we adopt without mod-ification in V ADAR . The recipes below apply uniformly across the base instantiations used in our experiments; specific details that interact with the policy update are covered in Appendix C. 

B.1 Critic Parameterizations Vector-valued multi-goal critic. As an alternative to the goal-conditioned critic Qϕ(s, a, g ), V ADAR can use a multi-goal critic Qϕ : S × A → RG that maps each state-action pair to a |G|-dimensional vector of Q-values. The g-th entry Qϕ[g]( s, a ) estimates the value of the state-action pair under the MDP induced by the per-subgoal reward rg . Crucially, all |G| entries share a single network backbone, enabling representation sharing across subgoals: features learned for predicting one subgoal’s completion are available to all others, Both parameterizations provide dense supervision across subgoals; Appendix P compares them empirically. Appendix N shows the vector-valued critic can be interpreted simply as a structured instantiation of successor features (Barreto et al., 2017), where each feature corresponds to subgoal performance.

In Figure 11(a), the policy places the kettle on the tray in a position that obstructs the subsequent mug place-ment. The “placing the kettle" subgoal is complete, but attempting to place the mug causes a collision that knocks the kettle off the counter. In Figure 11(b), the policy places the pan in the sink in a position that pre-vents stable placement of the sponge. Both these failure cases are manifestations of over-optimizing for the current subtask (Limitation 2). The policy does not distinguish between merely completing the current subgoal 

and completing it in a way that facilitates future subgoals . Our method, V ADAR , addresses this limitation by optimizing the policy for future-subgoal values (Section 5), providing a learning signal for actions that enable progress toward full-task completion rather than just myopically completing the current subtask.

E.1 Background and Limitations 

An alternative to HGC for solving the credit assignment problem is to craft a denser reward signal r. When oracle dense rewards are not available, the most popular data-dependent method has been preference-based dense rewards defined at the level of full trajectories τ = ( s1, a 1, . . . , s H , a H ). Preference based rewards have shown strong promise (Wirth et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2023; Tajwar et al., 2024), but are subject to other limitations: (1) assigning consistent numerical progress values on very long, multi-step tasks is challenging; (2) statistical errors in reward learning can lead to reward hacking; and (3) effective reward learning can require large datasets. Dense rewards are imperfect proxies for desired behavior, and agents may exploit errors in the learned reward rather than complete the task (Amodei et al., 2016; Krakovna et al., 2020). Online Training After offline reward-model pretraining, we continue updating the dense reward model during online RL following (Wang et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2021). The reward model is trained using the following iterative loop: the current reward model is used to train the policy, the resulting policy collects new trajectories, these trajectories are converted into additional synthetic preference comparisons, and the reward model is fine-tuned on the offline and online preference dataset. During online RL, the environment reward is replaced by the learned state-only reward model ˆrθ (s). The underlying RL algorithm is otherwise unchanged: OGPO , SAC , and FQL use their standard actor and critic losses, but with rewards given by ˆrθ instead of the environment reward. 22 Every Kint environment steps, the reward model is fine-tuned on the union of offline and online preference pairs using the same Bradley–Terry objective as in offline pretraining. The reward model gradually adapts to the on-policy state distribution induced by the improving policy, while still being regularized by the original offline preference data. After each reward-model update, we relabel the live RL replay buffer with the updated reward model: rt ←

ˆrθ (st). This step makes the online reward update immediately affect subsequent policy learning.

F Additional Baseline Comparisons 

F.1 Hindsight experience replay (HER) struggles with contact-rich control 

An alternative approach to mitigating the credit assignment problem in goal-conditioned RL is hindsight ex-perience replay (HER) (Andrychowicz et al., 2018), which relabels states reached during a rollout as goals, so that the policy receives learning signal even when the task reward is never observed. HER has largely been evaluated in reaching, pick-and-place and maze settings, where any state is a reasonable goal. To test whether it applies to contact-rich manipulation settings, we evaluate HER with SAC on L IBERO -Task 6, with goals given by object states and relabelled from future states of the same episode, and with the same BC pretraining, online step budget and networks as our other SAC baselines. We also steelman HER with two variants that provide it with the same subgoal information that HGC receives: an oracle that supplies a representative goal state for each subgoal in turn, and a learned goal embedding trained to recognize subgoal completions. In both the variants, we find HER to be incapable of performing multi stage complex manipulation tasks, with the eventual success rate remaining quite close to the initial BC success rate.

G DAG Extension for Non-Sequential Subtasks 

Multiple subgoals may be active simultaneously, as in bimanual robotic manipulation. The value-weighting function in Table 1 extends naturally to this setting: a DAG can express both parallel subgoals and prerequisites that must be completed before a subsequent goal becomes available. In the sequential setting, subtasks must be completed in a fixed linear order. However, many real-world tasks have more complex structures where multiple subtasks can be pursued simultaneously for optimal behavior. Moreover, some subtasks can form a precondition boundary for others, where policies can be rolled out in the future subtasks only if the preconditions are satisfied. VADAR can naturally be applied to a litany of these subtask dependencies often represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with each node corresponding to an individual Q-function. This provides the core benefits of value disaggregation to rich dependency structures dominant in long-horizon RL settings. 

Illustrative Example: Robomimic Transport. To illustrate this, consider the robomimic Transport task, a multi-robot task where two robot arms must coordinate to transport a payload from one bin to another while managing trash removal. The task decomposes into 7 interdependent subtasks forming a DAG: 1. lid_removed : Lid removed from start bin 2. payload_grasped : Payload grasped from start bin (Robot 0) 3. payload_lifted : Payload lifted above bins (Robot 0) 4. trash_grasped : Trash grasped from target bin (Robot 1) 5. trash_in_bin : Trash placed in trash bin (Robot 1) 6. payload_handed_off : Payload transferred from Robot 0 to Robot 1 7. payload_in_target : Payload placed in target bin (SUCCESS) The dependencies form two parallel execution paths: (1 → 2 → 3) for payload handling by Robot 0, and 

(4 → 5) for trash management by Robot 1. The handoff step (6) requires both paths to complete, and the final success (7) depends on the handoff, an illustration is provided in Figure 14.

DAG Formalism. Recall from Section 4 that the subgoals G are partially ordered, where gi ≺ gj means that 

gi must be completed before gj . We represent this structure with a DAG D = ( G, E ), with 

(gi, g j ) ∈ E ⇐⇒ gi ≺ gj and ∄ gk ∈ G such that gi ≺ gk ≺ gj .

L.1 Setup 

We evaluate a critic by using it to select among candidate actions of a frozen policy. The policy is the BC-pretrained flow policy of OGPO , trained once and never updated. L.2 Critic Training 

Starting from the frozen BC policy, only the critic is trained; the policy is never updated against the critic. The replay buffer is first seeded with 20 ,000 environment steps of frozen-policy rollouts. N Connections to Successor Features and Reward Shaping 

VADAR ’s aggregation step (Eq. (5.1)) is structurally similar to a success feature decomposition of a Q-function. Classical success features assume reward is linear in a feature map ϕ : S × A → R|G| (Dayan, 1993; Barreto et al., 2017), r(s, a ) = ⟨w, ϕ(s, a )⟩ for some fixed w ∈ R|G|. The Q-function then inherits the same decompo-sition Qπ (s, a ) = ⟨w, ψπ (s, a )⟩, where ψ is the successor feature, which represents the expected discounted 36 sum of returns under policy π. Crucially as motivated by (Barreto et al., 2017), this allows efficient policy extraction from arbitrary reward weightings without retraining policies across tasks. VADAR instantiates the reward features ϕ(s, a ) as per-subgoal rewards ϕg (s, a ) = rg (s, a ) so the succes-sor features are precisely the per-subgoal Q-values Qϕg (s, a ) = ψπg (s, a ). The weight vector w(s) =

GET WEIGHTING (d(s)) is a state-dependent current subgoal completion vector. This allows V ADAR to re-cover sufficient statistics for evaluating the entire ancestral tree of weighted objectives in a single multi-step RL training run with w(s) as the contraction operator.

This identity applies to fixed weights and exact Q-functions. It does not establish equivalence between VADAR ’s state-dependent weighting and clipped, approximate critic updates and learning a scalar critic on an aggregated reward. The experiments in Section 6.2 and Appendix O.1 distinguish reward aggregation from the benefits of dense critic supervision. Reward shaping does not explain Vadar’s success It might be tempting to attribute V ADAR ’s success to reward shaping. However, in Section 6.2, we find that even using the final subgoal critic can lead to strong performance in many benchmarks. We conclude that VADAR ’s benefits come as much from representation learning induced by denser critic supervision as they do from explicit reward shaping during policy extraction. 

O.1 Subgoal Reward Shaping Does Not Reliably Enable Long-Horizon Learning

This identity does not guarantee equivalent learning with state-dependent weights or clipped, approximate critics. Q.2 The MIQ Critic 

VADAR ’s vector critic synergizes with iterative critic parameterizations: each iteration can propagate infor-mation across subgoal heads, so the second iteration’s prediction for one subgoal can be conditioned on the first iteration’s prediction for the others. We instantiate this with MIQ, a critic-side adaptation of the Minimal Iterative Policy of Pan et al. (2026) that retains the two-step iteration with t⋆ = 0 .9.

Q.2.1 Architecture 

A MIQ critic is a single MLP Qϕ : S × A × R × [0 , 1] → R that takes a state-action pair (s, a ), a scalar z that plays the role of the noise variable in a flow-matching linear interpolant, and a time index t ∈ [0 , 1] . To recall the flow-matching analogy: a flow model is trained to map the linear interpolant It = t x + (1 − t) z between Gaussian noise z and a clean target x at time t ∈ [0 , 1] back to x, so that at t = 0 the input is pure noise and as 

t → 1 it approaches the target. MIP (Pan et al., 2026) retains this interpolant but supervises the network with the same target twice, at t = 0 and at t = t⋆ (with t⋆ = 0 .9), using a regression loss instead of a velocity-matching loss. MIQ is the same construction with the supervision target x instantiated as the scalar Q-target 

ytarget : the flow-matching interpolant is replaced by an interpolant in Q-value space, and z ∼ Unif[ −σ, σ ] is the noise variable that lives on the noisy end of that interpolant. At inference, MIQ runs two autoregressive steps: 

ˆq0 ← Qϕ(s, a, z, t = 0) , ˆq1 ← Qϕ(s, a, ˆq0, σ/ 10) , t ⋆), (Q.1) i.e., the second iteration substitutes its own iteration-zero output ˆq0 in place of an interpolated input, since ˆq0

has been trained to approximate ytarget .

MIQ introduces an explicit dependence between successive value estimates: its second pass conditions every subgoal prediction on the full first-pass vector ˆq0. The comparison therefore tests the additional benefit of iterative refinement beyond shared subgoal supervision; comparable success rates do not establish that the non-iterative critic performs the same internal computation. 41
