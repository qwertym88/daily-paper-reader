Abstract 

Model-based reinforcement learning in partially observable environments requires inferring hidden states, learning latent dynamics, and planning under the resulting belief. Practical algorithms often solve one aspect of the above pipeline while relying on approximations for the others that are often poorly understood, while existing POMDP theory typically analyzes computationally inefficient theoretical algorithms far from modern deep RL practice. To fill this gap, we propose a practical end-to-end algorithm that approximately solves the hidden state filtering problem via sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), updates a latent dynamics model online, and performs approximate online planning for d steps before bootstrapping leaves with a state-value critic (a QMDP approximation). We analyze each approximation and prove an end-to-end regret bound decomposing the error into model estimation error, particle filter error, Monte Carlo rollout error, and a structural bias term induced by the QMDP approximation controllable by the planning depth that we further analyze in various special cases. The resulting guarantee makes explicit how statistical sample size, particle budget, rollout budget, and planning depth affect performance. We instantiate our algorithm in an MPC-based deep RL framework, demonstrating its practicality on a variety of environments. On a POMDP that satisfies our assumptions, each term of the bound decays at its predicted rate. Our results provide a practical and theoretically grounded route for bridging theory and practice within online decision-making in POMDPs. 

# 1 Introduction 

Partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) provide a standard framework for se-quential decision making under latent state uncertainty [19]. They are a natural model for problems in robotics, healthcare, recommendation, and other settings [17, 33] where the learner must act using only indirect and noisy observations of the underlying system. In principle, optimal control in a POMDP can be reduced to planning in the belief-state MDP, where the belief is the posterior distribution over latent states given the observation history [4, 19]. This makes model-based RL especially natural in POMDPs: to act under a belief, the learner must already learn a model rich enough to perform filtering, and this same model can be used for planning. However, the belief state reduction does not remove the main computational and learning difficulties. An online model-based decision maker must simultaneously (i) learn the unknown model, (ii) maintain a posterior over latent states online, and (iii) plan over this continuous belief space. These three tasks– learning, inference, and planning –interact in a way that makes exact online control in POMDPs intractable in both computation and sample complexity in general [32] without special structure, motivating a large literature on approximate methods for each component individually. Existing methods typically make one component of the above POMDP pipeline tractable while leaving the others either computationally expensive or theoretically uncontrolled. For instance, 1arXiv:2610.27575v1 [cs.LG] 20 Oct 2026 on the planning side, Monte Carlo tree search has led to scalable online POMDP solvers such as Partially Observable Monte Carlo Planning (POMCP), sparse tree search, and progressive widening methods [26, 34, 36]. However, these are primarily planning algorithms under a known generative model, and do not natively provide end-to-end learning guarantees for unknown latent dynamics. A more aggressive approximation is QMDP, which replaces the belief-MDP continuation value by the fully observed MDP value averaged under the current belief [27]. QMDP is computationally efficient but can introduce substantial bias. Existing theory gives limited guidance on when this bias is small or how it can be mitigated. Regarding inference, particle filters, a.k.a. sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), provide a flexible approximation to Bayesian filtering in nonlinear non-Gaussian systems [8, 38], but SMC theory for POMDPs [25, 26] has not been fully integrated with approximate online planning and model learning. On learning, recent theoretical work has made significant progress on sample-efficient model estimation for POMDPs. [28, 29] develop Optimistic MLE as a generic model-based algorithm with polynomial sample complexity for a broad class of POMDPs. These results establish statistical feasibility under exact or abstract planning oracles. They do not specify how to perform approximate filtering or planning with the learned model in a computationally practical way. A separate practical line of work uses recurrent policies or differentiable planning modules. Recurrent model-free deep RL methods, such as GRU or LSTM-based PPO and Q-learning variants, can perform well empirically under partial observability, but their hidden states are difficult to interpret as beliefs and are hard to analyze theoretically. QMDP-net takes a more structured approach by embedding approximate Bayesian filtering and QMDP planning into a differentiable architecture [22]. This is closer in spirit to our goal: combine model learning, belief state inference, and planning in a way that resembles modern deep learning practice, while maintaining provable guarantees. However, the approximation error of learned variational or differentiable filtering is difficult to control. Our view is that using SMC as the filtering primitive yields a more principled bridge: it is compatible with learned latent dynamics and has well-developed stability and approximation theory [8, 38]. In summary, existing work advances planning, inference, or learning separately, but no current method provides a unified pipeline that combines all three with both computational practicality and end-to-end theoretical guarantees. This leaves open the central question 

Can we design a practical model-based deep RL pipeline for POMDPs that still admits end-to-end theoretical guarantees? 

1.1 Our Contributions 

This paper develops a practical and analyzable route from POMDP theory to model-based deep RL. We answer the central question by developing a model-based learning-inference-planning pipeline for POMDPs called PF-MCTQ that is compatible with deep RL practice. Our contributions are: 1. A practical inference-learning-planning pipeline. We propose an online algorithm that learns a latent dynamics model, tracks the current belief with particles, and plans under this belief. Each is modular and can be instantiated with standard deep RL primitives. 2. A controlled alternative to full belief-tree planning. Instead of treating the particle belief as the planning state, we plan particle-wise and average action-values of the fully observable MDP. This gives a practical middle ground between expensive belief-tree search and cheap but potentially biased QMDP. Our analysis shows that the bias decays geometrically after d steps of belief-MDP lookahead. For cheaper state-level planners, we show that QMDP is exact for the belief-linear component of the value, with error only from an uncertainty-sensitive residual, clarifying when the approximation is small. 2Figure 1: Particle beliefs for the unobserved velocity of a double integrator LQR over the last 1000 steps. Mean, 10th and 90th quantiles visualized. Beliefs track the state when the system is stable. 3. An end-to-end regret guarantee. We prove a regret bound that decomposes performance loss into model estimation error, particle filtering error, Monte Carlo rollout error, and QMDP structural bias. The bound makes explicit how sample size, particle budget, rollout budget, and planning depth jointly control performance. 4. A bridge to deep RL practice. Compared with theoretical POMDP learning algorithms that rely on exact POMDP solvers or posterior sampling over parameters, our approach uses MPC-style online planning, approximate filtering, and critic bootstrapping. These are standard primitives in model-based deep RL, while remaining explicit enough to support theoretical analysis. We instantiate our algorithm in an MPC-based deep RL implementation and evaluate it on various POMDP environments, demonstrating its practicality. On a POMDP satisfying our assumptions, each term of the bound decays at its predicted rate (Section 5). Overall, the paper argues that particle filtering can serve as a practical bridge between POMDP theory and deep RL. It gives a belief representation that is explicit enough to analyze, flexible enough for nonlinear latent dynamics, and compatible with MPC-style planning and learned critics. This yields a route to online POMDP learning that is closer to modern model-based RL than exact belief-space planning, while providing a transparent theoretical account that lets users trace each source of approximation error and make informed design choices. Section 2 introduces the online learning problem and the regret criterion in the belief-state MDP. Section 3 presents the PF-MCTQ algorithm, describing the MCTQ planning subroutine and the full online pipeline. Section 4 develops the error analysis: we decompose the regret into four traceable terms, analyze the structural QMDP bias and its variance interpretation, bound each error source, and combine them into the end-to-end regret guarantee. We conclude with case studies on LQG, contextual MDPs, and smooth-value POMDPs that illustrate when the QMDP approximation is benign.

4.1 Assumptions and main result 

Modern SMC analyses [6, 9, 21] impose rather unavoidable but stringent conditions on the transition kernel and observation density. We inherit these in the form of the following Doeblin and bounded ob-servation density assumption (Assumption 1) on the latent dynamics. This choice aligns with our aim to provide an end-to-end regret bound that decomposes error across the learning–inference–planning pipeline, rather than to develop optimal exploration methods for POMDP model estimation, as Assumption 1 ensures that simple exploration strategies like ε-greedy have sufficient coverage. At the same time, the Doeblin condition resembles commonplace strong mixing assumptions within the RL literature [13, 23, 39, 40] and the α-revealing condition commonplace in the POMDP literature plays a similar role to the bounded observation density condition [28, 29].

Technical challenges. Bounding each source in equation 12 under online learning raises three difficulties. First, controlling (B1) requires working with a test-function class defined directly over S,rather than only the parametric family {Q⋆,B θ }θ. This is because the belief-space value functions may lack the regularity needed for uniform particle-filter error control. In fact, the planning-induced class over multiple steps is effectively uncountable over the possible model parameters, so we control it through truncation. The required truncation level depends on the desired particle approximation accuracy: as the particle error target decreases, the tail must be made smaller, which enlarges the truncated function class. This creates a tradeoff between the complexity of the truncated class and the particle ensemble size, leading to a slower ( 1/J 1/2κpf ), essentially nonparametric, rate. Second, the filtering analysis must handle data generated online by a changing policy. Classical particle-filter stability assumes a fixed generative model, whereas here the filter receives actions from a non-stationary, improving policy. We extend time-uniform Lp bounds [9] to this setting by exploiting the Doeblin condition uniformly over actions. Third, Doeblin coverage applies to states but not actions, so we ensure action coverage via ε-greedy exploration. This lets us transfer MLE concentration across policies, controlling terms (A) and (C) and the Monte Carlo error in (B2). 

Remark 1 (Relation to POMDP hardness) . POMDP sample complexity may in the worst case scale exponentially in the effective horizon 1/(1 − γ). Our bound is polynomial in 1/(1 − γ) for two reasons. First, under the Doeblin condition the Bayes filter forgets its initialization at a geometric rate ρ = 1 − εmix gmin /g max < 1 uniformly over actions, so errors add up over time rather than compound. Second, bounded observation densities play the role of the α-revealing condition [28]: they bound importance weights, so data transfer across policies without an (|A| /ε )H blow-up, and particle weights stay non-degenerate. This does not circumvent the lower bound: ε−1mix and gmax /g min can be exponential in the effective horizon for hard instances, and Cest and Cpf depend polynomially on them. Appendix F illustrates failures when gmin = 0 and when Ξθ is large.

# 6 Discussion 

We presented an online model-based POMDP pipeline combining particle filtering, latent-dynamics learning, and approximate planning with an end-to-end regret bound. We decompose regret into model estimation, particle approximation, Monte Carlo planning, and QMDP structural errors, clarifying how statistical and computational budgets affect performance. The framework bridges POMDP theory and model-based deep RL. Experiments with a simplified rollout-based version show that it can be made operational and competitive in low-sample partially observed control. 

Limitations. Our analysis relies on the Doeblin mixing condition and bounded observation densities, which ensure passive state coverage and obviate the need for strategic exploration. These assumptions fail in many POMDPs. In item search with exact observations, the observation density is not bounded below, and a particle set of size J misses the true location among K with probability 

(1 − 1/K )J . In Light–Dark-type problems, information must be actively gathered, so Ξθ is large and shallow planning never gathers it (see Appendix F). Second, the bounded reward assumption excludes settings such as LQG. Third, the deep RL experiments use d = 0 with a learned critic and actor, which is the QMDP case of our algorithm, and our guarantees apply to non-adaptive action selection inside the tree but not to UCT-style selection. Natural extensions are strategic exploration to relax the dynamics assumptions, unbounded rewards, and guarantees for adaptive tree search.

# A Further Related Work 

Our work is related to research on approximate planning in POMDPs, particle-based belief represen-tations, and inference in latent-state models. 

Planning in POMDPs. POMDP planning can be formulated as dynamic programming over the belief state [19], but exact planning is difficult because the belief space is continuous and must be updated online. Practical methods therefore rely on approximations. Sampling-based planners such as POMCP combine Monte-Carlo tree search with particle-based belief updates and scale to large problems without explicit belief-stateenumeration [26, 34], while progressive-widening methods extend this approach to continuous spaces [36]. Another common approximation is QMDP, which replaces belief-space continuation values with fully observed MDP values averaged under the current 15 belief [4, 27]. Our method is connected to this line of work through combining finite-depth MCTS with a QMDP-style bootstrap, but differs in that we study this approximation in an online learning setting and explicitly quantify the resulting structural error. POMCP, POWSS, and sparse PFT are online planners in that they replan from the current belief at every step. However, they are analyzed with the model given to the planner, so their analyses are not online learning results. The same holds for our planner when it is analyzed at a frozen θ. Their guarantees concern a single planning call at an exactly known θ, with no estimation error and no horizon T , so they do not directly yield a regret bound when the planner is called with a re-estimated ¯θk. Because the planner enters our decomposition only through ϵmc (t, d ), one could substitute such a planner by re-deriving that term (and term (B1), which a particle-belief MDP absorbs) under ¯θk. Planning on the particle-belief MDP also requires O(J) generative calls per node, compared with O(1) for a state-level simulation. 

Particle-based inference under partial observability. A related line of work uses particle filtering to approximate the belief state. Classical particle filtering theory provides convergence and stability guarantees for approximate Bayes filtering [8, 38]. [25] studied the particle-belief MDP directly and established optimality guarantees relative to the true belief-state MDP. More recent work has studied planning with particle methods in structured partially observed systems [11] as well as joint state and parameter inference in state-space models [5]. Our paper connects these directions in a different way: rather than planning over propagated particle beliefs, we use particles to represent the current belief, perform state-level lookahead from those particles, and combine this with online model learning. This leads to an end-to-end regret analysis that separates estimation error, particle approximation error, Monte-Carlo planning error, and QMDP-style approximation bias. 

Model-free approaches. Model-free alternatives instead avoid explicit filtering by giving the learner a short horizon lookback or memory [20, 31]. The latter are often effective in practice, especially with deep recurrent policies, but their hidden states are difficult to interpret as beliefs, and remain challenging to analyze. Existing sample complexity guarantees for the former often contain caveats, such as only quasipolynomial rather than polynomial sample complexity [14, 15], or non-standard assumptions [2]. We will focus on the model-based approach. 

# B MCTQ Planning Subroutine 

We describe the full MCTQ (Monte Carlo Tree search with QMDP leaves) planning subroutine referenced in Algorithm 1. MCTQ estimates the depth-d belief-tree action-value Qθ,d (bJ , a ) defined in equation 9 by Monte Carlo tree search on the particle belief. It is inspired by the POMCP framework [34], with three departures: internal actions are drawn uniformly at random rather than by UCB1, every simulation descends to the full depth d, and values are computed by a bottom-up dynamic programming (DP) backup after all simulations complete.
