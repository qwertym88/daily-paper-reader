## ABSTRACT 

Retargeting human motion to humanoids requires preserving motion seman-tics while accounting for the robot’s physical constraints. Kinematic pipelines prescribe a robot trajectory through geometric matching, without establishing whether it can be executed under the robot’s dynamics. Prior physics-supervised neural methods train bidirectional full-sequence models on physics-refined kine-matic trajectories, remaining tied to prescribed kinematic solutions and requir-ing noncausal context. We propose SMR, a streaming neural motion retargeter learned from semantic-conditioned physics experts. We convert human motion into robot-aware task-space targets for body landmark positions, orientations, and velocities. Physics experts track these targets directly in simulation under con-tact, dynamics, and actuator constraints. We train a single strictly causal Trans-former on their executed state trajectories that complete the motion and pass se-mantic and physical validity checks. At each incoming frame, the student uses only current and past human observations to generate a robot motion reference for a downstream whole-body controller. Experiments demonstrate that semantic-conditioned physics experts generate motion with higher semantic fidelity and physical validity than direct optimization-based retargeting, while the resulting streaming retargeter achieves improved closed-loop performance over kinematic and physics-refined baselines under a common whole-body controller. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large-scale human motion datasets such as AMASS, LAFAN1, and BONES-SEED provide a rich source of whole-body motion for humanoid learning and control (Mahmood et al., 2019; Harvey et al., 2020; Bones Studio, 2026). Transferring these motions to robots requires more than geomet-ric matching because humans and robots differ in morphology, joint structure, mass distribution, contact mechanics, and actuation. For streaming applications such as live teleoperation, retargeting must satisfy two requirements: preserving human motion semantics in physically executable robot references, and generating those references from current and past human observations. The first requirement depends on how robot motion is generated for supervision. Kinematic retarget-ing pipelines use inverse kinematics or geometric optimization to construct robot trajectories (Penco et al., 2018; Darvish et al., 2019; Araujo et al., 2025). Although these methods can incorporate con-tact and collision constraints, geometric agreement alone does not establish dynamic executability. Using the resulting trajectories as neural supervision can tie the learned mapping to solutions se-lected by the kinematic solver. Physics-supervised methods such as NMR (Zhao et al., 2026) refine these trajectories through simulated tracking, but still use the prescribed kinematic motion as the ex-pert’s tracking target. This motivates specifying the motion to preserve directly through robot-aware task-space semantics and using the resulting physical execution to generate robot supervision. The second requirement is causal inference from a live human stream. NMR’s learned retar-geter (Zhao et al., 2026) uses bidirectional modeling over a complete motion sequence and therefore

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 OPTIMIZATION -BASED MOTION RETARGETING 

Early motion retargeting methods transferred motion between articulated characters through con-strained optimization (Gleicher, 1998; Popovi´ c & Witkin, 1999; Tak & Ko, 2005). For humanoids, geometric methods use Cartesian objectives to accommodate differences in morphology and joint structure (Penco et al., 2018; Darvish et al., 2019). Later approaches improve morphology adapta-tion and geometric matching (Araujo et al., 2025), incorporate physical constraints into data genera-tion (Lee et al., 2025), and preserve human–object or human–environment interactions (Yang et al., 2025). UMR further uses learned surface correspondences to guide constrained pose matching and contact transfer (Cao et al., 2026). Dynamics-aware methods incorporate force equilibrium (Rouxel et al., 2022), optimal control (Grandia et al., 2023), or kinodynamic trajectory optimization (Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Dhedin et al., 2026). Direct Dynamic Retargeting uses sampling-based model predictive control to optimize task-space imitation directly in simulation, without an intermediate kinematic trajectory (Roux et al., 2026). These methods generate robot motion by op-timizing input poses or trajectories. SMR instead learns from accepted physics-expert rollouts and generates streaming references with a causal neural model, without solving a retargeting optimiza-tion problem at deployment. 22.2 LEARNING -BASED AND PHYSICS -SUPERVISED RETARGETING 

Learning-based retargeters learn cross-skeleton mappings through shared latent spaces, skeleton-aware architectures, and paired or unpaired motion translation (Villegas et al., 2018; Aberman et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023; Choi et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2023). Online upper-body retargeting has also been demonstrated using unpaired adversarial learning (Yagi et al., 2024). Physics enters these models through feasibility constraints in Human2Humanoid (Huang et al., 2026) and simulated tracking feedback in IKMR (Chen et al., 2025a). ReActor jointly adapts reference motions and trains a tracking policy through bilevel optimization (M¨ uller et al., 2026), while OmniTrack uses privileged simulation rollouts to generate references for training a downstream tracking policy (Li et al., 2026). NMR is most closely related to our approach: it trains clustered RL experts to track GMR-generated trajectories and uses their rollouts to supervise a full-sequence neural retargeter (Zhao et al., 2026). In SMR, experts directly track robot-aware human semantics, with IK used only for initialization. Accepted state rollouts then supervise a human-only, strictly causal motion-reference generator. 2.3 PHYSICS -BASED MOTION TRACKING 

Physics-based motion tracking studies how robots and simulated characters execute reference mo-tions under dynamics and contact constraints. DeepMimic and PHC address this problem through reinforcement learning in simulation (Peng et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2023). Subsequent humanoid systems improve sim-to-real transfer (He et al., 2025) and motion diversity (Liao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025b; Han et al., 2025), introduce online dynamics adaptation (Zhang et al., 2025), and scale motion tracking through larger datasets and models (Luo et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026). MimicLite focuses on efficient training of general motion-tracking policies (Robotparty Lab, 2026). These approaches use robot-state feedback to produce control actions, whereas SMR generates motion ref-erences from human observations. We evaluate these references with a frozen whole-body controller to isolate the effect of retargeting on downstream execution. 

## 3 METHOD 

3.1 OVERVIEW 

SMR learns streaming motion retargeting from physics experts that execute human semantic com-mands. In Section 3.2, we construct robot-aware task-space commands and train physics experts to track them in simulation. We retain the resulting state rollouts that pass semantic and physical validity checks. In Section 3.3, we use these rollouts to train a single strictly causal Transformer that generates streaming motion references. Figure 1 illustrates the complete SMR pipeline. 3.2 SEMANTIC -CONDITIONED PHYSICS EXPERTS 

Human semantic commands. We assemble a corpus from AMASS (Mahmood et al., 2019), LAFAN1 (Harvey et al., 2020), BONES-SEED (Bones Studio, 2026), 100STYLE (Mason et al., 2022), and several additional smaller sources. We use the source human motions rather than con-verted robot trajectories and represent each motion as a canonical SMPL-H sequence at 50 Hz (Loper et al., 2015; Romero et al., 2017). Let Ht denote the SMPL-H state at frame t, including the root transform and articulated body pose. For a target robot R, a deterministic morphology-aware trans-port maps the human motion to robot-shaped body landmarks using a fixed neutral-leg scale and the robot’s neutral geometry. The resulting landmarks form human semantics for the target robot. The transport only produces task-space targets; it neither solves IK nor produces a robot joint trajectory. From the transported positions and orientations, we form a predefined set K of 15 body landmarks and construct the human semantic command:

Motion clustering. To organize the heterogeneous motion patterns in the corpus, we partition the motions into training pools before fitting the physics experts. For the large-scale sources, we fol-low Humanoid-GPT (Qi et al., 2026) and apply Harmonic Motion Embedding (HME) directly to 50-Hz human semantic sequences, whereas Humanoid-GPT embeds retargeted robot motion. A Pe-riodic Autoencoder (Starke et al., 2022) extracts harmonic amplitude and frequency features from two-second windows. We concatenate the mean and standard deviation of these features for each motion into a 256-dimensional descriptor, cluster the descriptors, and use each nonempty cluster as the training pool for one semantic-conditioned expert. Smaller or specialized motion sets by-pass clustering and form one single-motion expert per motion. This procedure produces more than 400 experts that share the same human semantic command definition and training objective; the clustering only organizes expert training and rollout generation.

Expert rollout supervision. After training, each expert deterministically replays its assigned mo-tions in simulation and records the joint, root, and body states at 50 Hz. A rollout is accepted only if it reaches the final frame without termination and passes the fixed semantic-fidelity and physical-validity gates. Accepted state sequences are paired frame by frame with their source human motions and corresponding human semantic commands, where met denotes the simulator state at timestamp 

t. The resulting supervision is dynamics-grounded because each rollout is executed and accepted under the specified robot, contact, actuator, and simulator models. 3.3 NEURAL RETARGETING 

Input and output representation. SMR distills the accepted expert rollouts into a single retar-geter that uses only human observations. The student receives no simulator state, expert action, expert identity, or future human frame. At time t, its input is an 85-D feature consisting of a 79-D human semantic pose and a 6-D human-root twist. The pose features contain (i) the positions of 14 non-root landmarks relative to the pelvis (42-D), (ii) pelvis height (1-D), (iii) a 6-D heading-removed pelvis-tilt representation, and (iv) 6-D orientations of the torso, wrists, and ankles (30-D). The twist (v) contains linear and angular backward differences of the human root, expressed in the preceding human-root frame at the native observation rate. Landmark positions and orientations use the heading frame of the current human root. The pose features contain no absolute horizontal position or yaw. Causal transformer student. To support streaming retargeting, we instantiate fθ as a strictly causal Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) with four pre-normalized blocks of width 512, multi-head self-attention with 16 heads, and rotary position embeddings (Su et al., 2024). A final normalization and linear head produce bmt. Each position attends only to itself and the 127 preceding tokens, so each arriving frame is processed with bounded computation independent of stream length. Training uses the same causal mask. Rotary positions are scaled by 50 /f at input rate f so the 128-token context covers the same physical duration across rates. During streaming inference, each layer maintains a ring KV cache and processes one arriving frame per forward pass. Both the attention path and the feature derivatives use only observations available at time t, enforcing zero lookahead. 

Training. We train the retargeter on the accepted expert rollouts with 

L = Ldist + 0 .1Lv + 0 .01 Lpen + 0 .1Lslip . (4) where met is the accepted simulator state expressed in the student’s 35-D output contract. The dis-tillation term Ldist is a per-dimension normalized mean-squared error between bmt and met . The velocity term Lv matches joint and reconstructed-root motion, Lpen penalizes foot geometry below the ground, and Lslip matches horizontal foot speeds on support frames inferred from the expert rollout. To support real-time teleoperation at different downstream input rates, we sample causal windows at f ∈ { 30 , 50 , 90 } Hz and align human observations with expert labels at the same phys-ical timestamps. Only the input history is corrupted with temporally correlated pose noise and one-or two-frame causal holds to model imperfect or delayed observations. The prediction is passed directly to the downstream whole-body controller as its motion reference.

At 50 Hz, SMR’s model step takes 0.37 ms per frame, compared with 4.87 ms for GMR’s iterative solve (Table 6). The values average three warm-run medians. XLarge was measured on a separate GPU. SMR also achieves higher completion and lower execution errors under the frozen WBC (Table 4), combining faster reference generation with better closed-loop simulation performance. GMR’s kinematic optimization does not enforce dynamically stable execution: the GMR-driven robot becomes unstable while kneeling, and its raised-arm motion falls out of phase with the operator. With SMR references, MimicLite produces smooth, stable motion throughout the demonstrated teleoperation sequences. 95 CONCLUSION 

We presented SMR, which uses semantic-conditioned physics experts to produce supervision for a strictly causal neural retargeter. The experts track robot-aware task-space commands directly, with IK used only to initialize episodes. Accepted simulator state rollouts, executed under the Unitree G1 contact and actuator models, train the student to generate Unitree G1 motion references from current and past human observations. This separates the semantic task from its physical realization while preserving a streaming input contract. In the shared 400-motion Unitree G1 evaluation, SMR completes 97.0% of motions under a frozen whole-body controller, compared with 90.0% for GMR and 92.0% for NMR, while lowering MPJPE and E-MPJPE (Table 4). The semantic expert also achieves lower landmark error and fewer self-collisions than direct optimization-based GMR (Table 3). These results support using accepted expert rollouts to train streaming references under the tested simulator and controller. 

## 6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Our evaluation is centered on the Unitree G1 under a single simulation and control setting. The semantic transport, expert policies, and closed-loop evaluation are tied to this embodiment and con-troller, so transfer to other morphologies, actuation, contact conditions, controllers, and hardware remains untested. SMR also faces a causal distillation gap: the student sees only current and past human observations, whereas the physics expert can access robot state and limited future seman-tic context. Its performance ceiling may therefore be lower than that of a bidirectional model with access to the complete motion sequence, which is the cost of zero-lookahead streaming inference. Future work should evaluate broader embodiments, controllers, and hardware, develop uncertainty-aware causal predictors, and extend the semantic interface to robot-to-human retargeting. Human-motion and teleoperation recordings are treated as potentially identifiable data, and their collection, storage, and sharing follow participant consent and privacy protections. Generated robot motion references can lead to falls or collisions during execution. Acceptance of an expert rollout in simulation does not establish hardware safety, and the qualitative demonstration does not establish safety across robots, environments, or human interactions. Physical deployment therefore requires separate risk assessment, supervised testing, and appropriate safeguards. REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

Morphology-aware transport. All transport parameters are fixed by the neutral SMPL-H rest pose and the Unitree G1 neutral configuration. Let ℓR and ℓS be the sums of the hip–knee and knee–ankle rest lengths over both legs of the Unitree G1 and SMPL-H skeleton, respectively. We scale the three coordinates of the human root by sR = ℓR/ℓ S and anchor this scaling at the world origin. A constant vertical offset then aligns the lowest toe of the scaled human rest pose with the corresponding robot rest pose. Cropping a sequence therefore does not change the transported pose at an unchanged timestamp. The remaining landmarks are assembled along the human segment di-rections with lengths from the robot rest pose rather than by pointwise scaling. The neck is attached to a fixed waist anchor, and the head is placed using the neutral Unitree G1 head geometry. For every landmark, including the root, we map the SMPL orientation into a reference frame fixed at the Unitree G1 neutral configuration. This transport produces task-space landmark targets only. It performs no inverse-kinematics solve and produces no robot joint trajectory. 

Command fields. For each landmark, the command contains the transported position and orienta-tion and their linear and angular velocities. All four fields are computed after transport and archived in the world frame at 50 Hz. The velocities are finite differences of the transported quantities and are used by the expert critic and tracking rewards, not as additional student inputs. We partition the heterogeneous motion corpus into offline training pools before fitting the physics experts. This subsection details the Harmonic Motion Embedding (HME) grouping summarized in Section 3.2. The grouping input is the robot-aware semantic command sequence at 50 Hz. Motions 14 shorter than the 101-frame, two-second window are padded by holding the terminal pose, so no motion is removed by the windowing step. 

Periodic autoencoder. A Periodic Autoencoder (PAE) (Starke et al., 2022) encodes each 101-frame window with a convolutional encoder and 64 phase channels. A Fourier decomposition of each phase channel provides a harmonic amplitude and a power-weighted frequency. We train one PAE per clustered source with reconstruction error as the only objective, using 50,000 AdamW steps, learning rate 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 10 −4, batch size 512, 16 windows per motion, a 2% validation split, and random seed 0. We slide the same window with a stride of 0.2 s over each complete motion. Concatenating the mean and standard deviation of the 64 amplitudes and 64 frequencies over these windows yields one 256-dimensional descriptor per motion. We do not segment a motion into separate descriptors.

A.3 PHYSICS EXPERT TRAINING 

We train each expert as a closed-loop PPO policy in the target-robot simulator. The semantic com-mand window contains eight samples at 50 Hz: the current sample, three samples from the preced-ing 160 ms, and four samples from the following 80 ms. The proprioceptive history uses offsets 

{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 16 } control steps in the past. Within the command window, the actor observes only position and orientation fields. Its full observation also includes the sparse proprioceptive history and the three most recent policy actions. Landmark velocities are reserved for the privileged critic and for tracking rewards. The policy outputs a 29-dimensional joint-position target at 50 Hz. Each of the eight tracking terms uses the kernel exp( −¯e/σ ) with its own width. Position tracking cov-ers the root and every landmark of K except the head, and the five selected orientation bodies are the torso, both wrists, and both ankles. Root displacement and orientation residuals are encoded in the current robot heading-local frame for the actor, while root tracking errors are evaluated in the world frame and body-landmark errors in the current heading-local frame. A curriculum changes the tracking widths from 1.25 to 0.5 times their base values during the first 1,050 iterations, while the weights in Table 2 remain fixed. An episode terminates when a root or landmark error exceeds its threshold for consecutive control steps or when the motion ends. 

Optimization, episodes, and reset states. The actor and critic are MLPs with Mish activations and running observation normalization. The actor has three 1,024-unit hidden layers, and the critic has hidden layers of 1,024, 512, and 256 units. PPO uses a clip ratio of 0.2, entropy coefficient 0.012, AdamW learning rate 5 × 10 −5 with an adaptive KL schedule, discount 0.99 , and λ = 0 .95 .We train with 16,384 parallel environments for 1,500 iterations for single-motion experts and 4,000 iterations for cluster experts. After a failure, an episode restarts with probability 0.8 from 25–125 frames earlier, with the start frame selected by an adaptive difficulty sampler. The initial robot state is obtained from offline per-frame differential IK using landmark positions as primary targets. The 15 IK solve uses neither contact reasoning nor cross-frame continuity. It supplies only the simulator reset state and is not a tracking target for the expert. A.4 EXPERT ROLLOUT SUPERVISION 

After training, each expert deterministically replays every assigned motion once at 50 Hz with its mean policy, starting from the same offline IK reset state used during training. ## B NEURAL RETARGETING 

This section specifies the causal student’s input and output contracts, streaming state, and training recipe. 

Input and output contract. At time t, the input is the 79-D human pose representation from items (i)–(iv) and the 6-D human-root twist from item (v) in Section 3.3. The twist is a backward difference computed at the native observation rate and is zero at the first frame of each session. The student receives no simulator state, expert action or identity, or future human observation. The output is the 35-D robot motion reference bmt = ( bqt, c∆pt, c∆rt): 29 Unitree G1 joint positions, a 3-D pelvis translation offset, and a 3-D relative pelvis rotation. The two offsets are expressed in the current human-pelvis frame and are composed with the current human pelvis pose to reconstruct the robot root. This representation avoids integrating a predicted root velocity, but the reconstructed root can still inherit human-root drift, heading noise, and ground-height error. The student produces a motion reference, not a torque or action. The downstream whole-body controller executes this reference. 

Architecture and streaming. The deployed model has four pre-normalized Transformer blocks of width 512 and 16 attention heads. Each head has dimension 32, and the feed-forward layer is four times the model width with GELU activations. Rotary embeddings use base 10,000, dropout is disabled, and the model has approximately 12.7 M parameters. RoPE positions are scaled by 50 /f at input rate f , so the 128-token context spans the same physical duration across rates. The causal mask gives each output access only to the current token and the 127 preceding tokens. During streaming inference, each layer maintains a bounded ring KV cache and processes one arriving frame per call. The cache is reset at a session boundary. Input normalization is folded into the deployed graph, and outputs are reported in physical units. Strict causality describes this retargeter interface. The 16 downstream WBC may use an 80-ms future-reference buffer. This buffer is not retargeter lookahead and is reported separately. 

Training recipe. Student-Final uses a single-stage run of 15,000 optimization steps with batch size 32, AdamW, and a cosine learning-rate schedule from 3 × 10 −4 to 3 × 10 −5. Each training window contains 256 frames and is sampled at the input rates used in the main text. Positions are linearly interpolated and orientations use shortest-arc interpolation at the resampled timestamps. Human features and root offset labels are recomputed at those timestamps under the causal alignment, so the input at time t does not use an observation after t. With probability 0.5, only the input history is corrupted. The corruption combines temporally correlated pose noise of 2 mm for positions and 0.01 rad for orientations with one- or two-frame causal holds, applied with a per-frame drop probability of 0.06. Offset labels are recomputed from the uncorrupted expert rollout, whereas the root twist is recomputed from the corrupted input stream. 17
