# Watch Your Step: Anticipated Perception for Humanoid Locomotion 

Matthias Heyrman 1,∗, Chenhao Li 1,2, Ioannis Dadiotis 1, Marco Hutter 1

Abstract — Perceptive locomotion policies typically observe a dense robot-centric elevation map, although only the terrain beneath and around the next footsteps informs the control decision. Learned encoders compress the whole visual input, leaving the policy to work out which inputs matter while learning the baseline locomotion task. Learning both at once slows training and, without computationally expensive attention mechanisms, makes generalization to new terrain harder. We instead remove redundant inputs from the elevation map before the policy sees it. A lightweight foothold prediction model (FPM) predicts future footholds from proprioception and the velocity command alone, and we sample the elevation map only in small patches around these predicted footholds. We train the prediction model alongside the locomotion policy, periodically retraining it on new policy rollouts as the robot’s gait matures. This allows the perception to anticipate where the policy will step, while the policy learns to leverage terrain information around those steps. With the proposed lightweight preprocessing, the policy reaches a given terrain difficulty in roughly a third of the training iterations required with the complete map. It outperforms policies that observe the complete map where footholds are sparse, including in narrow walled corridors, and on unseen terrain, and it matches an attention-based encoder trained at several times the cost. The policy transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1 humanoid traversing various obstacles, stairs, gaps, and step-downs beyond the training distribution in both indoor and outdoor environments. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision is an indispensable tool when traversing unstruc-tured terrain. People constantly survey the ground ahead of them to plan their next steps and to adjust the trajectory of a foot in mid-air. Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has driven significant progress in legged robot locomotion [1], [2]. Us-ing high-fidelity physics simulators, modern RL algorithms with parallelized environments, and effective techniques to reduce the sim-to-real gap, humanoids can be conditioned on a robot-centric elevation map [3] to traverse rough terrain. However, this reliance on dense environmental maps in-troduces fundamental limitations related to sample efficiency, redundancy, and distraction. Only the small regions of terrain where the feet will next make contact should inform control decisions, yet policies typically sample and process the full map uniformly, even though most of it describes terrain the robot will never step on, such as cells far to the side or directly opposite the commanded velocity direction. By contrast, people tend to look at the ground approximately 1.5 seconds, i.e., 2 to 3 steps, ahead of them, focusing on their planned footholds instead of the entire surrounding scene [4]. On more challenging terrains, this look-ahead lets them intuitively adapt a step in mid-air: if a foothold seems insecure or they notice a better spot near the original target, they shift their focus and change the step trajectory. Existing work primarily aims to reduce redundant informa-tion from the full elevation map through learned embeddings that feed the control policy. Typical encoders compress the full input into a latent vector [2], scale well to complex terrains [5], [6], and transfer to real-world locomotion [7], [8]. However, encoders such as CNNs still have to learn to extract the relevant features from the full map, and thus inherit the redundancy and sample inefficiency of the visual input rather than addressing it. Attention-based encoders, conditioned on proprioception arXiv:2610.29612v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 and the elevation map, weigh terrain features to focus on possible future steps [9], and later work extends them with local and global feature streams [10], [11] and uncertainty-aware terrain estimates [10]. Using egocentric depth images alongside a height scan enables an attention-based antic-ipatory gaze that better respects onboard compute limita-tions [12]. These methods learn where to look, but nothing in the encoder structure ensures that the attended features are future footholds; the encoder has to discover the focus implicitly from the task reward. Other depth-based methods encode the images directly [13], [14]. All of these methods process the entire visual input when deployed and leave it to the policy to ignore what does not matter. Terrain outside of its next steps can therefore still distract it. Another approach analyzes the terrain and plans where the robot’s feet should land, a long-standing body of work. Classical methods select candidate contact regions based on kinematic reachability and terrain geometry [15]–[18], and learning-based approaches evaluate candidate footholds based on the height map around the nominal landing po-sition [19] or across the local map under kinematic and collision constraints [20]. These foothold selection methods run as standalone modules that hand a target to a downstream tracking controller. This requires strongly defined hand-crafted selection criteria for each platform, such as thresholds on the slope, roughness and curvature of a foot-sized patch, its distance to edges, and robot-specific checks of kinematic reachability and leg collision. These criteria encode the designer’s notion of a safe foothold and must be manually retuned for a new foot geometry or gait. The module is also not necessarily conditioned on how the downstream controller actually moves, which leaves its output liable to be inaccurate or suboptimal for the gait that executes it. The mismatch between planner and controller is especially pertinent for modern legged locomotion controllers, which are RL policies trained in simulation to maximize a reward. RL-based approaches can learn precise foot placement in simulation through a terrain difficulty curriculum and crafted rewards [21], [22] and can learn to track foothold plan-ners [23], [24], use them to shape rewards [25], or track a decoupled learned planning policy [26]. In these cases, however, the foothold serves as a tracking target or a reward that shapes the policy’s actions, not as a filter that shapes the policy’s observations. Prior work has used predicted footholds to reward safe locomotion using privileged infor-mation acquired in simulation during training [14], but the predictions never reach the hardware. Filtering the elevation map around future footholds before the policy sees it raises two challenges. First, the filter cannot observe the terrain to decide where to look, as it would inherit the overhead of the full elevation map that it is meant to remove. It must therefore predict the footholds entirely from the robot’s own state and command, while the policy, like a person, can still adjust its trajectory to choose footholds based on the terrain it sees around each prediction. Second, where the feet will land is a property of the gait, which the policy is still learning. A predictor fit to an early, unsteady gait would misplace its patches once the policy improves, and a policy trained on misplaced patches may learn to distrust them. In this paper, we propose a human-inspired, state-based sampling module that filters the elevation map before the locomotion policy receives any terrain information. To fil-ter the elevation map without observing it, a lightweight 

foothold prediction model (FPM) predicts the robot’s next two footholds from its current proprioceptive state and ve-locity command alone. We treat only the terrain around these predicted footholds as relevant for locomotion and sample the elevation map in patches surrounding them. These patches act as the exteroceptive observation of the locomotion policy, creating a state-conditioned representation of the terrain. To ensure that the predicted footholds, and with them the sampled patches, stay up-to-date as the locomotion policy learns more difficult terrains, we co-train the FPM and the locomotion policy in an iterative training loop. We train the locomotion policy with RL in simulation and, after a fixed number of iterations, refit the FPM in a self-supervised fash-ion on the footholds the current policy produces, allowing the perceptual filter to adapt as the policy converges. We demon-strate that this approach reaches a given terrain difficulty in roughly a third of the iterations required by other methods using the complete elevation map, at a lower perception cost to the policy and across a variety of rough and sparse terrain styles. We also show indoor and outdoor hardware experiments, where the resulting policy successfully transfers to a Unitree G1 humanoid (Fig. 1). In summary, our contributions are as follows: 1) A self-supervised foothold prediction model (FPM) that accurately predicts the next two footholds from the current proprioceptive state and velocity command, co-trained on policy rollouts during RL training. 2) A human-inspired perceptive locomotion pipeline that samples the elevation map only around the footholds predicted by the FPM, with insights into its key de-sign choices, such as the prediction horizon and the encoding of the sampled patches. 3) Simulation and hardware experiments, including com-parisons against state-of-the-art elevation-map en-coders, that demonstrate the advantages of restricting the policy’s exteroceptive input to the terrain around its next predicted footholds over the complete map. C. Co-Training Schedule 

As described in Alg. 1, we co-train the locomotion policy and the FPM in an alternating loop in which we refit each component to the latest version of the other. The schedule runs in units of PPO iterations, each comprising 24 

environment steps for Nenv = 8192 parallel environments. 

1) Bootstrap: The policy has not learned to stand or walk at the beginning of training, so no FPM is initially fit. We instead bootstrap the locomotion policy to learn to walk on easy terrain and fix the foothold predictions directly beneath the base so that the policy observes a height scan under its own body. We use this bootstrapping mode for the first 

n0 = 500 iterations of RL training, at which point the policy walks adequately and its footholds carry information worth learning.

to 0.2 m ), upright and inverted pyramid stairs ( 15% each; step height 0.1 m to 0.25 m ), and boxes ( 15% ; 0.45 m grid, height 0.05 m to 0.3 m ). The remainder comprises upright and inverted pyramid slopes ( 7.5% and 5% , gradient up to

Leveraging the robot’s symmetry, we augment each batch with the mirrored transition instead of adding a symmetry loss [30]. We use standard domain randomization parameters during training [31] to modify environmental and robot model parameters or apply external disturbances to add robustness to inaccurate modeling, non-ideal terrain conditions, or po-tential events that may occur to the robot. In addition, we add uniform noise to all proprioceptive observations and inject occasional large spikes into the base velocity observations to emulate state-estimator faults. IV. The table aggregates the results of two tests: the locomotion test, tracking a randomly sampled linear velocity command on 8 m tiles, and the corridor test, tracking a forward velocity command through a narrow corridor with tall walls. The corridor test checks how each policy handles OOD terrain outside of the robot’s commanded path. We categorize terrains as Sparse (e.g., stepping stones), Stepped

A success rate of 100% is not realistically achievable as it would require perfect velocity command tracking over the entire length of the course. Our FPM policy performs best or second best in most experiments. In the locomotion test, FPM has the highest success rate on the training terrains ( 84 .6% ), the best per-formance on the sparse stepping stones ( 80 .2% ) and gaps (77 .7% ), and the lowest fall rate on the OOD terrains ( 5.2% ). 83 .1% ), including the sparse pallets ( 87 .1% ), and has a lower fall rate on the training terrains ( 2.4% ). Both policies out-perform the other tested methods on aggregate performance in this test. This demonstrates that our lightweight sampling approach matches the performance of a much more expensive attention-based approach. The results of the corridor test are more conclusive about the benefits of the FPM sampling approach on sparse terrain. FPM has the highest overall success rate ( 89 .2% ), scoring highest on the sparse terrains ( 74 .2% ) and second highest on the continuous terrains ( 99 .5% ). AME2 performs second best on the sparse terrains ( 71 .7% ), and both are well ahead of all other approaches, which cover less than half the commanded distance.

The FPM-based locomotion policy additionally traversed a variety of outdoor terrains, including a staircase with 18 con-tinuous steps. Shown in Fig. 1, the policy also successfully traverses rough terrain in corridors, validating the simulation performance of the corridor test in Sec. IV-B. The reader is encouraged to watch the videos on the project page for a full overview of the conducted hardware tests. 

D. Ablations 

We examine how the foothold prediction horizon affects locomotion performance. We define two alternative horizons, denoted by the furthest step in the future that they predict, so our FPM is FPM2 under this naming. All variants output two foothold predictions from the contact sequence but differ in the horizon. Our FPM predicts the next two future footholds, 

p1 and p2, FPM1 pairs the latest foothold p0 with p1, and 

FPM3 outputs p2 and p3. FPM1 tests whether the terrain around the latest and the next foothold suffices for the current policy step. FPM3 instead explores whether a longer horizon benefits locomotion at the cost of no longer observing the next foothold. We also test adding a CNN encoder to each FPM ablation, trained alongside the policy, to extract further features from the sampled elevation-map patches. As shown in Table III, FPM1 performs below FPM, most clearly on the sparse corridor terrains ( 62 .2% against 

74 .2% ). The CNN encoder improves the success rate of FPM1 on both the training and OOD terrains and gives it the second best overall corridor performance ( 86 .3% ). FPM CNN has the lowest fall rate on both the training and OOD terrains ( 1.4% and 4.0% ). This suggests possible benefits of additionally encoding the sampled patches; however, FPM without an encoder still performs best overall, suggesting that the samples already form effective features for the locomotion policy. FPM3 performs lower overall on the locomotion test. However, FPM3 shows a lower fall rate than FPM and FPM1, and performs well on the stepped corridor terrains (86 .3% ). Fig. 5 reports the error of each FPM horizon at each prediction step on the training and OOD terrains. It shows that FPM3 predicts the second future foothold less accurately than FPM, and that its third-step prediction has roughly 22 cm of error, approximately the length of the G1 foot. Adding a CNN to FPM3 leaves success within two points of FPM3 and does not lower the OOD fall rate, likely because the further features lie too far ahead of the robot to matter for the current step. Fig. 5 also shows a roughly linear relationship between the prediction horizon and the error, of roughly 6 cm per step. Given the Unitree G1 foot of 20 .8 cm length and 

7.6 cm width, this suggests that the prediction is accurate to within a foot length up to the second future foothold. The standard deviation increases with the horizon, likely due to the difficulty of predicting far ahead on rough terrain from a current motion that is only weakly related to it. V. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Our co-trained, state-based sampling shrinks the percep-tion learning problem: rather than finding the relevant cells in the full map, the policy learns how to use two small patches chosen for it. FPM learns faster than the tested full-map methods (Sec. IV-A). Wild and Naive, which sample as many heights as FPM or more, but around the current feet or along the commanded direction, perform worse than HM (Table II), suggesting that the benefit comes from placing the patches at the predicted footholds, not from the smaller observation. Despite the limited exteroceptive observations, the FPM-based policy performs best on most tested sparse terrains. On hardware, it slows down and adjusts its body and steps before crossing large gaps or stepping off high drops (Sec. IV-C), demonstrating emergent cautious behavior. We conjecture that because the observations depend on the locomotion policy’s actions, this may encourage it to probe the terrain before committing while still tracking the velocity command. AME2 is the only alternative that matches FPM on the locomotion test, at a much higher training cost of 6.3 s per policy update on four GPUs compared to 0.55 s on a single GPU for FPM. Its weaker corridor results (Sec. IV-B) suggest that the attention mechanism can be distracted by obstacles it has never seen, whereas the FPM never shows the policy the wall outside the commanded direction. The corridor test also marks the limits of our approach. Full-map observation methods lead FPM on the stepped terrains, and all methods perform similarly on continuous terrain, where a dense scan suffices. The FPM discards all terrain the robot does not intend to step on, and obstacles approaching from the side would enter the patches late or not at all, which our corridor walls, parallel to the commanded path, do not test. Finally, our effective prediction horizon is limited to predicting the second future foothold. The FPM3 ablation (Fig. 5) shows that the step predictions become less accurate with greater variance as the horizon increases, also degrading the locomotion policy’s performance (Table III). VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented a perceptive locomotion frame-work for humanoids in which a lightweight foothold predic-tion model, trained alongside the locomotion policy, predicts the next two footholds from proprioception and the velocity command, and we sample the elevation map only around them. The policy learns several times faster than policies that observe the complete map, matches an attention-based encoder at a fraction of its training cost, performs best on most sparse terrains, and transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1. In the future this work may be extended to an active depth-based perception system that does not rely on an eleva-tion map, but instead directly observes the future predicted footholds. This approach may also extend to manipulative locomotion, using predicted hand and foot contacts to guide the policy to observe points for arm and leg support.
