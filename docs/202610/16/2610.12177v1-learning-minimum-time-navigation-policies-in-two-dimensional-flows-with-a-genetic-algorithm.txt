We study minimum-time navigation between two points in a known two-dimensional fluid flow for a vessel with fixed-magnitude slip velocity and controllable direction. The navigation policy is parameterized by a neural network and optimized using a genetic algorithm that evolves an ensemble of candidate strategies through Dar-winian selection and parameter mutation, without crossover. The method recov-ers analytical and numerical optimal-control solutions in several benchmark cases, including two-dimensional turbulence, and outperforms Q-learning and one-step actor-critic method. We further show that the learned navigation strategies are robust to variations in the starting position and to unresolved small-scale turbulent fluctuations, provided that the characteristic velocity of these fluctuations remains small compared to the vessel’s slip velocity. The proposed approach provides a com-pact representation of the navigation policy, is readily parallelizable, and eliminates the need for reward shaping, offering an efficient alternative to existing analytical and numerical methods for computing minimum-time trajectories in complex flows. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

The minimum-time navigation of a vessel with fixed-magnitude slip velocity and con-trollable direction in a prescribed two-dimensional flow is known as Zermelo’s navigation problem 1. Nearly a century after its formulation, it remains a fundamental problem in flow-assisted navigation, with applications ranging from micro-robots for targeted drug and gene delivery 2–7 to autonomous vehicles and ocean gliders operating in oceanic and atmospheric currents 8–14 . The objective is to optimally exploit the interplay between self-propulsion and environmental advection to minimize travel time. In the present formulation, the flow field is assumed to be known a priori and available to the vessel, in contrast to navigation strategies based solely on local sensory information 15–22 .The classical optimal-control formulation transforms Zermelo’s problem into a two-point boundary-value problem, yielding locally optimal trajectories 23–25 . Analytical solutions ex-ist for several simple flow configurations 26,27 , whereas more general flows require numerical integration combined with shooting methods 24,28,29 . Extensions have considered three-dimensional flows 9, navigation on curved manifolds 30,31 , and alternative objectives such as fuel consumption and energy dissipation 27,32 . A fundamental difficulty is that the resulting optimal trajectories can exhibit strong sensitivity to initial conditions and controls. The steering degree of freedom makes the system susceptible to chaotic behavior, causing optimal trajectories to become unstable even in regular currents 24,25,30,31 . Consequently, shooting methods offer no general guarantee of finding the global optimum and yield solutions that are highly sensitive to small perturbations, limiting their robustness in complex flows. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an alternative framework in which robust navigation strategies can be learned through repeated interactions with the flow 33 . In particular, Q-learning has been applied to path planning in steady environments 34,35 , while a one-step actor-critic method has been used for navigation in more complex two-dimensional

2turbulent flows 24,36 . A recent comparison of such tabular RL approaches with optimal-control methods across flows of increasing complexity showed that, although these methods can reliably reach the target, the resulting trajectories are generally suboptimal, with the discrepancy becoming particularly pronounced in time-dependent turbulent settings 25 . This motivates the use of more expressive deep RL methods for Zermelo’s navigation problem. An initial step in this direction was taken in Ref. 37, where an Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) algorithm reproduced analytical optimal-control solutions for several steady flow configurations. These results demonstrated that deep RL can recover optimal navigation strategies and suggested a route toward problems for which conventional optimal-control methods become challenging. Here, we develop a complementary approach that combines neural-network represen-tations of navigation policies with population-based optimization using a genetic algo-rithm 38,39 . The parameters of the neural network (NN) define the genome of each individual in a population, and individuals are evaluated according to a fitness function based on the travel time. The best-performing policies are selected to form the next generation through mutations of their NN parameters, and the procedure is iterated until the fitness ceases to improve. Unlike gradient-based optimization, this approach does not require differentiabil-ity of the objective with respect to the policy parameters. Moreover, fitness evaluations for different individuals are independent and can therefore be performed in parallel, allowing efficient utilization of computational resources. We validate the approach across a hierarchy of flow configurations with increasing com-plexity considered in Ref. 25. For steady vortex and time-dependent sink-vortex flows, for which analytical optimal-control solutions are available 25,26 , the learned policies reproduce the corresponding optimal trajectories. For both a snapshot of two-dimensional turbulence in the inverse-cascade regime and a fully time-dependent turbulent flow, the resulting tra-jectories closely follow the numerically computed optimal-control paths, achieving shorter travel times than Q-learning and a one-step actor-critic method, while also exhibiting ro-bustness to variations in the vessel’s starting position. For the time-dependent turbulent flow, we further examine the robustness of the learned policies to unresolved small-scale velocity fluctuations that may arise from imperfect flow forecasts. We train the NNs us-ing coarse-grained velocity fields with different filtering thresholds, thereby controlling the quality of the available flow forecast, and evaluate the resulting policies in the full-resolution flow. The learned strategies successfully reach the target provided that the characteristic velocity of the unresolved fluctuations remains sufficiently small compared with the ves-sel’s slip velocity. The obtained results demonstrate that genetic-algorithm optimization of neural-network policies provides a practical framework for learning efficient and robust navigation strategies in complex flows.

A standard approach to solve this two-point boundary value problem is the shooting method 28,29 : an initial guess for θ0 is refined until the resulting trajectory, obtained by for-ward integration of the system, satisfies the terminal condition RT = rB within a desired tolerance. Importantly, the considered system may admit multiple solutions: Zermelo’s equation (2) provides a necessary condition for a local extremum but does not guarantee a global optimum 24 . Moreover, the resulting trajectories can be unstable and characterized by positive finite-time Lyapunov exponents. In these cases, infinitesimal uncertainties in start-ing positions or control inputs are exponentially amplified, leading to drastic deviations from the optimal route 24,25,30,31 . Consequently, shooting methods provide no general guarantee of finding the global optimum and the practical application of optimal control is severely limited. For steady flows, one can apply heuristic algorithms on top of unstable optimal trajectories to compensate for deviations, thereby enabling more robust navigation 40 . 

> III. GENETIC ALGORITHM

We propose a complementary approach to Zermelo’s navigation problem that learns ro-bust navigation strategies through repeated interactions with the flow. An overview of the algorithm is provided in Fig. 1, followed by a detailed description below. The navigation strategy is represented by a fully connected NN with Nl = 2 hidden layers, each containing Nn = 16 neurons with SiLU activation 41 . The NN takes the system state as input: the vessel position (Xt, Y t) for steady flows and the vessel position and time (Xt, Y t, t )

for time-dependent flows. Its single linear output represents the steering direction θt. Unlike previous tabular RL approaches 24,25,36 , which define the system state solely by the vessel position and thus effectively formulate navigation in time-dependent flows as a partially observable Markov decision process to avoid an excessively large state space, our approach explicitly includes time in the system state. Moreover, the proposed strategy representation has a highly compact parameterization, requiring substantially fewer trainable parameters than previous approaches 24,25,35–37 , while operating directly in continuous state and action spaces without discretization. The strategy is evaluated over episodes, each corresponding to an attempt to reach the target. The initial position R0 is sampled uniformly within a disk of radius dA centered at rA, thereby promoting robustness to variations in the initial condition. The heading θt

is updated according to the NN prediction at discrete intervals ∆t and held fixed between updates, while Eq. (1) is numerically integrated. The interval ∆t is sufficiently small, with the resulting minimum-time trajectories being two orders of magnitude longer. An episode terminates when the vessel reaches a circle of radius dB centered at rB , the maximum duration Tmax is exceeded, or the vessel collides with the domain boundary. The value of Tmax is chosen to be several times the typical travel time, allowing sufficient time to explore the flow while terminating episodes in which the vessel becomes trapped. The strategy performance is quantified by the fitness, defined as the negative episode duration, with boundary collisions incurring an additional strong penalty of −2Tmax . Compared to tabular RL methods 24,25,36 , no distance-based potential is required in the reward. The introduced parameters define the navigation problem independently of the optimization procedure. For the turbulent flows considered below, rA, rB , dA, dB , and ∆t are identical to those used in Ref. 25, facilitating direct comparison. To optimize the navigation strategy, we employ a genetic algorithm 38,39 , in which a population of N = 320 randomly initialized strategies evolves over successive generations through selection and parameter mutation. In each generation, the population is evaluated over nep episodes and ranked according to the mean fitness. > IV. RESULTS

To evaluate the resulting navigation strategy, we perform neval = 5000 evaluation episodes with perturbed initial positions rA, as described above. We quantify performance using the success probability p, defined as the fraction of trajectories that reach the target, and the mean travel time ⟨TG⟩ computed over successful trajectories. For navigation in turbulent flows, these performance metrics may exhibit variability arising from the stochasticity of the training process, particularly when the flow forecast is coarse-grained. We therefore also report their standard deviations, estimated across 10 independent training runs.

V. CONCLUSION 

We have demonstrated that optimizing a neural-network navigation policy via a genetic algorithm provides an effective and robust solution to Zermelo’s navigation problem in two-dimensional flows. The proposed method successfully recovers optimal-control trajectories in both steady and time-dependent turbulent flows, and achieves shorter travel times and lower run-to-run variability than the tabular RL approaches such as Q-learning and one-step actor-critic method. Additionally, the learned navigation strategies exhibit strong robust-ness to initial position variations and imperfect flow forecasts, provided that unresolved turbulent velocity fluctuations remain smaller than the vessel’s slip velocity. These results highlight the potential of genetic optimization as a compact, parallelizable, and broadly applicable method for learning minimum-time navigation strategies in complex flows.
