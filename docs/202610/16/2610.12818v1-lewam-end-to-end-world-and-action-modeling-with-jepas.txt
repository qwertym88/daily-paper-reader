## ABSTRACT 

Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn compact latent world models without pixel reconstruction. Existing JEPA pipelines typically train vi-sual representations and action-conditioned dynamics before adding a control pol-icy or solver. We introduce LeWAM , an end-to-end JEPA for control with: (1) a joint training objective over next latent state prediction and action flow matching, and (2) an isotropic Gaussian constraint on the latent state, enabling a single model to support direct policy execution and efficient planning by evaluating sampled actions with its learned latent dynamics. Compared with prior JEPAs in visual planning, LeWAM improves success rate from 28.6% to 89.7% on contact-rich tasks and from 10.9% to 46.2% on long-horizon tasks, and plans 32 .3× faster. On behavior cloning tasks in simulation and physical robots, LeWAM is also com-petitive with established methods. Interpretability analyses show that LeWAM learns control-relevant representations and action distributions effectively guiding test-time planning and control. Enc Enc

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Visual control requires autonomous agents to complete tasks by selecting actions based on visual observations of the world. World models address this challenge by predicting the consequences of candidate actions, allowing agents to anticipate how the world will evolve before executing them. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) (LeCun, 2022) offer a compact world modeling paradigm where future world states are predicted in latent space rather than by reconstructing pix-els. Recent works such as DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024) and LeWorldModel (LeWM) (Maes et al., 2026b) demonstrate the promise of applying action-conditioned latent world models to down-stream control. These pipelines obtain visual features from pretrained encoders (Zhou et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025), or train representations from scratch with distributional regularizers such as SI-GReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025; Maes et al., 2026b) to prevent collapse. However, these methods alone do not reliably retain control-relevant information; JEPAs can favor temporally persistent dis-tractors (Sobal et al., 2022), while action-conditioned dynamics may retain extraneous observation information (Brandfonbrener et al., 2023). Control with these models typically requires an online solver such as the cross-entropy method (CEM) (Rubinstein, 1999) or a separately trained action predictor. When initialized with a broad Gaussian prior over action sequences, CEM demands extensive rollouts to identify viable candi-dates, particularly as action dimensionality or planning horizon grows (Huang et al., 2021). Con-tact transitions also complicate local action optimization because the dynamics can switch between modes (Pang et al., 2023). A post-trained action predictor can provide direct control, as in GC-IDM (Nguyen et al., 2026), or guide action search, as in PRISM (Wang et al., 2026). Training these predictors on frozen JEPA embeddings leaves action supervision disconnected from visual representation learning. We introduce LeWAM , an end-to-end JEPA that jointly learns representation, action-conditioned dynamics, and a stochastic policy in a single training stage (Figure 1). Training combines two in-gredients: a joint training objective over next-latent-state prediction and action flow matching, and SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) to regularize the latent states. A Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) (Liang et al., 2025) predictor uses clean actions for latent dynamics prediction and noisy actions for policy learning through flow matching, so both objectives train the shared visual rep-resentation. Consequently, LeWAM supports both direct policy execution and visual planning by refining action sequences sampled from its learned policy. Our contributions are threefold: • To our knowledge, the first JEPA that jointly learns representation, action-conditioned latent dy-namics, and stochastic policy, using a joint prediction objective, one weight for latent-space regu-larization, and approximately 17M parameters. • A planning procedure that jointly imagines the consequences of actions and how to act in the resulting states, which samples candidate action sequences from the learned action distribution and refines them with gradient descent through learned dynamics. • Across ten heterogeneous simulated environments, LeWAM improves goal-reaching success over LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b), particularly on contact-rich tasks. Four-task comparisons extend 2Preprint these gains to longer goal horizons and show faster planning. On goal-agnostic task comple-tion in simulation and physical robot execution, LeWAM performs competitively with established baselines. Linear probes reveal decodable physical-state and action information, recorded-action rollouts visualize its learned dynamics, and planner ablations measure the gains from its stochastic action proposals. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

World Models. World models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) learn environment dynamics to en-able agents to simulate candidate action outcomes for planning and control. Early approaches like PlaNet (Hafner et al., 2019) perform planning via latent rollouts, whereas TD-MPC (Hansen et al., 2022) and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024) ground latent dynamics in reward and value predictions. Dreamer (Hafner et al., 2020; 2023) learns generative latent dynamics with pixel reconstruction, while VideoGPT (Yan et al., 2021) and IRIS (Micheli et al., 2023) model discrete visual tokens. LeWAM learns latent dynamics and a policy directly from offline trajectories, without reward su-pervision or pixel reconstruction. 

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures. JEPAs predict target states in representation space without pixel reconstruction, using variance and covariance constraints (Bardes et al., 2022), mo-mentum targets (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024), or distributional regularization (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025; Wu et al., 2026) to prevent collapse. For action-conditioned world modeling, DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024) and V-JEPA 2-AC (Assran et al., 2025) build on pretrained rep-resentations, whereas LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b) leverages SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) to learn the encoder and latent dynamics from scratch. Additional action prediction objectives at-tempt to make representations action-aware in PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025) or as an anti-collapse signal in SMWM (Ivashkov et al., 2026). LeWAM jointly trains action-conditioned latent dynamics and policy in one predictor, so both consequence prediction and action supervision shape the shared representation. 

Control with Latent Dynamics. Conventional planning in latent world models uses population-based solvers, such as CEM (Rubinstein, 1999), iCEM (Pinneri et al., 2021), MPPI (Williams et al., 2017), or gradient-based methods (Henaff et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2021). Population-based search can require many model rollouts as the action dimension or horizon grows (Pinneri et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021), and contact mode changes in manipulations also complicate local optimiza-tion (Pang et al., 2023) (more discussion in Appendix E). GC-IDM (Nguyen et al., 2026) post-trains a goal-conditioned action predictor for direct control, whereas PRISM (Wang et al., 2026) trains a Gaussian prior head on frozen world-model features; its predicted mean and standard deviation guide MPPI sampling. LeWAM jointly trains the latent world model and a flow-based action pre-dictor, using its sampled actions for direct control or gradient-based planning. 

World Action Models. These models couple world prediction with action prediction (Table 13, Appendix E). Built on pretrained video generative models, DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026) jointly models future video and actions, while Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) retains video co-training but skips future video generation at inference. UVA (Li et al., 2025), UWM (Zhu et al., 2025), and Cosmos 3 (NVIDIA, 2026) specifically support both forward dynamics modeling (FDM) and policy learning, but optimize only one objective per sample, rather than both jointly on the same pass. LeWAM jointly trains its encoder and predictor end-to-end using latent-state prediction and action flow matching, without pixel reconstruction. Each training sample supplies clean actions for FDM and noisy actions for stochastic policy modeling. LeWAM learns action-conditioned dynamics from expert and non-expert interactions and uses them to evaluate and refine actions at test time.

Planning. LeWAM uses a gradient-based refinement strategy over a population of action can-didates sampled from the learned action distribution (Figure 4). To simplify notation, let A =(at, . . . , at+L−1) denote a plan to reach the goal zg after L raw environment steps from time t,where L is a multiple of frameskip K.Starting from the observed history z≤t, one population of N candidate plans is generated from the learned action distribution by alternating action sampling and latent dynamics. Heterogeneous Simulated Environments We evaluate control across ten simulated navigation and manipulation environments (Figure 5). The environments vary in task functionality and temporal horizon, and their datasets include expert demonstrations, random-policy interactions, and mixed-quality data. Four environments adapt the benchmarks used in LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b). The other six add challenging contact-rich manipulation and navigation. Appendix B details task con-struction, data, and success criteria. For Cube , success checks both cube and end-effector positions. 

Evaluation Protocols. We distinguish two separate evaluation settings. Visual planning assumes access to a goal image, and a goal-conditioned policy or planner must reach the depicted state from its current (random) starting position within a fixed step budget. Task completion provides no goal image and evaluates behavior cloning from demonstrations: policies execute actions from start to finish to complete one specific task. Within each setting, methods share training and evaluation data, observations, action limits, goal offsets, execution budgets, and success predicates. Appendix B.2 details goal construction, evaluation conditions, and success criteria. 4.2 VISUAL PLANNING WITH LEWAM We compare with four goal-conditioned baselines spanning latent-dynamics planning, an inverse-dynamics policy on a frozen world model, and direct policy execution: 

LeWM (CEM) (Maes et al., 2026b). Learning compact action-conditioned latent dynamics end-to-end with SIGReg, planning with CEM over learned latent dynamics. 

LeWM (IDM) (Nguyen et al., 2026). Post-trains a goal-conditioned IDM on frozen LeWM. 

DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024). Learns dynamics on a frozen DINOv2 encoder with CEM planner.

Long-Horizon Planning. Using the same checkpoints, we move the goal farther along each recorded trajectory while keeping its starting frame fixed on Push-T , Cube , Scene , and Puzzle .Unlike navigation, these tasks introduce significantly higher behavioral diversity as the planning horizon grows. At a 100-step goal offset, LeWAM achieves 46 .2% mean success across these four tasks versus 10 .9% for LeWM (Figure 7).

Latent Representation Probing. To better measure what information is learned by our joint-training objective, we evaluate how well linear probes recover physical states, proprioceptive infor-mation, and actions from LeWAM’s representations. As highlighted in Table 1 and Appendix C.3, training with an action modeling objective noticeably improves LeWAM’s linear action and propri-oception decodability compared to LeWM, increasing average proprioception, action, and IDM R2

by +0 .21 , +0 .20 , +0 .19 respectively (see Table 9, Appendix C.3). 

The Role of SIGReg. SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025; Maes et al., 2026b) constrains the latent distribution to prevent representation collapse. Without it, latent-only LeWM yields R2 = 0 .00 on every reported Push-T probe, while LeWAM with either action head retains decodable state and action information (Table 1). This supports action supervision as an anti-collapse signal : observed actions anchor the learned representation to targets independent of the encoder. However, only constraints from action prediction is not enough, beyond anti-collapse, we find that SIGReg can largely improve planning: on Push-T , the planning gain is 14.7 percentage points with SIGReg, compared to 2.7 without it (Table 7). The probes in Tables 1 and 9 show that SIGReg improves object-state decodability as well. This is because SIGReg can retain informative latent representation and dynamics which help downstream planning, and make latent distance-to-goal more sensitive to changes in object state. ## 5 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

In this work we introduce LeWAM, an end-to-end JEPA for joint prediction of future latent states and actions, that supports control through both planning and policy execution. On goal-reaching envi-ronments, LeWAM demonstrates improved performance in dynamics-based planning over previous JEPA-based world models. On behavioral cloning from expert demonstrations on both simulated and physical robots, LeWAM is competitive with established policy baselines. 9Preprint 

Limitations and Future Work. 

• Data Coverage. Our task suite combines expert demonstrations and non-expert interactions at limited scale. Broader play data and exploration on contact-rich tasks would extend coverage of difficult contact transitions and support training across a wider range of behaviors. • Generalization. Learning action-conditioned dynamics could support broader generalization than policy-only approaches, motivating tests on unfamiliar objects, camera viewpoints, and contact states. • Single-Task and Single-Embodiment Learning. Models are trained or adapted separately for each task and embodiment. Multitask training across robots and language-conditioned tasks would extend the shared dynamics and policy to broader settings. • Integration with Hierarchical Planning. LeWAM provides low-level planning and control. A high-level planner could decompose longer tasks into subgoals, each executed through LeWAM’s continuous control. • Scalability of Training & Planning. The visual-planning model has approximately 17 M param-eters. ## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Temporal Sampling. Given observation-history stride ∆, the predictor in Section 3.1 uses two history latents z≤t = ( zt−∆, zt), a dynamics stride of K = 5 raw steps, and an action chunk of 

C = 2 K = 10 raw steps. We use a stride of ∆ = K for goal-reaching tasks and ∆ = 1 for task-completion frames. To predict zt+5 , the dynamics branch receives all five actions at, . . . , at+4 , and the flow policy models all ten actions at, . . . , at+9 . Only frames are subsampled. During imagined rollouts, the history update retains the two most recent latent states. 

Inner Architecture. The observation encoder is a ResNet-18-style network (He et al., 2016) trained from scratch. Its convolutional backbone uses GroupNorm with 16 groups. Input images are cropped from 224 × 224 to 202 × 202 , with random offsets during training and a center crop at evaluation. A 

1 × 1 convolution produces 32 spatial maps, and a spatial softmax pooling converts these to 32 ex-pected normalized image-coordinate pairs. After that, a two-layer projection MLP with BatchNorm maps these 64 features through a hidden width of 384 to a 192-dimensional latent state, which is the same as LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b) in simulations for a fair comparison. The MoT predictor has width 192, 4 blocks, 4 attention heads, and dropout 0.1. Its token sequence contains two history latents, one learned state query, five clean action tokens, and ten action flow slots. Learned type and positional embeddings distinguish history, state query, clean action, and action flow tokens. The state readout uses LayerNorm followed by a linear map to the 192-dimensional latent, while the action readout maps each action flow slot to a velocity vector in raw-action space. Goal information ct is optional and is omitted for goal-free task completion. For goal-conditioned visual planning, ct contains the goal image latent zg and the normalized remaining horizon. The goal latent is supplied as an additional token in the action branch, while only noisy action tokens can attend to it. The horizon h between the current timestep and the goal is normalized into a [0 , 1] 

range relative to a maximal value Hmax which dictates the longest distance-from-goal seen during training. h modulates the noisy action tokens using AdaLN-Zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023) blocks with hidden width 512 and a 128-dimensional sinusoidal embedding. In total, the model has approximately 16.9M trainable parameters in visual planning. For the sim-ulated experiments, action sampling uses 8 Euler steps to balance speed and quality. The reported MSE dynamics configuration uses an online encoder target, no state noise, no state flow integration, no momentum target encoder, only SIGReg with 1,024 projections and 17 knots. For the training objective in Equation 5, we set λsig = 0 .04 .

Training Recipe in Simulated Environments. For goal-conditioned visual planning, LeWAM runs use a batch size of 128 and train for 50 epochs, with a base learning rate of 1.5 × 10 −4 and five warmup epochs. The goal-free task-completion runs use a batch size of 64 and train for 120 epochs, with a base learning rate of 10 −4 and ten warmup epochs. Both use AdamW with weight decay 10 −4 and a uniform learning rate across the encoder and MoT predictor. The learning rate increases linearly during warmup and then follows an epochwise cosine schedule. Training uses full precision, a gradient-norm clip of 1, and seed 42. We split valid decision points into 90% training and 10% validation, then sample training points with replacement within each epoch. After each epoch, we retain the checkpoint with the lowest validation action flow-matching loss, where task success is not used for checkpoint selection.

The policy takes two consecutive observa-tions, predicts a 16-action sequence, and executes eight actions starting at the current timestep before resampling. 

ToolHang uses the scene camera; DrawerCleanup and Transport use the scene camera and both wrist cameras. Inputs are 224 × 224 RGB images, with random 202 × 202 crops during training and center crops at evaluation, normalized to [−1, 1] . The policy uses visual observations only. Training windows advance at the raw frame rate, with boundary frames and actions repeated for padding. We randomly split windows into 90% training and 10% validation using seed 42. Each policy trains for 120 epochs with batch size 64 and AdamW, learning rate 10 −4, betas (0 .95 , 0.999) ,and weight decay 10 −6. The learning rate follows cosine decay after 500 warmup updates. Actions retain their recorded scale for ToolHang and Transport ; DrawerCleanup uses min-max scaling to [−1, 1] . We select the checkpoint with the lowest validation denoising loss and evaluate its raw weights without exponential moving averaging. Each checkpoint is evaluated with seeds 0, 1, and 42, using 50 episodes per seed.

Ill-Posed Action Solver in Contact-rich Events. Prior JEPAs for control e.g. , DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024) and LeWM (Maes et al., 2026b) learn action-conditioned latent dynamics without an action-prediction objective and uses CEM (Rubinstein, 1999) for downstream control. In smooth dynamics like 2D navigation, the action solver is well-posed : a diverse representation can invert the action trajectories by argmin the cost of action-conditioned dynamics. However, a contact event can switch the active mode, so nearby actions yield distinct outcomes (Pang et al., 2023). If sampled plans miss useful contact, an object-based goal cost provides little ranking signal (Yamada et al., 2024). With a finite population, CEM ranks the contact branches it samples, and additional iterations incur more rollouts. 

Behavior and Consequence. Joint prediction additionally supplies a learned action distribution for planning proposals. Learned proposals can improve contact-rich planning under constrained bud-gets (Bruderm¨ uller et al., 2025). Figure 18 illustrates why the population-based search from a broad prior cannot work on contact-rich manipulation, and how LeWAM uses the learned proposals and predicted consequences to alleviate this problem. The planning objective is defined in Section 3.2, and the refinement procedure is specified in Appendix A.1. Though this problem in 3D contact-rich manipulation is well known and our solution is heuristic, we consider the rigorous verification of the claim as future work. 32 Preprint
