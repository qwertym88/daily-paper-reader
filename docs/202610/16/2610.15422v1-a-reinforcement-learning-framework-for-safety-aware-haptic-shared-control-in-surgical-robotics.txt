# A Reinforcement Learning Framework for Safety-Aware Haptic Shared Control in Surgical Robotics 

Zihao Zhang , Karl Handwerker , Balint Varga, Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Haptic shared control in surgical robotics combines human-side and robot-side forces at a shared end effector while preserving operator involvement. However, the effects of geometric safety choices on task execution and force coordi-nation remain unclear. We model the interaction as a two-agent cooperative Markov game under centralized training and decentralized execution, comparing learned human control with a task-tuned fixed-impedance baseline. This model-free multi-agent reinforcement learning framework supports joint policy adaptation without requiring a complete analytical interaction model. It compares MADDPG and rMAPPO with an epigraph-constrained approach under common geometric task definitions. In simulation, rMAPPO generally achieved the highest return and tracking performance, whereas the epigraph approach pro-duced more conservative motions and lower geometric exposure, including no observed collision or dangerous-region exposure af-ter geometry transfer. With real operator input, fixed-impedance-trained policies were faster and successful in all recorded trials. Policies trained with a learned partner exhibited greater com-mand opposition, while the fixed epigraph condition produced the most aligned commands. This work provides a benchmark for studying human-control representation and constraint-handling trade-offs in haptic shared control. 

Index Terms —haptic shared control, human–robot collabo-ration, multi-agent reinforcement learning, safe reinforcement learning, surgical robotics 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# TELEOPERATED surgical robots and shared control sys-tems use force coupling to support precise and safe task execution while preserving the surgeon’s authority under stringent clinical constraints [1]–[4]. Fully manual teleop-eration preserves human authority but often imposes high workload, whereas full autonomy in surgery remains difficult to deploy widely because of safety, regulatory, and account-ability concerns [1], [2], [5]–[7]. Human-robot shared control therefore offers a clinically relevant middle ground between pure teleoperation and full autonomy [8]–[10]. In haptic shared control setting considered here, the human and the robot jointly influence a shared surgical instrument via combined force commands applied at a shared end effector. Such settings require accurate motion execution and strict con-trol of tool-environment interaction for safe manipulation [9], [11]–[14]. Force feedback can further improve operator awareness, motion quality, and force-related safety [4], [15], [16]. Since

the human and the robot are tightly coupled on the same task, purely supervisory or leader-follower formulations are often insufficient. Instead, this setting is more naturally viewed as a cooperative sequential decision problem within the shared control paradigm. From this perspective, the interaction can be formulated as a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problem, in which the human-control and robot-control sides are modeled as coupled agents with a shared task objective. This model-free formulation permits joint policy optimization without a complete analytical model of the coupled behavior. However, MARL for this tightly coupled form of shared control remains only weakly explored. In surgical robotics, existing MARL work mainly addresses coordination among robotic agents [17], whereas continuous force coordination between a learned human control policy and a robot policy remains insufficiently studied. The choice of human control representation is another open question. Analytical formulations based on fixed impedance parameters or predefined cost functions assume essentially static behavior [12], [18], while data-driven methods trained offline do not jointly adapt the human and robot policies through interaction during training [19]–[21]. Consequently, online bidirectional co-adaptation remains insufficiently cap-tured. How robot policies trained with learned or fixed human control perform with real operators remains unclear. Safety handling in haptic shared control remains an open re-search problem. Penalty-based methods encode safety as a soft objective rather than an explicit constraint [5], while shielding methods depend on sufficiently accurate models of the coupled human–robot dynamics [13]. Epigraph-based MARL supports low-violation learning [22], but its performance–safety trade-offs in haptic shared control remain insufficiently studied [23], [24]. To address these issues, we establish a framework for simulation and real operator evaluation of haptic shared con-trol with safety-aware MARL under centralized training and decentralized execution. The framework compares learned and fixed human control conditions and evaluates penalty learning and the epigraph route under a common environment, geo-metric partition, and evaluation criteria. Frozen robot policies are then tested with two real operators through a calibrated coupling between the device and simulation. arXiv:2610.15422v1 [cs.RO] 11 Oct 2026 The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1) We formulate the shared control problem as a coopera-tive Markov game, enabling force-level shared control without a complete analytical model of the coupled interaction. 2) We compare a learned human control policy with a fixed impedance baseline, then evaluate the resulting frozen robot policies with real operator input through a cou-pling interface calibrated from the same two operators’ motion data. 3) We adapt an epigraph-constrained MARL formulation to this shared control setting for explicit geometric safety learning and compare it with MADDPG [25] and rMAPPO [26] under common evaluation criteria. 4) We design a geometric reward and constraint design based on a region partition to compare penalty-based learning and the epigraph route. We evaluate all three routes across spherical, cubic, transferred, and rigid nonconvex geometries, complemented by real operator task and force allocation analyses. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Shared Control in Surgical and Haptic Robotics 

Shared control is an important paradigm in surgical robotics since it provides an intermediate level of assistance between fully manual teleoperation and higher levels of autonomy [1], [2], [27]. Shared guidance or shared goal-level control keeps the human in the loop while the robot assists through virtual fixtures, path guidance, or safety intervention [9], [10], [14], [28]. For example, Tian et al. [9] and Iovene et al. [28] devel-oped shared control frameworks for osteotomy that improve path accuracy and reduce operator burden, while Qin et al. 

[13] introduced a supervisory controller with force constraints for safer teleoperation. These approaches usually retain an asymmetric structure in which the robot guides, filters, or constrains the operator [1], [9], [27], [28]. In the tightly coupled haptic shared control setting con-sidered here, human and robot commands are generated in a common control space and combined at a shared end effec-tor. Game theoretic approaches provide a formalism for this interaction [1], [14], [27]. Music and Hirche [12] formulated a continuous-time differential game for haptic cooperative track-ing and showed that game-theoretic modeling can improve cooperation while preserving intuitive interaction, assuming known human and robot cost functions. Li et al. [11] modeled trajectory-level shared control as a nonzero-sum differential game and used reinforcement learning to approximate value functions online, enabling cooperative policies that balance task performance and interaction effort. These studies support viewing haptic shared control as a joint decision problem rather than a purely supervisory one. MARL has also been applied to high-level human-robot collaboration (HRC) problems, including multi-agent task scheduling and collaborative assembly or disassembly plan-ning [29], [30]. These studies demonstrate the usefulness of Markov game formulations for multi-agent coordination, but primarily address discrete task allocation and sequencing rather than continuous force coordination at a shared end effector. Existing work rarely examines how a computational training controller affects a robot policy that is later paired with a real operator, while also comparing safety learning across varied geometry. This motivates the cooperative Markov game formulation adopted in this work. 

B. Human Control Representations 

A central question in shared control is how to represent the human contribution during controller development. Existing approaches include static control models, offline learning, joint adaptation, and hybrids of these strategies. Static models describe the human contribution through impedance characteristics, predefined objectives, or analytical control principles [1], [12]. These formulations are inter-pretable and tractable, but assume fixed behavior and cannot express variation between operators or strategies. Offline learning methods infer task-relevant skills, assis-tance policies, latent intent, or user-related behavior from demonstrations through inverse reinforcement learning, imi-tation learning, or related techniques [2], [18]–[21]. However, these approaches rely on offline-trained representations and do not jointly optimize human and robot policies through interaction during training. Joint adaptation instead treats both control policies as adap-tive decision makers that evolve through repeated computa-tional interaction. In this work, the learned human control policy and robot policy are initialized without demonstra-tion pretraining and optimized jointly during simulation. The learned policy is a computational training partner, not avalidated model of human neuromuscular behavior. Hybrid paradigms combine demonstration based learning with subsequent adaptation to reduce exploration risk and im-prove data efficiency [31], [32]. Co-GAIL, for example, learns a joint human-robot policy from human-human demonstrations and subsequently optimizes its two components during training [33]. Such approaches depend on the availability and quality of demonstrations, which can be expensive to obtain in surgical settings [2], [19]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we compare a fixed impedance baseline, for which only the robot policy is trained, with a learned condition in which both policies adapt jointly. The resulting frozen robot policies are then paired with real operators to evaluate compatibility rather than fidelity to human behavior. 

C. Safety-Aware Learning for Surgical Collaboration 

Safety is a primary concern in surgical robotics, where ex-cessive interaction forces, unintended contacts, and workspace violations can compromise procedural quality [3], [4], [13], [23], [24], [34], [35]. Penalty-based methods encode unsafe events directly in the reward and integrate easily with standard reinforcement learning algorithms, but they treat safety as a soft objective and therefore do not directly optimize an explicit safety constraint [5]. Runtime safety filters based on control barrier functions (CBFs) or quadratic programs (QPs) modify nominal com-mands online and can provide direct intervention when their model and feasibility assumptions hold [13]. The epigraph route studied here instead incorporates the geometric con-straint into policy learning, so the nominal policy is optimized under that constraint. These strategies are complementary: the present study does not establish superiority over CBF or QP filtering, whose application to tightly fused human-robot commands additionally requires a suitable interaction model [13], [23]. Constrained optimization methods instead impose explicit safety constraints during learning through constrained Markov decision processes [5], [22]. Standard Lagrangian methods can become numerically unstable when the safety threshold ap-proaches zero because the dual variable may grow excessively to enforce the constraint [22]. The epigraph formulation instead introduces an auxiliary variable and combines the safety and performance branches through a maximum operator. Its additive use of the auxiliary variable avoids scaling the policy gradient directly with a growing multiplier under zero or low violation objectives [22]. Although promising for safe multi-agent learning, this approach has not yet been studied in tightly coupled haptic shared control, and systematic comparison between penalty-based and epigraph-constrained safety learning across convex and nonconvex constraints in this setting remains limited, see e.g. [23], [24]. A. Task and Environment 

The experiments are conducted in Isaac Lab on an ab-stracted haptic shared control task. The task abstracts pre-contact surgical navigation: the two sides apply forces to a shared EEF that follows a reference trajectory, generates its own avoidance motion around a rigid obstacle, and then re-joins the path. No obstacle-avoidance trajectory is prescribed. Human control is provided by either a learned policy or fixed impedance, and replaceable obstacle geometries support the evaluation settings described below. The evaluated constraints are spherical, cubic, and rigid nonconvex; the last is a concave, perforated conical shell whose nominal path crosses two separated walls. Its lower efficiency in several settings shows that joint adap-tation is not automatically beneficial. The real operator study therefore evaluates robot policy compatibility. Although the fixed versus learned efficiency trend persists, method rankings and force allocation change, showing that coordination learned with a computational partner need not transfer unchanged to real operators. The results reveal geometry- and metric-dependent differ-ences among the methods. rMAPPO often achieves stronger progress or tracking, whereas the epigraph route more consis-tently limits observed exposure, although its wider avoidance can reduce efficiency. No method consistently dominates in the nonconvex task under the available training budgets. A potential extension is to combine learned constraint-aware policies with CBF or QP safety filters, subject to suitable interaction models and feasible runtime corrections. The nonconvex study uses a rigid object, three seeds, un-equal available training horizons, and one evaluation episode per seed; it is not a comparison at matched convergence and does not represent deformable tissue. The broader simulation comparison also uses limited geometry and randomization, and its exposure rates are empirical rather than formal safety guarantees. Controller form and the number of trained actors differ across complete configurations, so the data do not isolate one architectural factor. The real operator evidence is descriptive: it covers two operators, one spherical task, five episodes per condition, and one robot checkpoint per condition. The same operators contributed the motion trials used to calibrate the device coupling, so this is not an independent validation cohort. The results support feasibility and compatibility analysis, not population or clinical claims. The Phantom provides no direct force measurement, so the human command is reconstructed through the calibrated coupling controller. VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This work established a simulation and real-operator frame-work for safety-aware MARL in haptic surgical shared control. MADDPG, rMAPPO, and an epigraph-constrained route were compared with fixed and learned training partners on the spherical task. Learned policies were also tested on cubic and transferred geometries, and fixed-impedance policies on nonconvex geometry. All methods used common observations and geometric metrics but route-specific objectives. In simula-tion, rMAPPO generally achieved the highest composite scores and tracking ratios, while MADDPG was more sensitive to geometry and training condition. The epigraph route most con-sistently limited observed exposure, although wider avoidance sometimes reduced efficiency. Where directly comparable, fixed-impedance training generally produced higher scores and shorter execution than joint learning. In the descriptive two-operator study, all fixed-impedance-trained policies com-pleted every recorded trial. Learned-partner training reduced MADDPG and rMAPPO reliability and increased opposition between robot and reconstructed operator-side commands. Both epigraph policies completed every trial, and the fixed condition showed the most aligned commands. Thus, coordi-nation learned with a computational partner may not persist with real operator input. Future work will evaluate larger independent cohorts, ran-domized conditions, direct force sensing, broader geometries, deformable interaction, full robot dynamics, latency sensitivity, and comparisons with CBF/QP safety filters.
