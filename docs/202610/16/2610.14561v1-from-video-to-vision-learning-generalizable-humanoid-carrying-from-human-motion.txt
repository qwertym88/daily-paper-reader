# From Video to Vision: Learning Generalizable Humanoid Carrying from Human Motion 

Ziluo Ding X-Humanoid 

Abstract —In the field of whole-body interaction between hu-manoid and scene objects, achieving generalizable and flexible object interaction across diverse scenarios has remained a long-standing challenge. Most existing approaches rely on video or motion-capture data for reinforcement learning (RL) training; however, all of these methods require an upper-level module to generate reference trajectories in real time. In object carrying tasks, the humanoid can only lift objects from designated locations along the reference trajectory, resulting in poor spatial generalization. Moreover, existing policies can only be evaluated inside a motion-capture studio. To address these limitations, we propose MOVER, a multi-stage, Teacher-Assistant-Student, RL training framework for humanoid object carrying. We first collect data from videos and employ GENMO and GMR to retarget the video data, combining this with data augmentation techniques to generalize the data across objects of varying shapes and sizes. In the first stage, building upon the DeepMimic [1] motion-training framework, we train the humanoid to transport objects from specified locations along designated trajectories. In the second stage, we relax the tracking of the reference motion and adjust the reward so that the humanoid learns to transport objects from different positions. In the third stage, we apply a Dagger-PPO distillation method to distill away the proprioceptive position observations and reference joint position of the humanoid. This allows the deployed policy to operate without relying on global position information from a motion-capture system; instead, the humanoid can directly perceive the object’s pose from its onboard RGB-D camera to complete the carrying task. We design a series of simulation experiments to compare the success rates across stages (Stages 1 vs. 2) and before versus after distillation. We further transfer the policy to a real humanoid, successfully transporting objects at varying positions in a motion-capture-free setting. 

Index Terms —Humanoid, Object Carrying Reinforcement Learning, Multi-Stage Framework 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Enabling humanoid to interact with surrounding terrain and scene objects during whole-body motion control has attracted increasing attention from the research community. Meanwhile, the demand for humanoid in warehousing and logistics sce-narios is growing rapidly. While significant progress has been achieved in non-interactive motion imitation, such as walk-ing, crawling, dancing, and acrobatic performances, achieving general-purpose and flexible interaction between humanoids and scene objects remains a significant challenge. Using humanoid carrying as a representative application. For objects with varying placements and states, existing methods generally rely on training with large amounts of reference trajectory data. However, such training data can hardly cover every possible humanoid-object interaction posture. When the agent encounters trajectories that substantially differ from those seen during training, real-world deployment tends to produce unstable humanoid motions. Moreover, most existing methods can only be evaluated within mocap studios, where optical mocap is used to obtain the poses of both the humanoid and the object, severely restricting their applicability to production environments. To address these issues, we propose MOVER , a multi-stage humanoid learning framework for carrying task. We first extract reference motion data from videos of humans carrying objects, retarget the motion data to the humanoid’s trajectories, and employ data augmentation strategies to generate a large volume of training trajectories. These reference motions are integrated into our RL training framework. In the first stage ,we train a 29Dof Tiangong 3.0 humanoid to strictly track reference trajectories while carrying objects, yielding a teacher policy. In the second stage , our objective is to transition the humanoid from ”imitating fixed motions” to ”autonomously completing carrying tasks.” To this end, we relax the strict trajectory tracking requirements. Instead, we strengthen the rewards for physical interaction with objects and for track-ing feedback during the carrying process. This enables the humanoid to learn to locate and transport objects placed at different locations. In the third stage , we apply the Dagger-PPO [4] distillation method, using the Stage-2 policy as the assistant policy, to distill away the humanoid’s proprioceptive position observations and reference joint position information. At deployment, the humanoid perceives the relative position and pose of the object directly through its onboard vision system, enabling vision-based object tracking and carrying. However, the resulting student policy still retains reference information about the humanoid’s anchor pose. If this informa-tion is further distilled away, the humanoid cannot determine the direction of the destination after picking up the object. To completely eliminate reference motion observations, we design a LSTM neural network trained with supervised learning on large-scale motion data, which takes as input the object’s pose and the target position, and outputs the reference orientation of the humanoid. The final deployed policy’s observations contain only the humanoid’s proprioceptive information and the object’s relative pose information. Experimental results demonstrate that, on the object-carrying task, our method arXiv:2610.14561v1 [cs.RO] 10 Oct 2026 achieves a success rate of 93% in simulation, surpassing existing approaches. The distilled student policy transfers successfully to a physical humanoid, where it can complete carrying tasks in general, mocap-free scenes and exhibits a broader object-position generalization range than most existing humanoid object-carrying methods. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: 1) We propose a multi-stage humanoid learning framework that, given a single reference motion, effectively enables the humanoid to learn to grasp and transport objects at varying positions. 2) We design an neural network trained with supervised learning on reference motion data to learn the mapping from object-humanoid relative pose to humanoid refer-ence rotations, completely eliminating reference motion observations from the deployed policy’s observations. 3) We designed multiple sim-to-real experiments and suc-cessfully verified that our policy can complete object-carrying tasks for objects at different positions using vision alone. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Human Motion Recovery and Retargeting 

Human motion is an effective source of reference data for humanoid control, but the embodiment gap prevents direct transfer. Whole-body geometric retargeting matches selected body poses through inverse kinematics [5]–[9]. He et al. [6] and Luo et al. [7] instead fit human-body representations to the humanoid, while Tessler et al. [8] uses scaled Cartesian targets. These methods can introduce foot sliding, penetration, or self-collision when body proportions and contacts are not handled well. GMR reduces these artifacts through rest-pose align-ment, non-uniform local scaling, and two-stage constrained optimization [9]. Video-based pipelines reduce the need for motion-capture systems. SFV learns physical skills from monocular video [10], and Peng et al. [11] recovers world-grounded human motion under camera movement. Recent systems extend this idea to humanoid-scene and humanoid-object interaction [3], [12]–[14]. Li et al. proposed GENMO [15] to unify motion estimation and generation, which can provide robust priors under occlusions. We use GENMO for video-only human motion recovery and GMR [9] for humanoid retargeting, then augment the object and interaction states for broader spatial and geometric coverage. 

B. Humanoid Whole-Body Interaction 

Humanoid whole-body motion skill learning has achieved significant progress in recent years. Prior works on char-acter imitation learning in computer graphics have laid the foundation, among which the DeepMimic [1] paradigm has been widely adopted. However, compared to virtual charac-ters in graphics, humanoid must satisfy additional physical constraints, including joint limits, collision avoidance, and dynamics feasibility [16]–[18]. Consequently, humanoid mo-tion skill learning presents greater complexity than character animation. Some works use deep reinforcement learning to learn object interaction, they need to design complex reward functions in multiple stages, and due to the lack of reference actions, the actions learned by the humanoid are significantly different from normal human actions [19]–[21]. Methods such as ASAP [22] and BeyondMimic [23] provide standardized imitation learning frameworks for humanoid, enabling high-fidelity reproduction of retargeted human motions on physical platforms. Nevertheless, these approaches have not been ex-tended to humanoid-object interaction scenarios. HDMI pioneered whole-body interactive imitation learning, successfully demonstrating tasks such as door opening, box carrying, and chair unfolding [13]. The recent OmniRetarget introduces a retargeting pipeline for scene-interactive motions, producing high-quality reference motions that achieve ex-cellent imitation with minimal reward engineering [3]. Both methods, however, heavily rely on reference motions and struggle to generalize to objects in varied configurations. Furthermore, OmniRetarget does not incorporate object po-sition information in the actor observation, limiting objects to fixed poses matching the reference motion during testing. Our approach employs a multi-stage RL training framework that substantially reduces dependence on reference motions, enhancing generalization for object carrying and enabling effective handling of objects in diverse scenes and poses during real-world deployment. A. Video-to-humanoid Motion Data Generation 

Direct video retargeting produces only one humanoid–object trajectory, limiting the variation in object geometry and place-ment. We augment the object configuration while preserv-ing the demonstrated hand–object relationship, and adapt the upper-body motion using constrained inverse kinematics. This produces contact-consistent references across different object sizes, poses, and support heights using only a small set of videos. Given an RGB video V = {Ii}Ki=1 of a person carrying a object, we use the estimation mode of GENMO [15] to recover the human motion. Only video-aligned conditions are enabled; text, music, and keyframe inputs are masked out. The estimated state is hi = ( hroot ,i , h joint ,i ), where hroot ,i is the world-grounded root pose and hjoint ,i contains the SMPL joint rotations [24]. Sequence-level estimation separates camera and human motion and remains stable under partial object occlusion. We map {hi} to the humanoid with GMR [9]. Human and humanoid key bodies are aligned in their rest poses, and non-uniform local scaling accounts for different body proportions. Two-stage inverse kinematics then tracks link orientations and key-body positions under the humanoid joint limits. Each frame is initialized from the previous solution to keep the motion smooth. B. RL Training Setup 1) Stage 1 RL Training Setup: After processing video data to obtain enhanced motion trajectory data, this RL training is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The core training objective is for the humanoid to learn to transport objects to designated positions in the environment. We employ PPO [25] as the reinforcement learning algorithm. The MDP observation space encompasses the humanoid’s proprioception, object states in the scene, and reference trajectories for object carrying.The action space directly outputs joint angles for the humanoid’s entire body. 

Observation : Since we adopt a multi-stage RL training setup, each stage has distinct observation spaces and reward functions. In the first stage, we focus on training the humanoid to rigorously follow the reference trajectory and transport objects to designated positions. Stage 1 serves as the teacher model, where the observation space incorporates richer refer-ence trajectory information. In the teacher model training, we implement an asym-metric actor-critic architecture [26]. The critic’s observation space, beyond the standard observations, also includes the humanoid’s body position and orientation from the reference motion. Since we intend to test the teacher model in amotion capture room, the actor’s observation space excludes the humanoid anchor’s linear velocity, keeping it only in the critic. 

Rewards : In our task, the humanoid not only needs to track the specified motion trajectory, but also needs to move the object along the reference trajectory. Here, we employ five reward functions, as detailed in Table VII:

Body Tracking : DeepMimic-style tracking term for body position, orientation, linear and angular velocity.  

> •

Object Tracking : tracking term for object position and orientation.  

> •

Guided Contact Reward : tracking the relative position and orientation between the humanoid’s palm and the object’s center in the reference motion, and the contact state between the humanoid’s hand and the object.  

> •

Regularization Penalties : Self-Collision, Action Rate, Soft Joint Limit, joint acc, joint vel limit, joint torque  

> •

Foot Sliding Penalty : feet slide, foot contact consistency. Both Body Tracking and Object Tracking reward func-tions adopt a Gaussian-shaped exponential error form, where smaller errors yield higher rewards.

Instead, we only track the position and orientation of key body parts such as the feet, legs, and upper arms. The reward weight for orientation tracking is set higher than that for position tracking to prevent the humanoid from learning aberrant poses. By assigning different reward weights to key body parts, training converges faster and the humanoid learns the correct carrying behavior more easily. During training, we do not perform random or adaptive sampling on the reference motion. Instead, we always sample starting from the first frame of the motion. This is because in the object-carrying task, randomly sampling to the middle of the motion cannot guarantee that the humanoid can correctly carry the object. 

2) Stage 2: Assistant RL Training Setup for Enhanced Generalization: A key objective of this work is to improve the generalization of humanoid object carrying tasks. Through few-shot learning to rapidly adapt to varied manipulation scenarios, we seek to develop more robust and generalizable carrying policies. To enable the humanoid to transport objects at varying positions in the second stage, we modify the reward function by removing the global anchor position tracking reward and the humanoid’s self-body position tracking reward. We retain the body orientation tracking reward while increasing object position tracking and contact rewards. This encourages the humanoid to learn to transport objects at different positions while maintaining proper body orientation. The observations remain consistent with the first stage. Regarding termiations, we increase the threshold for anchor position tracking error compared to stage 1. Note that removing the self-body position tracking reward may lead the humanoid to learn motions ex-ceeding joint limits. Therefore, we add a termination condition for joint angle limit violations. This training setup reduces the humanoid’s reliance on pro-prioceptive position and body position information, allowing it to focus more on object position and contact tracking. This facilitates the distillation process in stage 3, where proprioceptive position observations are removed. 

3) Stage 3: Distillation of Proprioceptive Position Observa-tions: Intuitively, humans do not need to know their absolute position in the world coordinate system when carrying a ob-ject, only the object’s position relative to the body is required. Motivated by this observation, we perform distillation in the third stage to remove proprioceptive position observations from the policy input. The resulting student policy retains only the minimal set of observations necessary for the task, which are summarized as follows:  

> •

Reference Motion: the anchor orientation error between the reference motion and the current humanoid state.  

> •

Robot Proprioception: joint position, joint velocity, base angular velocity, and previous actions, with a history length of 5.  

> •

Object Perception: object position, object orientation, and object contact point positions. We attempt to remove all reference motion guidance during the distillation stage, but this prevents the humanoid from determining the direction of the target, since after lifting the object, the object’s relative pose no longer provides guidance to the humanoid. We adopt the Dagger-PPO algorithm for distillation, imple-mented following the rsl-rl codebase. This algorithm combines a standard teacher-student distillation scheme with PPO. At the beginning of training, the student policy primarily imitates the teacher’s actions under the reduced observation set. Once the imitation policy saturates the achievable reward, the PPO loss is introduced to further optimize the policy. A KL divergence constraint is imposed to limit the distributional discrepancy between the student and teacher outputs, while the coefficients of the remaining PPO loss terms are kept consistent with stage 1. The teacher provides PPO with a stable behavioral prior, and PPO further refines the teacher behavior using environment re-wards, allowing the student to potentially exceed the teacher’s performance. After distillation, the resulting policy achieves a higher average reward than standard distillation baselines, effectively mitigating the performance degradation caused by information mismatch when purely imitating the teacher. This translates to improved robustness in both simulation and real-world deployment. 

C. Anchor Pose Regression for Reference Motion-Free De-ployment 

A policy conditioned on reference motion during training inevitably inherits a trajectory-following bias at deployment, preventing it from acquiring genuine generalization capability. To completely eliminate the reference motion signal from the observation space at inference time, We design an LSTM-based [27] neural network, named QuatNet , which is trained via supervised learning on the object’s relative pose and the reference orientation of the humanoid’s anchor point from the reference motion dataset. The inputs to the training set are the object’s position and quaternion orientation relative to the humanoid, the humanoid’s current joint positions, and the target transport position, while the label is the reference orientation of the humanoid’s anchor point. The LSTM network infers the subsequent transport direction from the humanoid’s current joint positions and the target position.The architecture of QuatNet is illustrated in Fig 3. Training is performed by minimizing the L2 distance between the predicted and ground-truth anchor orientation quaternions.

D. Vision-based Object Pose Estimation 

At deployment, a fiducial tag attached to the object is detected by the humanoid’s head-mounted RGB-D camera. Let P , C, M , and O denote the pelvis, camera, tag, and object frames, respectively. The object pose in the pelvis frame is computed as P TO (t) = P TC (qt)C TM (t)M TO , where  

> C

TM (t) is obtained from tag detection, and M TO is fixed after the tag is mounted on the object. Since the head can rotate, P TC (qt) is computed from the current head joint configuration qt through forward kinematics, together with the calibrated camera extrinsics. The resulting object position and orientation in the pelvis frame are provided to the student policy. To handle intermittent tag occlusion, we use a visibility-aware pose update that combines visual detection with hand kinematics. Each tag measurement is validated based on its reprojection error and consistency with recent observations before temporal smoothing. During a short loss of detection, the object pose is propagated using recent valid observations. After the object is grasped, the object–hand transform remains approximately fixed, allowing the object pose to be updated from the palm poses through forward kinematics when the tag is occluded by the arms. Visual measurements are used again once the tag becomes visible. If detection is lost for an extended period before grasping, the humanoid pauses until a valid observation is recovered.

C. Results of ablation experiments 

Following the MOVER training pipeline, we compare four policies trained under different configurations: (1) without data augmentation, (2) stage-1 teacher policy, (3) stage-2 assistant policy, (4) stage-3 student policy. We evaluate all policies across four different object positions in simulation, measuring three metrics: grasping success rate (whether the object is successfully lifted by the humanoid), carrying success rate (whether the object is delivered to the vicinity of the target location), and carrying error (the Euclidean distance between the final object position and the target). As shown in Table II, across varying object position off-sets, the pick-and-place success rate decreases progressively, with larger offsets leading to more pronounced degradation. Meanwhile, training strategies with data augmentation consis-tently achieve higher success rates compared to those without augmentation. When there is no position offset, the stage-1 teacher policy and stage-2 assistant policy achieve similar carrying success rates; however, as the offset grows, the assistant policy signifi-cantly surpasses the teacher policy, demonstrating its superior position generalization capability. When the student policy is distilled to discard proprioceptive position information, its car-rying success rate under large position offsets drops noticeably below that of the non-distilled student policy, suggesting that proprioceptive position cues assist the humanoid in estimating the carrying target location. In our experiments, the actual delivery endpoint tends to fall slightly beyond the intended target. Moreover, after DAgger-PPO distillation, the student policy consistently achieves higher grasping success rates than both the teacher and assistant policies. We further conducted a comparative analysis of the grasping effects between the stage-1 and stage-2 policies in MuJoCo, as shown in Fig 4. Under conditions of significant object position offset, the stage-1 policy struggles to accurately guide the humanoid to the front of the object’s placement location, owing to its dependence on the reference motion trajectory. In contrast, the stage-2 policy exhibits a notable ability to track the object’s position throughout the manipulation.

E. Real-world test result 

For real-world evaluation, we use a motion-capture room to measure the global poses of the humanoid and the object. These measurements are provided as observations to evaluate the teacher policy. In a conventional indoor environment, an RGB-D camera estimates the object’s pose relative to the humanoid, and these relative observations are used to evaluate the student policy. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we presented MOVER , a multi-stage whole-body interaction training framework for humanoid object-carrying. We first retargeted video data into humanoid ref-erence trajectories and injected augmented object trajectories into the retargeted data. A teacher-assistant-student whole-body interaction imitation learning pipeline was then per-formed on these trajectories. Through the assistant policy training, the humanoid learns to transport objects placed at varying positions and can complete carrying tasks even when global position information from a motion-capture system is

This enabled reference-motion-free object interaction in simulation. In simulated object-carrying experiments with varying object positions, our approach achieved a higher success rate than existing methods. Finally, we deployed the policy on a physical humanoid and successfully completed the prescribed task in a motion-capture-free setting. Despite these promising results, our work still has several limitations. First, the volume of training data is insufficient, and the humanoid struggles to generalize to object-carrying behaviors with diverse shapes and motion patterns. In addition, after distilling the proprioceptive position observations out of the student policy, the humanoid cannot accurately transport the object to the designated location, resulting in a non-negligible error in the target position. In future work, we will further enrich and curate the training data and design a more robust policy that enables the humanoid to handle a broader spectrum of object-carrying behaviors with diverse object shapes.
