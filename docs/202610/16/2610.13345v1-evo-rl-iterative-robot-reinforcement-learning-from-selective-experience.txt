# Evo-RL: Iterative Robot Reinforcement Learning from Selective Experience 

Kai Ye 1, Yifan Jin 1,2,†, Qianhang Luo 1,3,†, Hongru Zhang 1,†, Jiedong Zhang 1,Shuyuan Hu 1, Song-Yuan Wu 4, Jihai Xu 4, Jie Zhao 4, Bo Zhao 1,∗

Abstract — Existing real-world reinforcement learning meth-ods improve robot policies by combining human corrections with autonomous rollouts. However, accumulated experience varies in its value for policy improvement, and even successful trajectories can contain inefficient or redundant behavior, moti-vating more selective experience reuse. To this end, we propose Evo-RL, an iterative reinforcement learning method that com-bines human interventions with advantage-guided experience selection. Evo-RL uses human corrections and experience with positive estimated advantages from successful trajectories to iteratively refine the policy. To support learning from these different experience sources, we introduce RW-RL-Dataset, which spans over 1,000 hours across four robot embodiments. On a real-world high-precision manipulation task, Evo-RL improves attempt success rate over RECAP by 30.46%. Our code, model checkpoints, and dataset are publicly available. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models generate robot ac-tions by conditioning on visual observations and language instructions [1]. Recent advances have extended their appli-cability to a wider range of tasks and robot embodiments [2], [3], [4] and improved deployment efficiency [5]. Yet policies trained on demonstrations can still encounter unfamiliar states during deployment, where errors can accumulate and recovery may be difficult [6]. These limitations motivate continued policy improvement using experience collected during execution. Existing approaches support this improvement through human corrections and reinforcement learning. Human inter-ventions supply corrective behavior at states visited by the deployed policy [7], [8], while autonomous rollouts provide feedback beyond the initial demonstrations. RECAP [9] combines demonstrations, autonomous rollouts, and human interventions, using value-estimated advantages to condition policy training and iteratively update the policy with deploy-ment data. The value of accumulated experience for policy improve-ment varies across and within trajectories. Even successful trajectories may contain hesitation, repeated attempts, or other inefficient behavior, so the contribution of experience to policy improvement must be assessed at a finer level. These observations motivate Evo-RL’s selective update procedure: estimated advantages guide the selection of experience for policy updates, while only human-controlled portions of successful intervention trajec-tories are retained for corrective fine-tuning. We propose Evo-RL, an iterative real-world reinforce-ment learning method that combines human corrections with advantage-guided experience selection. Starting from a demonstration-trained policy, Evo-RL collects human in-terventions and autonomous rollouts during deployment. It trains a value model on demonstrations, intervention data, and autonomous experience, using rewards that account for execution time and human takeovers, and then estimates advantages to guide experience selection. For policy fine-tuning, Evo-RL retains only the human-controlled portions of successful intervention trajectories, keeps the original demonstrations, and adds selected positive-advantage ex-perience from demonstrations and successful autonomous rollouts. RECAP trains the policy on accumulated experience with a binary advantage label as an additional input. Evo-RL instead uses estimated advantages to select experience for policy updates and increase the sampling weight of selected experience. The updated policy is then redeployed for the next iteration. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Vision-Language-Action Model and Robot Datasets 

Large-scale robot datasets collectively cover diverse household, office, and industrial manipulation tasks across single-arm, dual-arm, and humanoid platforms [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. This diversity provides a foundation for VLA pretraining, enabling policies to learn reusable manipulation skills across tasks and robots [14]. Building on these skills, models such as OpenVLA can adapt to new tasks and robot setups through fine-tuning on additional demonstrations [2]. However, even after fine-tuning, policies may still fail in situations poorly covered by datasets. Human interventions provide targeted corrections in these situations, yet the re-sulting corrective experience is less systematically captured and organized. 

B. Human Interventions and Data Aggregation 

Out-of-distribution (OOD) situations refer to scenarios that differ from those encountered during training and may therefore challenge the policy’s ability to generalize. During deployment, human interventions can be introduced in such OOD situations, not necessarily only after an execution error occurs. These interventions provide corrective demonstra-tions that can be incorporated into the training data, helping the policy improve its responses when similar situations arise in the future. Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) formalizes this process by querying expert actions for states visited by the current policy and aggregating the resulting state–action pairs into the training set [6]. Building on this paradigm, subsequent research focuses on two related questions: when human interventions should be triggered and how the resulting correction data should be incorporated into policy learning. Some methods let human operators decide when to intervene [7], whereas others use state novelty or failure risk to determine when to request human assistance [15]. Because intervention samples often constitute only a small fraction of the collected data, their influence on policy updates can be limited. IWR addresses this imbalance by increasing the relative weight of inter-vention samples, while using non-intervention samples to regularize policy updates and help retain previously learned skills [8]. However, corrective action labels alone do not explicitly indicate which states are likely to lead to failure or which trajectories are of higher quality. Incorporating such evaluations can provide additional supervisory signals for further policy improvement. 

C. Real-World RL and Iterative Policy Improvement 

Reinforcement learning enables robots to improve their policies through interaction with the environment. Early work on RL for VLA models focused on offline preference alignment[16], [17], such as GRAPE [17], which employs Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [18] to align robot trajectories with human intent. More recent approaches in-corporate experience collected during deployment into VLA post-training. RECAP trains a value function on demon-strations, human interventions and autonomous rollouts then uses advantage-based indicators to condition policy training and guide iterative improvement[9]. Evo-RL organizes policy improvement into a data loop with demonstration-based initialization followed by two complementary stages: fine-tuning with human interven-tions and advantage-guided policy refinement. Starting from demonstration data, we initialize the policy by fine-tuning a pretrained VLA model. Next, human interventions col-lected during deployment are used for policy fine-tuning to improve performance in challenging scenarios. Following intervention-based refinement, we use accumulated expe-rience for advantage-guided policy updates. Algorithm 1 summarizes this loop and the policy updates within each iteration.

These estimates guide experience selection and sampling for policy fine-tuning in each round.

RW-RL-Dataset organizes demonstrations, human inter-ventions, and autonomous executions to support research on iterative robot policy improvement. Figure 2 summarizes four key dimensions of RW-RL-Dataset: robot embodiment diversity, long-horizon real-world executions, frame-aligned semantic annotations, and multi-source collection regimes for iterative policy learning. 

A. Embodiments, Modalities, and Collection Regimes 

The dataset contains more than 1,000 hours of real-world interaction collected on four robot embodiments: AgiBot G2, Galaxea R1Lite, AgileX PiperX, and SO-101. These platforms span industrial mobile manipulation, modular dual-arm systems, and low-cost tabletop learning. G2 provides eight RGB video streams (top, top-back, paired fisheye and stereo views, and two wrist views), together with joint states, end-effector poses and wrenches, gripper/head/waist/base states, and actions. R1Lite provides four RGB streams (top, top-right, and two wrist views), together with arm joint positions and velocities, grip-per/torso/chassis states, chassis IMU readings, end-effector poses, and actions. The PiperX and SO-101 platforms are dual-arm systems. Both are fitted with an external RGB camera and a pair of wrist cameras. PiperX uses 14-dimensional state and action vectors, while SO-101 employs 12-dimensional counterparts. Beyond embodiment diversity, RW-RL-Dataset also covers a broad range of real-world task settings. The data span nine scene domains: store shelves, industrial production, com-mercial kitchens, home spaces, care environments, laborato-ries, warehouses, hotels, and education or training settings. Across these domains, the dataset instantiates more than 30 task templates, including object sorting, shelf replenishment, scanning and bagging, precision insertion, garment folding, and box stacking. RW-RL-Dataset further distinguishes episodes by how the actions are generated during collection. Demonstrations record human execution; autonomous episodes record policy execution; and intervention episodes combine both, with frame-level controller labels identifying human correction segments. 

B. Data Organization and Hierarchical Semantic Annota-tions 

Each episode in the dataset links visual observations, robot states, and executed actions through episode identifiers and timestamps. These records are accompanied by semantic annotations at three levels of granularity: dataset, episode, and frame (Fig. 3). Dataset-level fields specify the task, embodiment, difficulty, objects, and coordination or preci-sion requirements. Episode-level fields describe instructions, scenes, outcomes, execution quality, and mistakes. They also include action-source metadata, which categorizes episodes into three types: teleoperated demonstrations, autonomous policy rollouts, and human-intervention episodes. Frame-level annotations consist of subtask intervals, along with frame-wise controller labels that identify human and policy actions. A. Experimental Setup and Task Definitions 

We conduct experiments on two robot platforms: PiperX and SO-101. Policies are initialized from π0.5 and deployed with real-time chunking (RTC) [23]. Each trial has a five-minute time limit. A trial is considered a failure if the task is not completed within this limit. The setups for the two manipulation tasks are described below. PiperX insertion. Five screw–sleeve pairs are placed on the tabletop. The robot selects, grasps, aligns, and inserts each screw. Either arm can insert the screw into the sleeve held by the other. Each unit Uj corresponds to inserting one screw into a sleeve. SO-101 folding. Five T-shirts are loosely piled on the desk. The robot retrieves and folds them sequentially. Units U1–

U5 denote retrieval, spreading, folding in half, folding the sleeves, and folding the hem toward the collar. If the garment enters an erroneous state, an operator manually restores it to the state before the failed stage without fully smoothing it out. 

B. Evaluation Metrics 

For PiperX insertion and SO-101 folding, we evaluate the trained models using two metrics. ASR is the total number of successful attempts divided by the total number of attempts, aggregated across U1–U5 and all trials, reported in %. Throughput (TP) is the number of successful tasks per hour. Each Uj cell represents one individual stage: either a PiperX insertion unit or one SO-101 folding stage. It reports the number of successful completions for that stage and the number of attempts made for that stage. An attempt count of 0 indicates that the trial timed out before reaching this stage. 

N is the success target per unit or stage, at which evaluation stops: N = 10 for PiperX and N = 20 for SO-101. 

C. Data Efficiency of Human Interventions 

To evaluate whether targeted human corrections yield greater performance gains than equivalently scaled teleop-erated demonstrations under a fixed data-collection budget, we compare two data collection regimes with the same time budget: one collects demonstrations only, while the other reserves part of the budget (an hour) for human corrections. If interventions are more efficient, the same budget should translate into higher policy performance than additional demonstrations alone. We benchmark a demonstrations-only baseline (SFT) against SFT + Int., which substitutes one hour of teleoperated demonstrations with human-intervention data. The two regimes are evaluated as paired comparisons under identical total budgets, scaled from 2 to 16 hours and trained from the same pretrained checkpoint. With a four-hour budget, adding one hour of human-intervention data raises ASR from 27.03% to 51.52%, and TP from 12.00 to 20.40, compared with the baseline (Table I). The eight-hour mixture achieves an ASR of 78.00%, comparable to 16-hour SFT (78.26%), while reaching a similar throughput of 53.04 versus 53.27 completed stages per hour. Since the policy executes autonomously for most of the episode, human supervision intervenes only at a few critical moments, supplying pre-cisely the data the model most needs and thereby increasing the value of the collected training data. 

D. Intervention and Real-Robot RL 

Beyond the data-efficiency comparison, we evaluate how human interventions and reinforcement learning jointly sup-port continued policy improvement. We conduct ablation experiments on PiperX to assess the contribution of each training stage (Table II). All variants start from the same policy trained on demonstrations for 2.5 epochs (SFT). To evaluate human interventions alone, we fine-tune the policy on demonstrations mixed with one hour of human interventions, sampling interventions with probability p =0.5 (SFT + Int.). Alternatively, we apply advantage-guided selection to the baseline policy using only demonstration data (SFT + RL): advantages computed from the demonstrations identify high-advantage segments, which are then sampled with elevated probability during fine-tuning. The combined setting (Evo-RL) integrates intervention-based fine-tuning with advantage-guided sampling: it mixes original demon-strations, high-advantage demonstration segments sampled with elevated probability, and human-intervention data for policy fine-tuning. High-advantage segments from successful autonomous rollouts are also included in this round. Finally, we repeat the data collection and training cycle to evaluate the benefits of a second iteration (Evo-RL R2).

E. Value Learning and Advantage-Guided Sampling 

Our value models are task-specific and vision-only. Each model consists of a SigLIP visual encoder followed by an MLP output head, and is trained by randomly sampling frames from demonstrations Ddemo , human-intervention episodes Dinterv , and autonomous rollouts Dauto . Value training takes approximately 2 h 19 min on three A100 GPUs for Evo-RL, compared with 3 h 46 min on eight A800 80 GB GPUs for RECAP. The same value model and advantage computation apply to demonstrations and successful autonomous rollouts. We jointly select nonterminal starts with positive advantages in the top 10% across both sources, merging overlapping or adjacent retained chunks. Selected autonomous segments join the original demonstrations and human corrections for fine-tuning; selected demonstration segments receive increased sampling weight. Figure 5 plots the value and advantage estimates over a screw-insertion episode. Gold dots mark the selected starts, and the overlaid images show the corresponding observa-tions. Which segments are retained for training is determined by the advantage at each start, not by high state value alone. The selected states consistently correspond to critical transition phases of the task: grasping the target object, manipulating it (inserting the screw into the sleeve), and placing it at the target location. These are precisely the points where small errors most affect downstream execution, and where the policy most needs fine-grained supervision. Notably, states near task completion often exhibit high value yet low advantage, confirming that value alone is insufficient for selecting informative segments. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented Evo-RL, which combines human corrections with advantage-guided experience selection in an iterative policy improvement loop. Corrections address deployment difficulties, while estimated advantages guide subsequent experience selection and sampling. Experiments support their complementary roles: interventions outperform additional demonstrations under matched human-control budgets, and Evo-RL improves attempt success rate and throughput on high-precision and deformable-object manipulation tasks. On PiperX, it outperforms RECAP and either update stage alone, with further gains in a second iteration. To support research on this learning process, RW-RL-Dataset brings together demonstrations, interventions, and autonomous experience, including the training data used in our evaluation.
