# Traffic State Prediction and Control Using a Koopman Operator Framework with Polynomial Observables 

Shakib Mustavee (Member IEEE) 1, Arvind Singh 2, and Shaurya Agarwal (Senior Member IEEE) 3

Abstract — In this paper, we develop a Koopman operator framework for modeling and control of traffic flow governed by the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards (LWR) conservation law. We show that the nonlinear dynamics induced by the Green-shields flow–density relation naturally motivate a dictionary of polynomial observables, while the controlled dynamics become bilinear in the lifted Koopman coordinates. A reduced-order Koopman model is identified from spatio-temporal traffic-density snapshots and used as the prediction model in amodel predictive control (MPC) framework for ramp metering. The resulting Koopman-MPC is compared with two predictive control baselines that use, respectively, a fixed linearized LWR model and the nonlinear finite-volume LWR model. In aone-hour freeway experiment, the Koopman-based controller maintains the traffic density below the critical Greenshields value and closely reproduces the behavior of the nonlinear PDE-based MPC, with a maximum-density difference below 

1% . The Koopman predictor also reduces the mean online model-evaluation cost by approximately one order of mag-nitude relative to the nonlinear PDE predictor. In contrast, the linearized LWR predictor is computationally cheaper but exhibits substantially larger prediction errors and yields a more conservative metering policy. These results demonstrate that the analytical structure of the nonlinear traffic conservation law can be exploited to construct an interpretable data-driven surrogate for efficient traffic prediction and control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

At the macroscopic scale, traffic flow is described in terms of aggregate quantities such as density, flow, and velocity, providing a continuum representation of traffic dynamics. Among macroscopic traffic models, the Lighthill–Whitham– Richards (LWR) model is one of the most widely used [1], [2]. It describes the evolution of vehicle density through a nonlinear scalar conservation law. The LWR model can capture important traffic phenomena such as shock-wave formation and propagation, and queue evolution. Therefore, the model has become a basic building block for traffic-state prediction and control. However, the nonlinear and hyper-bolic nature of the governing partial differential equation (PDE) makes repeated prediction computationally demand-ing in feedback-control applications. This motivates reduced and data-driven representations that retain the essential nonlinear traffic dynamics while providing more efficient prediction models for online control. Koopman operator theory provides one such representation by describing a nonlinear dynamical system through the lin-ear evolution of functions of the state, referred to as observ-ables. Although the Koopman operator is generally infinite-dimensional, finite-dimensional approximations can be con-structed from data. Extended dynamic mode decomposition (EDMD) provides a systematic framework for approximating the Koopman operator using a prescribed dictionary of ob-servables [3], while generator EDMD (gEDMD) extends this viewpoint to approximation of the infinitesimal Koopman generator [4]. These representations are particularly attractive for control because nonlinear dynamics can be lifted into coordinates in which prediction is performed using linear or bilinear evolution operators. Koopman-based predictors have consequently been incorporated into MPC for general nonlinear systems [5], including nonlinear PDEs. Koopman- and DMD-based methods have been applied to a range of traffic problems, including highway traffic flow analysis, signalized traffic prediction and control, modeling queue evolution at intersections, and capturing microscopic and multi-lane traffic dynamics [6]–[10]. Koopman represen-tations have also gained increasing attention in model-based control of traffic flow because the lifted dynamics can serve as computationally efficient predictors within optimization-based control frameworks. For example, Gu et al. integrated a deep Koopman model with MPC for freeway ramp metering [11], and more recent work extended Koopman-based latent dynamics to model-based planning for ramp-metering control [12]. Xiang et al. incorporated LWR-based density evolution into a Koopman framework that couples macroscopic traffic flow with microscopic vehicle dynamics for traffic predic-tion [13]. In addition, Koopman theory was combined with graph-based learning to model and predict nonlinear spatio-temporal highway traffic dynamics [14]. 

Research gap: Despite this progress, the analytical con-nection between the classical LWR conservation law and the construction of Koopman observables has received con-siderably less attention. Existing traffic-oriented Koopman studies have largely relied on DMD, learned latent coordi-nates, or neural-network-based lifting. The observable space has generally not been derived directly from the analytical structure of the governing traffic PDE. Operator-theoretic representations of nonlinear PDEs have been studied in other arXiv:2610.19442v1 [eess.SY] 14 Oct 2026 settings, including Burgers’ equation [15]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the polynomial observable structure induced by the LWR model with Greenshields flux has not been explicitly derived and exploited for traffic control. 

Novelty: In this work, we identify suitable Koopman observ-ables using the mathematical structure of the LWR model and the Greenshields flow–density relation. The quadratic flux naturally leads to polynomial functions of traffic density and provides a direct basis for the lifting functions. We derive their evolution equations, quantify the finite-order trunca-tion error, and extend the formulation to ramp-metering control, yielding a bilinear reduced-order model identified from traffic-density snapshots. The model is used within a receding-horizon controller and compared with MPC based on a fixed linearization and the full nonlinear finite-volume LWR model under identical control settings.

Section II develops the Koopman representation of the LWR model with the Greenshields flow–density relation, discusses the finite-order approximation, and derives the controlled bilinear model used for receding-horizon control. Section III presents the ramp-metering case study, including validation of the learned Koopman predictor and comparison with MPC formulations based on a fixed linearized LWR model and the nonlinear finite-volume LWR model. R AMP -M ETERING CASE STUDY 

We evaluate the proposed Koopman predictor in a freeway ramp-metering case study. The Koopman model is first tested on an unseen traffic trajectory and is then used within a receding-horizon controller. Its performance is compared with predictors based on a fixed linearization of the LWR model and direct nonlinear finite-volume propagation. All controllers use the same nonlinear freeway model, prediction horizon, admissible inputs, and objective function; only the internal prediction model is changed. 

A. Experiment Design 

The case study considers a 5 km freeway governed by the LWR model with the Greenshields flow–density relation. The free-flow speed and jam density are vf = 100 km /h and 

ρj = 150 veh /km , respectively. The normalized upstream and downstream boundary densities are fixed at rin = 0 .30 

and rout = 0.08 . These parameters give a Greenshields capacity of 3750 veh /h and an upstream equilibrium flow of 

3150 veh /h, leaving approximately 600 veh /h of available capacity before additional vehicle inflow is introduced. The on-ramp is located at x = 2 km and is represented by the localized Gaussian source described in Section II. To assess the role of the prediction model, we compare three controllers that differ only in their internal predictor: (i) the proposed reduced Koopman model, (ii) a fixed linearized LWR model, and (iii) the nonlinear finite-volume LWR model.

The measured density is mapped to the polynomial observable space and then projected onto the reduced Koopman coordinates. The Koopman model is used to predict how the traffic state evolves under different candidate control inputs. These predictions are supplied to the controller, which selects an input based on the expected future traffic conditions. For the present case study, the input corresponds to the metered vehicle flow entering from the on-ramp. The selected control input is applied to the nonlinear LWR traffic model, which represents the actual freeway dynamics and is referred to as the plant in the control framework. The resulting traffic-density field becomes the updated state for the next control step, closing the feedback loop. The same nonlinear freeway model is used as the plant in all controlled cases, while only the prediction model inside the controller is changed. This allows the proposed Koopman, fixed linearized LWR, and nonlinear finite-volume LWR predictors to be compared under identical traffic and control settings. The numerical simulation and implementa-tion details are presented in the next subsection. 

B. Numerical Simulation and Controller Setup 

We will compare the controllers under a time-varying ramp-demand scenario. The simulation spans one hour. The ramp demand is specified as 400 , 800 , 1400 , 600 , 1200 , 400 ,

1600 , 1000 , and 400 veh /h over the consecutive intervals 

0–5, 5–10 , 10 –18 , 18 –23 , 23 –33 , 33 –38 , 38 –50 , 50 –55 ,and 55 –60 min , respectively. Four cases are considered: (i) an uncontrolled case in which the full ramp demand is admitted, (ii) Koopman-MPC, (iii) linearized LWR-MPC, and (iv) nonlinear LWR-MPC. The same nonlinear finite-volume LWR model governs the actual freeway traffic in all cases. The three controlled cases differ only in the model used to predict future traffic conditions. Koopman-MPC uses the proposed reduced Koopman predictor, linearized LWR-MPC uses a fixed affine model linearized about r = 0 .43 ,and nonlinear LWR-MPC directly propagates the nonlinear finite-volume model over the prediction horizon. The LWR model is discretized using Nx = 80 finite-volume cells ( ∆x = 0.0625 km ) and propagated using a Rusanov flux with third-order strong-stability-preserving Runge–Kutta (SSPRK3) integration and a 1 s time step. The Koopman model is trained from 25 independent 10 min 

trajectories with inputs held for 30 s ; snapshots are retained every 2 s , yielding 7500 training samples. The Koopman predictor uses polynomial observables up to degree p = 5 ,resulting in 401 lifted coordinates, which are reduced to 40 

coordinates for prediction and control. The controller is up-dated every Tc = 30 s and uses H = 10 prediction intervals, corresponding to a 5 min horizon. To reduce the number of candidate input sequences, two-block move blocking is used. One input is held over the first five prediction intervals and a second input over the remaining five. All three controllers use the same move-blocking structure and the same admissible input grid U = {0, 200 , . . . , 1600 } veh /h.The generic stage cost introduced in Section II-C is specialized to penalize four quantities: (i) density above the desired operating level ( wh = 30 ), (ii) density above the critical value ( wc = 350 ), (iii) withheld ramp demand (wu = 0.35 ), and (iv) changes in the metering input (w∆u = 0.05 ). All weights (wh, w c, w u, w ∆u) are kept identical for the three controllers and selected empirically to place the strongest emphasis on avoiding operation above the critical density while also accounting for ramp admission and smooth metering actions. Weighted traffic-performance objectives with penalties on control variation are commonly used in freeway MPC [18]. The first two terms are evaluated as mean-squared density exceedances over the control region, while the latter two are normalized by the maximum admis-sible ramp flow. For this experiment, the desired operating level is rtar = 0 .43 and the critical density is rc = 0 .50 , and the density penalties are evaluated over the region extending from 0.35 km upstream to 1.20 km downstream of the ramp. At each control update, only inputs that do not exceed the forecast ramp demand are considered. The control sequence with the lowest predicted cost is selected, and only its first input is applied before the traffic state is updated and the optimization is repeated.

IV. C ONCLUSION 

This study showed that the analytical structure of the LWR model can be used to guide the selection of Koopman observables instead of relying entirely on heuristic or learned lifting functions. For the Greenshields relation, the resulting quadratic flux leads naturally to polynomial density observ-ables, and the controlled formulation leads to a reduced bilinear model. The ramp-metering study highlights the main trade-off among the three prediction models considered. The fixed linearized LWR model is the least expensive computa-tionally, but its prediction errors are substantially larger and it produces a more restrictive metering policy. Direct nonlinear LWR prediction provides the highest fidelity but at the largest online cost. The Koopman predictor lies between these two extremes: it closely reproduces the nonlinear MPC response and ramp admission while reducing the repeated online computation by approximately one order of magnitude. As a proof of concept for the proposed polynomial Koop-man observables, the present study focuses on a freeway scenario. The case study uses the Greenshields flow–density relation, a single on-ramp, and synthetic traffic data. The case study demonstrates the potential of the proposed framework in a controlled setting and evaluates the effectiveness of the Koopman-based predictor. Future work will extend the framework to more complex freeway configurations, other flow–density relations, explicit ramp-queue dynamics, and real traffic data. We also plan to investigate additional traffic prediction and control problems. More broadly, the same physics-guided Koopman idea will be explored for other systems governed by nonlinear conservation laws, where the structure of the flux may provide useful guidance for selecting observables.
