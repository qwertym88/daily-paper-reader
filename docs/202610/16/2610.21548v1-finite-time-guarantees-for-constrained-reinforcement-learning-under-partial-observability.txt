## SYCAMORE, EPFL 

Abstract 

We study reinforcement learning in constrained partially observable Markov decision processes (CPOMDPs), where an agent maximizes reward subject to a cost constraint using only incomplete information about the underlying state. The lack of state observability may require optimal policies to depend on arbitrarily long observation-action histories, making planning and learning challenging. We leverage recent advances in finite-memory approximations of POMDPs and represent finite history windows as states of a constrained MDP. We then develop a primal–dual algorithm that provides the first non-asymptotic guarantees that jointly bound average reward suboptimality and constraint violation in the original CPOMDP. A key challenge is that this finite-window approximation affects both reward values and the set of feasible policies. Under a filter-stability assumption, we derive bounds relating optimality and feasibility in the finite-state model to those in the original CPOMDP, with approximation errors that decay geometrically with the window length. Experiments in partially observable gridworlds demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. 

# 1 Introduction 

Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) model sequential decision-making prob-lems in which an agent seeks to maximize its expected cumulative reward without directly observing the state of the environment. Instead, the agent acts on observations that provide only partial information about the underlying state. For example, a robot may not know the exact positions of surrounding objects and must instead rely on noisy camera observations (Lauri et al., 2022). Despite the success of POMDPs in domains such as robotics and healthcare (Ding et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2021), maximizing reward alone does not ensure safe behavior, such as collision avoidance in robotics. Incorporating safety constraints into POMDPs leads to Constrained Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (CPOMDPs), where agents maximize expected cumulative reward subject to constraints under partial observability. Despite their practical relevance and successful empirical applications (Hogewind et al., 2022; Carr et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025), theoretical guarantees for learning in CPOMDPs remain limited. Existing theoretical work has focused primarily on planning with explicit knowledge of the model, providing guarantees on feasibility (Undurti and How, 2010), approximate optimality and feasibility in time polynomial in the model size for any fixed horizon (Khonji et al., 2019), and finite-iteration bounds on reward optimality and constraint satisfaction (Kalagarla et al., 2022). Other work pro-poses an online Monte Carlo planning method that uses a black-box generative simulator and provides an asymptotic convergence result under additional assumptions (Lee et al., 2018). However, these methods do not provide finite-time guarantees for both reward optimality and constraint satisfaction when the model is unknown, and the agent learns solely through interaction with the environment. Addressing this gap requires overcoming a fundamental difficulty inherited from POMDPs. Since the agent receives only partial observations of the underlying state, an optimal policy need not

This history dependence makes both planning and learning challenging. Even with a known model, planning is computationally intractable in general (Pa-padimitriou and Tsitsiklis, 1987); with an unknown model, learning a near-optimal policy requires exponentially many samples in the worst case (Krishnamurthy et al., 2016). To obtain tractable solutions, recent work on POMDPs approximates the full interaction history using a finite window of recent observations and actions (Kara and Yuksel, 2022; Kara and Y¨ uksel, 2023; Cayci et al., 2024; Anjarlekar et al., 2025; Jordan and Kamgarpour, 2026). In particular, Anjarlekar et al. (2025) represent truncated histories as states of a superstate Markov Decision Process (MDP) , and, under a uniform filter-stability assumption, show that the superstate MDP approximates the original POMDP, with the approximation error decreasing with the history length. They also develop an algorithm with finite-time guarantees for learning a near-optimal policy. Building on the same finite-window approximation, Jordan and Kamgarpour (2026) study model-based learning in the induced superstate MDP for the tabular setting and establish improved sample complexity bounds. These results provide a foundation for learning with finite histories, but address only unconstrained reward maximization. We extend finite-history methods to CPOMDPs by representing finite history windows as states of a superstate Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). Our goal is to learn policies in this model while guaranteeing reward optimality and constraint satisfaction in the original CPOMDP. This extension requires addressing three technical challenges. First, history truncation affects both reward and cost values. Thus, a policy that is feasible in the superstate CMDP may violate a constraint in the original CPOMDP. Second, comparing the optimal values of the two constrained problems requires more than bounding the error in individual policy values, since history truncation changes the set of feasible policies. Third, values in the superstate CMDP must be estimated from trajectories generated by the original CPOMDP, which introduces a mismatch that the learning analysis must account for. 

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows. 1. Under a uniform filter stability condition, we establish bounds relating policy values and constrained optima in the superstate CMDP to those in the original CPOMDP, quantifying the effects of history truncation on optimality and feasibility (Propositions 1 and 2). 2. We develop a primal-dual algorithm (Algorithm 1) and establish finite-time bounds on its average optimality gap and average constraint violation in the original CPOMDP, accounting for the estimation error arising when learning from CPOMDP trajectories (Theorem 1). 3. We evaluate our algorithm on a partially observable Gridworld and observe convergence across different history lengths, in accordance with our analysis (Section 6).

## 3.2 Approximating a CPOMDP with a Superstate CMDP 

The finite-history approach replaces the original CPOMDP P with a superstate CMDP Mg . This section examines how accurately the resulting model captures the original problem under a filter-stability condition. First, we bound the differences in reward and cost values between the two models (Proposition 1). We then use this bound to choose the constraint threshold dg for Mg and compare the optimal reward values of Mg and P under their respective constraints (Proposition 2). Both results rely on controlling how much information about the current latent state is lost when the history is truncated. For any history h ∈ H , let bh ∈ ∆( S) denote the posterior over current states given h, i.e., bh(s) := Pr( s | h) for all s ∈ S .The accuracy of the superstate approximation depends on how close the belief bh induced by the full history is to the belief bg induced by its superstate g = Gl(h), defined in Section 3.1. Without 4additional assumptions, observations from the distant past may affect beliefs over the current state, so that the approximation error cannot be controlled in general. We therefore adopt a uniform filter stability assumption, commonly used in the literature on POMDPs (Kara and Y¨ uksel, 2023; Anjarlekar et al., 2025; Jordan and Kamgarpour, 2026). Under this assumption, as stated below, the influence of the past on the current belief decays as new actions and observations are appended to the history.

# 7 Conclusion & Future Work 

We addressed RL in CPOMDPs by representing finite observation-action windows as states of a superstate CMDP. We quantified how this approximation affects both reward values and the set of feasible policies, showing that the resulting errors decay geometrically with the window length under filter stability. Building on these bounds, we developed a primal–dual algorithm with non-asymptotic guarantees on average reward suboptimality and constraint violation in the original CPOMDP. Experiments on a partially observable constrained Gridworld supported our theoretical results. Our analysis is limited to tabular superstate CMDPs and our experiments to Gridworld. Extensions to continuous spaces, realistic benchmarks, and a weaker uniform filter-stability assumption remain for future work.

## F.1 Experimental Setup 

Hardware Specification Experiments are conducted on Apple Mac Studio equipped with Apple M4 Max chip (16-core CPU, 50-core GPU), 64 GB unified memory. 

Environment Details The state space consists of the grid cell coordinates. The action space is 

A = {up , right , down , left }. The observation space is O = {safe , edge , near-hazard , unsafe , goal }.The agent starts from the middle cell of the left boundary. The absorbing goal is located at the opposite of the grid. The agent receives reward +1 whenever the current state is the absorbing goal state, 0 otherwise. The cost is 1 when the current state is unsafe, 0 otherwise. The intended action is executed with probability 0 .7, while with probability 0 .3 one of the four action is sampled uniformly. Boundary-crossing actions leave the agent in place. The nominal observation is emitted with probability 0 .9, while with probability 0 .1 one of the five observations is sampled uniformly. We use the discount factor γ = 0 .9.

## F.2 Other Results 

Performance in the Superstate CMDP Fig. 4 reports the signed average optimality gap and average constraint violation in the superstate CMDP under exact policy evaluation. The constraint violation decreases with K for all tested history lengths. For some l, the positive average optimality gap approximately follows the displayed K−1/2 reference slope. For other l values, it becomes negative. A negative signed gap can occur because the finite-K mixture remains infeasible, attaining a reward larger than the constrained optimum. Average optimality gap and average constraint violation curves must consequently be interpreted jointly. Overall, these curves are qualitatively compatible with the O(K−1/2) optimization terms. 

Effect of Implementing an Estimation Oracle We use persistent tabular TD as an em-pirical implementation of the estimation oracle. The experiment evaluates the practical effect of approximate policy evaluation, it is not intended as an empirical verification of an end-to-end TD sample-complexity bound. At each primal-dual iteration, the TD(0) oracle collects 64 trajectories of horizon 100 under the current policy. An additional 2000 warm-up trajectories are collected during the first iteration. The action-value tables persist across the primal-dual iterations, and, therefore, are not re-initialized. For the nth visit to a superstate-action pair we use as stepsize: 

αn = 0.1(1 + 0 .01 n)1/2 .

Results for l ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, 4} and K ∈ { 100 , 500 , 1000 , 5000 , 10000 } are reported in Fig. 5. Compared with exact policy evaluation, the TD oracle introduces in general an additional bias in both reward and constraint performance. This is qualitatively consistent with the estimation-error terms ∝ δest 

appearing in the bounds. Since the TD estimates are retained across iterations, their actual errors vary with K. This illustrates the viability and the effect of approximate policy evaluation.
