Abstract 

Lung tumor segmentation plays a vital role in early detection and treatment planning of lung cancer. Although state-of-the-art deep learning models achieve high dice score coefficients (DSC), each forward pass requires computing large amounts of floating point operations, making them computationally expensive, requiring GPU acceleration, and having slow inference speed, limiting real-world clinical deployment. We propose a lightweight, energy-efficient model that leverages the ENet architecture for its real-time inference capability and low com-putational cost, enhanced with UNet-style skip connections between encoder and decoder stages for feature reuse and the lightweight Convolutional Block Atten-tion Module (CBAM) for improved feature representation. The model is trained on publicly available data from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) via the Medical Segmentation Decathlon Challenge (MSD) and evaluated on the Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) dataset. Our model achieves a DSC of 0.90812 (threshold of 0.5) with its compact architecture of 430,052 parameters and 0.549 Giga Multiply-Accumulate operations (GMAC), making it up to 10 ×faster, requires 79 × less GMAC than 

1arXiv:2610.33479v1 [cs.CV] 24 Oct 2026 UNet, and has 22 × fewer parameters than SegNet. These characteristics make it well-suited for deployment in clinical settings where computational resources are limited and decrease the cost of utilizing such a system. Additionally, the explain-able AI technique of Grad-CAM is used to provide visual interpretability of the model’s predictions and to identify regions where segmentation performance requires improvement.

# 1 Introduction 

Lung cancer or Bronchial Carcinoma remains one of the most fatal malignancies worldwide, owing to its aggressive nature and often diagnosed at advanced stages [1]. Therefore, improving early detection is crucial, and Computed Tomography (CT), which is considered one of the best modalities for imaging, has emerged as a vital tool in this effort by offering detailed lung tissue imaging for analysis and identification of malignant tumors [2]. The advent of automated segmentation techniques that use deep learning models in Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) systems has gained attention for reducing reliance on manual annotation and improving the consistency of tumor localization [3]. However, these models often rely on computationally expensive 3D convolution operations, which involve a high number of floating-point computations. As a result, they require GPU hardware acceleration for real-time inference on data [4– 6], making them less feasible for deployment in environments with limited resources, such as small clinics or mobile health units, limiting their scalability and accessibil-ity. Thus, energy-efficient neural networks which balance the inference speed, resource usage, and segmentation accuracy are critical for facilitating widespread adoption of AI-driven diagnostics in resource-constrained medical workflows. In order to accurately segment the tumors, numerous neural network architec-tures for image segmentation have been proposed, such as SegNet [7, 8], UNet and its variants [9–11] or fully convolutional networks [12, 13]. Moreover, the integra-tion of transformer-based modules into encoder-decoder architectures such as UNETR [14] and Swin-UNETR [15] has demonstrated improvements in Dice Score Coefficient (DSC) across various tasks. These powerful encoder-decoder architectures and the self-attention mechanism of transformers have large knowledge capacities and work with a high number of feature maps, enabling them to have strong feature represen-tation capabilities. However, the base architecture of UNet and SegNet is based on the VGG-16 architecture [16, 17], a parameter-heavy model designed for multiclass image classification, leading to high Giga Multiply-Accumulate operations (GMAC) and long inference times. The integration of the transformer module, another com-putationally expensive and parameter-heavy architecture, adds to the existing high GMAC and inference time. In resource-constrained conditions, they become unsuitable for real-time use in resource-constrained settings. 2Lightweight attention mechanisms have been developed as a promising way to enhance segmentation performance without additional computational overhead. The attention gate mechanism is a lightweight operation without adding any additional parameters and integrated in the UNet base architecture [18] improving the represen-tation power by reducing the focus on irrelevant target regions and enhancing spatial attention on relevant areas, but it does not account for inter-channel relationships. The Squeeze-and-Excitation module [19] introduced channel-wise attention using global average-pooling, but these features were argued to be suboptimal. The Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) [20] overcomes these by sequentially applying atten-tion maps along both the channel and spatial dimensions and uses global max-pooling along with global average-pooling for channel attention. Understanding what is impor-tant in an image is achieved through the channel attention module while the spatial attention module utilizes inter-spatial relationships of features to focus on where the informative region lies. This design leads to an improved representation power of net-works with minimal computational overhead. Due to the ease of seamlessly integrating CBAM into any Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture with negligible overhead and can be trained jointly with the base CNN, it is an ideal choice for the pro-posed model. Furthermore, its original implementation with the ResNet architecture is structurally similar to the bottleneck modules in ENet, simplifying its integration into our proposed framework. The ENet architecture was originally designed to overcome the limitation of tradi-tional deep learning models that are computationally expensive and unable to perform real-time semantic segmentation on low-power mobile devices and autonomous vehicles [21]. It is specifically optimized for low-latency and resource-constrained environments while maintaining competitive performance in terms of segmentation accuracy. Com-pared to UNet and SegNet, the ENet architecture offers a compelling balance between speed and accuracy. However, while it is computationally efficient, its segmentation accuracy can be further enhanced by improving its attention and feature re-usability capabilities. By integrating CBAM and UNet-style skip connections between encoder and decoder stages, the ENet architecture will have improved representation power and feature reuse through the skip connections. These skip connections also mitigate the need for pretraining the encoder to leverage transfer learning in order to fine-tune the decoder. Further discussion of this point is given in Section 3.2. The main contribution of this research is an energy-efficient architecture for lung tumor segmentation by modifying the ENet architecture by adding UNet-style encoder-decoder skip connections and integrating CBAM into the encoder, improv-ing feature reusability and representation without significant computational overhead, striking a strong balance between segmentation performance and computational efficiency. Demonstration of the model’s effectiveness through comprehensive evalua-tion on the publicly available TCIA dataset [22] through the Medical Segmentation Decathlon Challenge (MSD) and LIDC-IDRI dataset [23], demonstrating that it sig-nificantly outperforms several state-of-the-art architectures without compromising inference speed and GMAC. The proposed architecture is up to 10 × faster, requires 79 × fewer GMAC than UNet and contains 22 × fewer parameters than SegNet, mak-ing it the ideal model for resource-constrained environments like mobile health units 3or rural clinical settings, reducing the cost of usage and eliminating the need for expensive GPU hardware acceleration. 

# 2 Related Work 

With ongoing advances in deep learning-based CAD systems having lead to the creation of numerous convolutional neural networks dedicated to lung tumor and nod-ule research, they predominantly explore three categories: 2D convolutional neural networks, 3D-based methods, and transformer-based methods. 

# 2.1 2D convolutional neural network 

The original U-Net architecture [10, 24], is a 2D encoder-decoder CNN with skip con-nections that enable high-resolution feature fusion for biomedical images. The encoder structure focuses on what features are present in the image, but due to downsampling, spatial information of where these features are present is lost. Hence, the decoder with feature reuse through skip connections from the encoder restores this spatial con-text, improving the segmentation accuracy of the model. Many subsequent 2D models inspired by this base architecture have been introduced with improvements. Atten-tion UNet [18] improves spatial attention in relevant areas by reducing the focus on irrelevant parts through its attention gate mechanism, UNet++ [25] introduces nested and dense skip connections across all levels between the encoder and decoder, pro-viding more flexibility to utilize the features across various levels and thus narrowing the semantic gap between the encoder and decoder. However, a major drawback of 2D networks is the loss of contextual 3D spatial information, which is argued to lead to performance degradation. Since CT scans are inherently volumetric, slice-wise pro-cessing may ignore inter-slice continuity and introduce artifacts or inconsistencies in the segmented output. But working with the complete 3D volume is computation-ally expensive, with the 3D UNet itself having 542.19 GMAC for an input patch of 128 x 128 x 128. Moreover, even with a 2D architecture, the downsampling process exponentially increases the number of feature maps produced by the encoder after each convolution block, and the upsampling process doubles this number through the skip connections. Working with such a high number of feature maps leads to better performance with the trade-off being higher GMAC and more computation. On the other hand, the SegNet architecture mimics this downsampling process while opting to use max-unpooling instead of skip connections to restore lost spatial information [8], allowing the model to work with a similar number of parameters and feature maps as UNet while requiring 1.6 × less GMAC than UNet. 

# 2.2 3D convolutional neural network 

To fully exploit the 3D information of the CT image, 3D UNet was introduced [11, 24], extending the original UNet by replacing 2D convolutions and pooling operations with their 3D counterparts. This helps in learning the volumetric context, which is cru-cial to accurately delineating small nodules with subtle features in adjacent slices. Although 3D UNet offers performance improvements in volumetric segmentation, its 4increased memory footprint and training time pose practical limitations. In an effort to mitigate this, optimized 3D variants have emerged. 3D Res2UNet combines the benefits of Res2Net’s multi-scale feature learning and residual connectivity in a 3D setting [4]. RRc-UNet 3D enhances 3D UNet with residual recalibration, enabling better feature reuse and representation learning [6]. nnU-Net [26], a self-configuring 3D pipeline, automatically adapts its network architecture and training to the tar-get dataset, consistently ranking among the top performers in medical segmentation benchmarks. These models show better performance over their 2D counterparts, espe-cially for small or complex lesions. For example, 3D-Res2UNet captures fine-grained structural information better than 2D variants owing to its deeper architecture and hierarchical feature extraction capability. 

# 2.3 Transformer architectures 

Recent development in deep learning for medical imaging has been significantly influ-enced by the success of Vision Transformers (ViTs), which have shown superior performance in capturing long-range dependencies in image data compared to tradi-tional CNNs [27]. Inspired by their success in natural image tasks, researchers have introduced transformer-based models to medical image segmentation, particularly for 3D volumetric data where global context is crucial. UNETR (U-Net with a ViT encoder for Medical Image Segmentation) [14] is a pioneering work that integrates pure trans-formers into the UNet framework, outperforming 3D UNet and nnU-Net in tasks such as spleen, liver, and brain tumor segmentation. Unlike the traditional UNet which relies solely on convolutional encoders, UNETR uses a ViT encoder that directly pro-cesses the volumetric input through non-overlapping 3D patches. The resulting patch embeddings are passed through multiple transformer layers, capturing global spatial dependencies across the entire volume. The decoder follows the UNet-style design and progressively upsamples the features to construct the final segmentation output. Skip connections between the transformer blocks and corresponding decoder blocks are present to fuse multi-scale semantic features. Swin-UNETR improves on UNETR with the Swin Transformer acting as the encoder [15] and has faster convergence and reduced memory usage, due to efficient windowed attention. The Swin Transformer introduced the shifted window self-attention mechanism, which restricts computa-tion of attention scores to non-overlapping windows while allowing interaction across windows through a shifting mechanism. This design brings linear computational com-plexity relative to image size, making it significantly more efficient for large 3D volumes and outperforming state-of-the-art models for brain tumor segmentation from MRI scans. However, transformer modules require high memory consumption during train-ing and inference because of the computationally expensive transformer encoder and have slower inference speeds compared to CNN-based models. 53 Methods 

# 3.1 Dataset 

The Medical Segmentation Decathlon Challenge (MSD) is a collection of 10 different medical imaging segmentation challenges, each focusing on a specific organ or dis-ease type. Task 6 of the Medical Segmentation Decathlon is dedicated to Lung Tumor Segmentation, which involves segmentation of lung tumors within CT scans. The fol-lowing parameters were used to obtain the images: automatic tube current modulation range of 100–700 mA, a helical pitch, 0.9–1.0, a tube rotation speed of 0.5 s; section thickness below 1.5 mm; 120 kVp; and a sharp reconstruction kernel [28]. These vol-umes were acquired from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) [22] and made publicly available through the MSD challenge. To mitigate the computational expense of processing the whole 3D volume, each CT scan, with dimensions of 512 x 512 x X, where X is a variable that denotes the number of slices present in each CT scan, is converted into individual two-dimensional slices. These two-dimensional slices, as visualized in Figure 1, are used as the training and evaluation datasets. There are roughly 8.5 times more slices that do not contain a tumor, indicating a class imbalance of slices. Undersampling the majority class leads to a small dataset that does not represent the whole structure of the chest and lungs. Hence, oversampling of tumor slices is necessary to prevent the model from learning an all negative class prediction [29]. This makes the model prone to overfitting, especially for attention-based architectures [30, 31] and requires data augmentation to bring more variety to existing data [32]. Hence, it is necessary to select a wide variety of data augmentation techniques that complement each other and are suitable for the given type of disease, imaging modality and organ being diagnosed [29, 33, 34]. A detailed explanation of the selected techniques is given in Section 3.4.  

> Fig. 1 2D slices from the MSD dataset with the CT slice (left), the ground truth segmentation mask (middle) and the overlap of both (right).

While the MSD dataset is used to train and validate the model performance, test-ing is performed on the entire Lung Image Database Consortium Image Collection 6(LIDC-IDRI) dataset. It consists of 1018 instances and 244,527 images stored in Dig-ital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM), with a separate XML file associated with each volume that is produced after a two-phase image annotation pro-cess performed by four expert thoracic radiologists, necessary for creating the ground truth segmentation masks [23]. The test metrics obtained for each model are after comparing the model’s predictions and the ground truth binary mask extracted from these XML files. Although the MSD and LIDC-IDRI dataset contain CT images, they exhibit notable differences in pixel intensity distribution and image quality. In the MSD dataset, the pixel intensities of a CT image are consistently bounded within a fixed range of −1024 to 3071 Hounsfield Units (HU), while the LIDC-IDRI dataset con-tains a wider and more variable intensity range, with values extending from −3024 up to 3888 HU. Additionally, MSD images are generally of higher definition and exhibit consistent lighting conditions, resulting in clearer and more visually coherent scans. In contrast, LIDC-IDRI images can vary significantly in both lighting and resolution, with some scans appearing pixelated or poorly lit with low contrast, making it dif-ficult to analyze the image and accurately distinguish nodule boundaries. Figure 2 visualizes a CT slice with suboptimal lighting, where even a large tumor is difficult to detect. To address these inconsistencies and ensure compatibility across datasets, all pixel intensities in CT images were clipped to the range of −1024 to 3071 HU. Furthermore, data augmentation techniques that simulate variations in lighting and image quality were applied during training to improve model robustness and general-ization across diverse imaging conditions. A detailed explanation of the pre-processing and data augmentation methods is provided in Section 3.4.

# 3.2 Network Architecture 

The proposed network architecture is presented in Table 1 with expanded diagrams for each module in Figure 3. The core design principle of the proposed modified ENet is early downsampling, followed by a deep but narrow network that significantly reduces 7the number of floating point operations for each forward pass, memory footprint, and inference time, without sacrificing accuracy. Its architecture is divided into several stages, as highlighted in the stage column.  

> Fig. 3 (a) Initial block (b) Regular bottleneck module (c) Downsampling bottleneck module (d) Upsampling bottleneck module

The initial block, as shown in Figure 3a, processes the input images through a par-allel max-pooling operation that results in 1 feature map and a convolution operation that results in 15 feature maps. The output of the convolution branch is concatenated with the output of the max-pooling branch responsible for preserving important spatial information. This final output passed through a batch normalization and activation layer. This architecture design is heavily inspired by the Inception architecture [35]. The bottleneck modules are inspired by the ResNet architecture [36], with an extension branch consisting of convolutional filters and a main branch. These branches are then merged through element-wise addition, as shown in Figure 3. This similarity simplifies the process of integrating CBAM as it is added after the convolution oper-ations and before the residual connection in the form of the main branch is added. CBAM is integrated into all regular bottleneck modules of stage 2 and stage 3 of the architecture, improving the feature extraction capabilities on the encoder. Each regular bottleneck module consists of three convolutional layers in the exten-sion branch: a 1 × 1 projection that decreases the feature maps by a factor of the internal ratio value, which is set to 4, a main convolutional layer (termed as ’conv’ in Figure 3b), and a 1 × 1 expansion. The ’conv’ layer is either a regular 3 × 3 convolu-tion, a dilated convolution [37], or a 5 × 5 convolution decomposed into two 5 × 1 and 1 × 5 asymmetric convolutions. According to the integration flag passed to the regular bottleneck module, either CBAM or a skip connection is added. In the downsampling block shown in Figure 3c, an additional 2 × 2 max-pooling layer with stride of 2 is added to the main branch followed by zero padding in the channel dimension. Zero padding ensures equal number of feature maps present in the main and extension branches in order to merge them through element-wise addition. The initial 1 × 1 projection in the extension branch is replaced by a 2 × 2 convolution with stride of 2. The ’conv’ layer is replaced by a 3 × 3 convolution with stride of 1 and 81 layer of padding. In the upsampling block shown in Figure 3d, a 1 × 1 convolution is performed, exchanging number of feature maps to increase the feature map size, followed by 2 × 2 max-unpooling with stride of 2 in the main branch. The ’conv’ layer from the extension branch is replaced with a 2 × 2 transposed convolution of stride 2. Spatial dropout is used as the regularizer with p = 0 .01 before bottleneck2.0 and 

p = 0 .1 afterward. Batch normalization and activation layers are placed between all convolutions in a module. The encoder modules use PReLU as the activation, whereas the decoder modules use ReLU as the activation.

The initial stage contains a single initial block. Stage 1 consists of five bottleneck blocks, while stages 2 and 3 have the same structure, after excluding the first down-sampling block of stage 2. The initial stage along with stages 1, 2 and 3 make up the network encoder, while stages 4 and 5 make up the network decoder. 9The indices of the max-pooling features are saved for the corresponding unpooling operations inspired by the SegNet architecture [8, 24], unlike the UNet architecture, which does not save the indices and relies on skip connections for spatial context. Rather than choosing one over the other, skip connections along with saving the indices for unpooling was chosen to provide maximum spatial information to the decoder, mitigating the need of pre-training the encoder to fine-tuning the decoder to focus on reconstructing this lost spatial context after performing max-unpooling. Without the use of skip connections, training the model end-to-end from random weight ini-tializations leads to a plateau in performance for the first 100 epochs, after which a big jump in performance is observed. This is because the encoder has to first learn a strong representation of the data before the decoder can leverage this representa-tion for spatial reconstruction and predicted segmentation. With the addition of skip connections, no such plateau and jump is observed. Moreover, the convergence time of the model is reduced by 32%. The skip connection in bottleneck4.1 concatenates the output of stage 1 while in bottleneck5.1 it concatenates the output of the initial block. Integrating the attention gate mechanism [18] into the encoder-decoder skip connections to enhance spatial attention does not improve the model’s performance any further and only adds more parameters and computation to the model. Hence, CBAM provides enough channel and spatial attention, negating the need to integrate any additional attention mechanisms into the architecture.

Binary cross-entropy, or log loss, is used because of its ability to provide flexibility in output probability predictions. The dice loss trains the model from an all positive 10 prediction and to removing the pixels mislabeled as the positive class. This forces the model to output only extreme probability values, i.e. a high probability for tumors and a low probability for background. This makes the model perform better from a pure probability point of view, but the model will only assign a high probability to tumor pixels if it is strongly confident in its decision. Binary Cross Entropy, on the other hand, trains the model with flexibility as it allows the model to assign probability values from 0 to 1 for both the positive and background classes and only penalizes high probability misclassifications. This allows for probability output values according to the confidence level of the model. Due to such flexibility, the model could predict a tumor pixel with probability of 0.6 and when a threshold value is applied, classifying probabilities above the threshold as positive class and below as background class, provides an additional increase in the metric scores up to 6.5%. This increase is not observed when the model is trained using dice loss because of the stricter prediction probabilities, leading to worse performance. Unlike in the original ENet training process of pre-training the encoder before fine-tuning the decoder [21], transfer learning was not performed, and the model was trained from randomly initialized weights. Adam optimizer was selected with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 through experimentation and research literature. A learning rate scheduler was used, which reduces the learning rate by a factor of 0.9 to 1 × 10 −6

if the best validation DSC fails to improve in ten epochs. Moreover, the encoder-decoder skip connections in the architecture improve the gradient flow and feature reuse, further enhancing the performance. 

# 3.4 Data Preparation and Augmentation 

Before any preprocessing takes place, it is ensured that all CT scans followed the left, anterior, superior (L, A, S) alignment. After this assertion, the pixel intensities of each image are clipped to be in the range of 3071 to -1024 and standardized by dividing by 3071. Training the model using a lung window of 800 to -1024 degraded the segmentation performance. As the model should focus on lung tumors, the first 30 slices of the axial axis (which displays the images from the lower abdomen to the neck) consisting of parts of the lower abdomen are skipped for each volume, thereby reducing the complexity of the features to learn. Due to the low number of slices that contain a tumor, it is important to choose the right data augmentation techniques that are known to work well in conjunction with CT scans of lung tumors. As the images are monochromatic, it is not possible to apply color space transformations like it would be with RGB images, which limits the options to geometric distortion, noise addition and contrast modification. Figure 4 illustrates a sample of images after passing through the augmentation pipeline. The order of the transformations matters to avoid introducing unnecessary artifacts [29]. Hence, the final pipeline for data augmentation is as follows: horizontal flipping, vertical flipping, linear contrast, additive gaussian noise, gaussian blur, affine transformations, and elastic deformation. Experimentation results have shown that introducing Gaussian noise and Gaussian blur transformations into the pipeline helped reduce overfitting, as basic geometric transformations proved to not challenge the Spatial Attention Module (SAM) enough, 11 Fig. 4 A sample of 9 augmented images of a CT slice after passing through the data augmentation pipeline. 

leading to overfitting [38]. Vertical flipping is generally avoided as it can distort the anatomical orientation and introduce unrealistic scenarios, which can hinder model performance. However, because CT scans were reoriented to the (L, A, S) orientation, high angles of rotation and flipping would still result in a realistic valid image and introduce invariance to reflection [39], improving the model’s robustness. Preserving the natural orientation would be mandatory when working with a 3D CNN but the advantage of working with 2D slices allows for such representation.

Table 4 compares the best DSC with the leading architectures. UNETR and Swin-UNETR struggle to achieve a high DSC as the vision transformer and swin transformer easily overfit on the training data, which has fewer tumor slices available within the dataset. Data augmentation was also not enough to prevent overfitting as the self-attention mechanism would negate the effects of any geometric transformations. The proposed model beats the DSC of the leading architectures, with the exception of 3D-Res2UNet. This model was trained on a 70% training split, a 20% validation split, and a 10% test split of the LUNA16 dataset [23], benefiting from being trained on a larger subset of the LIDC-IDRI curated by slice-thickness criteria ( >2.5 mm are excluded, yielding 888 scans). Taking into consideration that LUNA16 is a much larger dataset than the MSD dataset, the proposed model is still able to achieve a 0.90812 DSC. Moreover, The model maintains DSC >0.90 across datasets such as MSD and LIDC-IDRI without retraining, suggesting strong out-of-distribution generalization. To better understand the model, the explainable AI technique of Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) [44] is used to enhance its inter-pretability and provide us with a visual understanding of its decision-making process. Grad-CAM enables us to visualize which areas of the CT scan captures the most atten-tion due to the integration of CBAM. Figure 6 gives us a deeper understanding of the output, where the model focuses more on the main area of the tumor but struggles to capture any thin outgrowths that branch away from the main tumor. This behavior is evident when a high threshold value, such as 0.8, is used, causing more false nega-tives in the model predictions and decreasing the recall. These observations highlight 15 the importance of refining the model’s sensitivity to subtle tumor structures in future work either through architecture refinements or through model training.

# 4.3 Discussions 

Despite achieving a competitive Dice score value of 0.90812, beating architectures such as SegNet and UNet, recent advances such as transformer blocks, self-attention mech-anisms, and the integration of other multimodal imaging in conjunction with CT scans have demonstrated significant improvements with DSC as high as 0.97 [45]. Moreover, the Grad-CAM visualization of the model’s decisions, as shown in Figure 6, reveals that the primary limitation of our model is that it struggles to capture any thin branches of tumor growth away from the main tumor volume with high levels of confidence. Training on a larger dataset, such as LUNA16, is expected to improve segmentation performance and lead to a higher DSC. Additionally, the use of 2D slices results in the loss of 3D context, which is argued to cause a loss of correlation between adja-cent slices and reduce performance [4]. Integrating 2.5D training by including adjacent slices above and below the target slice would include the benefits of 3D information and provide better edge detailing while retaining the benefits of low computational costs, potentially improving performance, especially for thin tumor branches [46]. Fur-thermore, the deliberate design choice of a compact architecture with strict GMAC constraints enables deployment on resource-constrained CAD systems without a hard-ware accelerator but imposes an upper bound on representational capacity compared to much larger transformer-based models. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present an energy-efficient deep learning model for lung tumor seg-mentation by modifying the ENet architecture through the integration of lightweight CBAM attention modules and UNet-style encoder-decoder skip connections. These enhancements strengthen feature representation and attention capabilities with min-imal computational overhead. The results obtained by testing on the LIDC-IDRI dataset show that the proposed model achieves a DSC of 0.90812, precision of 0.917452, 17 recall of 0.898976, and IoU of 0.831703 with a threshold of 0.5, outperforming archi-tectures such as UNet and SegNet, while being up to 10 × faster, requiring 79 × fewer GMAC and having 22 × less parameters. The explainable AI technique of Grad-CAM visualization highlights the primary limitation as its inability to identify thin branches of tumor growth away from the main tumor volume with a high level of confidence.
