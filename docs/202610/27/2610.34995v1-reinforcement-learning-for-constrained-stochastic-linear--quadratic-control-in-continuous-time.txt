# Reinforcement learning for constrained stochastic linear–quadratic control in continuous time 

Weiping Wu a, Qitong Pan a, Xun Li ∗b, Peng Wu ∗a

> aSchool of Economics and Management, Fuzhou University, Fuzhou, 350108, Fujian, China
> bDepartment of Applied Mathematics, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong, China

Abstract 

We study continuous-time stochastic linear–quadratic control with a scalar state and nonnegative control constraints. With quadratic running costs, randomized exploration can change the optimal mean feedback. We intro-duce an auxiliary problem without entropy in which the conditional policy mean is nonnegative and the covariance is prescribed in proportion to the squared state. Moment reduction and state separation yield two regional Riccati equations and characterize the finite-horizon value function and op-timal conditional mean. We give a sufficient control–cost compatibility con-dition under which this mean coincides with the optimal feedback of the original constrained problem. For time-invariant coefficients, we character-ize the undiscounted infinite-horizon optimum and establish mean-square asymptotic stability under the stated assumptions. For unknown dynamics, we develop model-free actor–critic schemes for finite and infinite horizons. Numerical experiments compare the learned policies with their analytical targets and examine the effect of exploration on the optimal mean. 

Keywords: Linear quadratic regulators, Optimal control, Stochastic control, Continuous time systems, Constraints, Reinforcement learning 

1. Introduction 

For classical unconstrained stochastic linear–quadratic (LQ) problems, Riccati equations provide a means of constructing optimal controls in linear state-feedback form; see Yong and Zhou (1999, Chapter 6). Its connec-tion with mean–variance portfolio selection was established by Zhou and Li arXiv:2610.34995v1 [math.OC] 26 Oct 2026 (2000), using the indefinite stochastic LQ theory of Chen et al. (1998). Con-trol constraints introduce difficulties even when the model coefficients are known. In the no-shorting problem, Li et al. (2002) showed that the usual completion-of-squares argument does not apply directly and that the asso-ciated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation need not have a smooth solution. They characterized the solution through two Riccati equations and viscosity analysis. Hu and Zhou (2005) developed cone-constrained stochastic LQ control with random coefficients through two extended stochastic Riccati equations, while Li and Xu (2016) studied wealth and portfolio constraints by a martingale approach. For scalar-state systems with linear control con-straints, Wu et al. (2020) established state separation and derived regional Riccati equations, including an infinite-horizon characterization under addi-tional conditions. These model-based approaches rely on specified system dynamics (Wu et al., 2020; Wang and Zhou, 2020). Their use in mean–variance portfolio selection faces two distinct difficulties. First, financial model specification must balance applicability and tractability, and models that retain more realistic market features can become difficult to solve with classical stochas-tic control tools (Hambly et al., 2023). Second, implementing model-based portfolio policies requires coefficient estimates. Accurate estimation is diffi-cult, and portfolio allocations are sensitive to estimation errors (Wang and Zhou, 2020, Section 2.2). Model-free reinforcement learning (RL) can bypass explicit coefficient estimation and learn controls from observed interactions (Hambly et al., 2023; Wang and Zhou, 2020). However, the squared mean in a variance objective makes it nonlinear in expectation. The usual learning rules for expected cumulative rewards therefore cannot be applied directly to mean–variance criteria (Wang and Zhou, 2020, Introduction). Exploratory stochastic control treats exploration explicitly in continuous time by replacing actions with probability distributions (Wang et al., 2020). The LQ structure provides analytical optimal exploratory policies in the set-tings of Wang et al. (2020); Wang and Zhou (2020). Auxiliary formulations without entropy have also recovered original optimal controls through pol-icy means for specific investment criteria (Dai et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). We study this auxiliary approach for scalar-state stochastic LQ control with nonnegative control constraints and quadratic running costs. We character-ize its finite-horizon value and optimal conditional mean. Quadratic running costs can make exploration change the optimal mean, so its relationship with the original constrained feedback requires a separate analysis (Theorems 1 2and 2). We also study the corresponding undiscounted infinite-horizon prob-lem and construct learning methods for unknown dynamics (Sections 3.6 and 4). The main contributions of this paper are as follows. 1. Auxiliary formulation and finite-horizon solution. We formu-late an auxiliary problem without entropy for scalar-state stochastic LQ control with nonnegative controls, using general randomized feed-back policies with nonnegative means and prescribed state-dependent covariance. Moment reduction and state separation characterize its finite-horizon value function and optimal conditional mean (Section 2.3 and Theorem 1). 2. Consistency with the original constrained problem. We give a sufficient control–cost compatibility condition under which the optimal conditional mean of the auxiliary problem coincides with the original optimal constrained feedback (Theorem 2). 3. Undiscounted infinite-horizon control. We characterize the infinite-horizon optimal value and conditional mean, and establish mean-square asymptotic stability under the stated assumptions (The-orem 3). 4. Learning with unknown dynamics. Using the martingale meth-ods of Jia and Zhou (2022a,b), we develop finite- and infinite-horizon model-free actor–critic schemes. Numerical experiments compare the learned policies with their analytical targets (Sections 4 and 5). The paper is organized as follows. Section 1.1 reviews the related lit-erature. Section 2 defines the original and auxiliary problems. Section 3 establishes the exploratory-control results. Section 4 presents the learning methods, and Section 5 reports the numerical studies. Section 6 concludes the paper. The appendices provide the proofs and learning derivations. 

1.1. Related literature 

Mean–variance (MV) portfolio selection minimizes the variance of termi-nal wealth for a prescribed expected terminal wealth (Wang and Zhou, 2020). Zhou and Li (2000) embedded the continuous-time MV portfolio problem into a class of auxiliary stochastic LQ problems. In this formulation, wealth is the scalar state and portfolio positions are the controls (Wang and Zhou, 2020, Section 2.1). Hambly et al. (2023) reviewed applications of RL to portfolio 3optimization and hedging. Continuous-time portfolio learning without ex-plicit coefficient estimation has been developed by Wang and Zhou (2020); Huang et al. (2024). Variance-related objectives and constraints have also been studied for cumulative rewards (Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh, 2016, Section 3). Tamar et al. (2016) developed methods for evaluating variance under a fixed policy. Tamar et al. (2012); Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016) studied variance-related objectives and constraints using local policy optimization. These evaluation and local optimization results do not provide a general guarantee of a globally optimal mean–variance policy. To model the exploration–exploitation trade-off explicitly in continuous time, Wang et al. (2020) introduced an exploratory stochastic control for-mulation. In this formulation, the drift, diffusion variance, and instanta-neous reward are averaged with respect to action distributions. Entropy regularization balances exploration and exploitation. In the unconstrained LQ setting of Wang et al. (2020), the optimal policy is Gaussian. Its mean represents exploitation, and its variance represents exploration. The policy and value function converge to their classical counterparts as the exploration weight vanishes. For finite-horizon mean–variance portfolio selection, Wang and Zhou (2020) used the LQ structure to derive a globally optimal ex-ploratory policy. They also proved a policy improvement theorem. Under exact policy evaluation and their stated Gaussian initialization, policy itera-tion converges to that optimum. These results give a global characterization for this specific LQ problem beyond the local optimization results discussed above. Martingale methods subsequently provided continuous-time policy evaluation, policy-gradient representations, and q-learning (Jia and Zhou, 2022a,b, 2023). Related analyses address exploratory HJB equations (Tang et al., 2022), entropy annealing (Sethi et al., 2025), LQ policy convergence (Giegrich et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2026), and the accuracy of discretely sam-pled stochastic policies (Jia et al., 2026). Control constraints also complicate exploration. Constraining the sup-port of randomized policies can exclude ordinary Gaussian policies and make theoretical analysis more difficult (Li et al., 2026). The interval constraints studied by Chau et al. (2026) lead to truncated Gaussian policies with ad-ditional normalization and boundary terms. An alternative is to formulate an auxiliary problem without entropy. Dai et al. (2026) proved that the mean of an optimal randomized policy solves their original unconstrained Merton problem. For no-shorting mean–variance portfolio selection, Li et al. (2026) imposed the constraint on the policy mean and proved that, with a 4suitable prescribed covariance, the optimal mean recovers the original op-timal portfolio. These investment results do not establish the mean-policy relationship with quadratic running costs or characterize the undiscounted infinite-horizon auxiliary problem with prescribed state-dependent covari-ance for constrained stochastic LQ control.

3. Main Results 

We first reduce the auxiliary problem to conditional-mean optimization and characterize its finite-horizon value by state separation. We then iden-tify a condition for equality between the exploratory optimal mean and the original optimal feedback. Finally, for constant coefficients, we derive the infinite-horizon optimal feedback with constant regional gains. These results provide the targets for the learning methods in Section 4.

Conclusion 

This paper studied continuous-time stochastic LQ control with a scalar state and nonnegative control constraints. We introduced an auxiliary prob-lem without entropy, using randomized policies with nonnegative conditional means and prescribed state-dependent covariance. Moment reduction and state separation yielded two regional Riccati equations and characterized the finite-horizon value function and optimal conditional mean (Theorem 1). Quadratic running costs can make exploration change the optimal mean. We gave a sufficient control–cost compatibility condition under which that mean coincides with the original optimal constrained feedback (Theorem 2). For constant coefficients, we characterized the undiscounted infinite-horizon op-timum and established mean-square asymptotic stability under the stated assumptions (Theorem 3). For unknown dynamics, we developed finite- and infinite-horizon actor– critic schemes using the martingale methods of Jia and Zhou (2022a,b). In the reported running-cost comparison, including the running cost reduced both critic and policy errors. The compatible and incompatible cases illus-trated, respectively, agreement of the two optimal mean feedbacks and a distinct exploratory target. The learned gains were close to their analyti-cal targets in the reported finite- and infinite-horizon experiments (Tables 1 and 2). 39 Appendix A. Proofs for the exploratory-control results
