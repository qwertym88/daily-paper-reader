# Learning Direct Control from Optimization for Dynamic Target Tracking 

Bing Sun, Yue Lin, Canhui Wen, Xin Chen, Yang Liu, Dong Wang*, Huchuan Lu 

Abstract — Dynamic target tracking requires a mobile robot to maintain target visibility while navigating obstacles with smooth, feasible controls. Online trajectory optimization explic-itly encodes these objectives but requires repeated numerical solving, whereas waypoint-based neural policies separate geo-metric prediction from control execution. We introduce LECO, a framework that transfers tracking optimization into offline learning of a direct-control policy. LECO predicts control se-quences and maps them to trajectories through a differentiable kinematic model, allowing trajectory-level objectives to directly guide control learning. A decaying expert-guidance curriculum initializes effective tracking behavior and progressively shifts learning toward these task objectives. At deployment, a predic-tive safety lock checks short-horizon obstacle clearance and invokes a classical planning and control fallback when the predicted motion is unsafe or the target prediction is invalid. Across three simulated obstacle densities, LECO reduces root-mean-square linear jerk by 14.1–31.2% relative to the best competing method for this metric and achieves the highest tracking success rate, with per-cycle computation times of 4.25–5.63 ms. Experiments on two robot platforms further demonstrate tracking through indoor narrow passages and outdoor forest environments. These results demonstrate that learning direct control from optimization enables smooth and robust dynamic target tracking. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dynamic target tracking requires a mobile robot to keep a moving target in view while navigating the surrounding environment [1], [2]. In cluttered scenes, these requirements are tightly coupled: a collision-free maneuver may place an obstacle between the robot and the target, while maintaining visibility may require turning through narrow passages. The robot must therefore coordinate target distance, line of sight (LoS), obstacle clearance, and control smoothness under its own kinematic constraints. The central challenge is to translate these spatial tracking objectives into feasible control actions as both the target and the local environment change. Trajectory optimization provides an explicit way to ex-press these objectives. Optimization-based trackers account for target motion, visibility, and obstacle avoidance when generating robot trajectories [3]–[5], while model predictive control (MPC) incorporates motion constraints into control computation [6]. This approach requires numerical solving as observations change. Learning-based planners offer acomplementary route by transferring trajectory costs into network training [7], [8]. For example, YOPOv2-Tracker learns motion-primitive offsets from trajectory costs and con-verts the resulting predictions into quadrotor commands [7]. Kinematics-aware learning also incorporates motion models into planning [9], [10]. These developments motivate learn-ing tracking behavior directly in the control space of a mobile （a） （b）

Our key idea is to retain the explicit objectives of tracking optimization while moving their optimization into offline learning of a direct-control policy. A differentiable kinematic model provides the connection: it maps a predicted control sequence to a robot trajectory and propagates trajectory-level costs back to the policy. Target visibility and obstacle clear-ance can then guide control learning together with control smoothness, using the platform’s motion model throughout. This formulation connects what the robot should achieve in the environment with the controls it will execute. We instantiate this idea in LECO , a framework for learning direct control from optimization for dynamic target tracking (Fig. 2). Given local geometry, robot motion, and target-relative observations, LECO predicts a future control sequence and rolls it out through a platform-specific differen-tiable kinematic model. Tracking objectives evaluated along this rollout jointly encourage the desired target distance, clear lines of sight, obstacle clearance, and smooth motion. A decaying expert-guidance curriculum establishes effective tracking behavior early in training, then progressively shifts learning toward these task objectives. The expert thus pro-vides an initialization for control learning, while the final policy is refined by the quality of the motion it predicts. At deployment, LECO executes the first predicted con-trol in a receding-horizon manner, avoiding iterative task optimization in the neural branch. A. Classical Optimization-Based Target Tracking 

Traditional planning methods include MPC, MPPI, ESDF-based nonlinear optimization and so on [12]–[15]. They typ-ically encode line-of-sight maintenance and obstacle clear-ance as cost terms for dynamic obstacle avoidance and target tracking. However, in nonconvex scenarios involving narrow passages and tight corners, online solvers such as SQP and L-BFGS are prone to local minima and zero-velocity deadlocks [16]. The computational burden depends strongly on the solver, optimization horizon, and environment com-plexity. Online optimization is still required at every control cycle and becomes more expensive as problem complexity grows. Meanwhile, modeling and state-prediction errors can accumulate over long-horizon rollouts. 

B. Learning-Based Motion Planning and Control 

To avoid repeated online optimization, DRL and be-havioral cloning have been widely used in robotic mo-tion planning [17]–[19]. Recent cost-guided methods further train policies with trajectory-level objectives. For example, YOPOv2-Tracker [7] back-propagates trajectory costs for target tracking, but still predicts intermediate motion rep-resentations before execution. Such waypoint- or trajectory-based architectures separate geometric prediction from plat-form control [7], [20], [21]. This separation can introduce a representation mismatch between training objectives and executable controls [22]–[24]. LECO follows the cost-guided learning paradigm, but directly predicts control sequences and introduces explicit target-tracking and LoS objectives. C. Optimization Distillation and Safe Deployment 

Optimization-to-Policy transfers the knowledge encoded in traditional optimizers, such as MPC, into neural poli-cies [9], [25]. It provides a promising way to combine phys-ical constraints with efficient inference. However, existing optimization-distillation methods typically mainly focus on generic motion planning or point-to-point navigation [20]. Direct cost optimization can also converge to near-zero controls during early training. Moreover, black-box policies lack deterministic safety guarantees in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios [26]–[29]. Control barrier function (CBF)-based safety filters add an online constrained safety-filtering step [30]. In contrast, fallback strategies, such as emergency stoppage or global replanning, can disrupt the continuity of dynamic tracking. III. M ETHODOLOGY

The target moves through a narrow multi-turn passage along an S-shaped or figure-eight route. This continuously changes the relative distance and bearing while introducing sharp turns, constrained free space, and short-term occlusions. The experiment mainly evaluates control smoothness, jerk, and target observability under complex geometric constraints. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the robot completes long-range continu-ous tracking through the narrow passage while maintaining stable obstacle avoidance. The target also remains within the effective field of view for most of the tracking process, demonstrating stable tracking under consecutive turns and intermittent occlusions. 

Temporary obstacle disturbance. This experiment eval-uates the response of the predictive safety lock to sudden environmental changes. As shown in Fig. 8(b), a temporary obstacle is introduced during tracking and triggers the clas-sical fallback when the predicted neural motion becomes unsafe. The fallback controller guides the robot around the obstacle while maintaining the tracking task. After the obstacle is bypassed and the safety condition is restored, control returns to the neural policy, allowing continuous tracking without prolonged interruption. 

Long-range tracking in a complex forest. The outdoor experiment evaluates long-term operation in an unstructured environment. It focuses on continuous target tracking, natural obstacle avoidance, and target reacquisition after short-term loss. As shown in Fig. 9, the robot maintains stable tracking in a forest with irregular obstacle layouts and continuously changing free space. It remains responsive to local geometric changes while balancing obstacle avoidance and target reten-tion. After temporary occlusion or target loss, the robot can recover the target and resume tracking, demonstrating stable long-horizon operation on the Ackermann platform. VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK This paper presented LECO, a unified framework for safe and efficient dynamic target tracking. It combines optimization-to-policy learning, kinematic-integrated control, and predictive safety lock. Together, these components im-prove control smoothness, tracking robustness, and runtime safety. Simulation results show improved tracking success, target visibility, and control smoothness with competitive millisecond-level computation, while real-world experiments demonstrate stable operation in indoor and outdoor environ-ments. These advantages are maintained across dense obsta-cles, narrow passages, sudden disturbances, and unstructured environments overall. The current framework mainly considers planar kinematics and 2-D ESDF constraints. Future work will extend LECO to richer platform dynamics, 3-D environments, and more complex dynamic interactions.
