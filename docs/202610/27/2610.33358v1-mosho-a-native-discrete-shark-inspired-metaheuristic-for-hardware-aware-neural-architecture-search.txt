Abstract 

Hardware-aware neural architecture search (NAS) requires jointly optimizing predictive ac-curacy and device-specific latency over discrete search spaces. While multi-objective swarm optimizers show promise, continuous formulations such as the Multi-Objective White Shark Optimizer (MOWSO) can suffer from quantization noise when rounded to integer-encoded architecture vectors. To address this limitation, we propose MOSHO (Multi-Objective Shark Hunting Optimization), a native discrete metaheuristic that replaces continuous motion equations with discrete architecture-editing operators. MOSHO integrates adaptive operator scheduling, energy-regulated restarts, and deferred crowding-distance truncation to enhance exploration and search efficiency. On the merged NAS-Bench-201/HW-NAS-Bench bench-mark across 18 contexts, MOSHO achieves a median normalized hypervolume of 0.9987 and IGD + of 0.0064, yielding Pareto fronts competitive with NSGA-II while reducing search run-time by 96.4% (0.64 s vs. 17.72 s). These results demonstrate that tailored discrete search mechanisms can enable swarm optimizers to achieve high-quality multi-objective solution quality with substantially lower search cost in hardware-aware NAS. 

Keywords: Neural architecture search, Hardware-aware optimization, Multi-objective optimization, Discrete optimization, Combinatorial optimization, Swarm intelligence, White Shark Optimizer 

1. Introduction 

Deep learning has become a fundamental approach for image classification and visual recognition, with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) achieving strong performance across a wide range of applications (Elsken et al., 2019). The performance of a CNN depends not only on its learned parameters but also on architectural choices, including operation types, kernel sizes, channel widths, activation functions, and connectivity pat-terns. Manually designing these configurations is challenging because the number of possible combinations grows rapidly with the number of architectural decisions. Hyperparameter Op-timization (HPO) addresses this challenge by automatically searching for effective training arXiv:2610.33358v1 [cs.NE] 23 Oct 2026 and model hyperparameters, typically while keeping the network architecture fixed. Neural Architecture Search (NAS) extends this optimization paradigm by treating the architecture itself as a search variable (Elsken et al., 2019; Ren et al., 2021). NAS therefore searches an architecture space X for configurations offering desirable predictive performance. Early NAS approaches based on reinforcement learning and evolutionary algorithms demonstrated the potential of automated architecture design but often required substantial computational resources (Zoph and Le, 2017; Real et al., 2019). Subsequent weight-sharing approaches and tabular NAS benchmarks have made architecture search more practical and reproducible (Liu et al., 2019; Dong and Yang, 2020). For deployment on resource-constrained edge and Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices, how-ever, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient. Hardware-Aware Neural Architecture Search (HW-NAS) therefore incorporates hardware-related objectives such as inference latency, memory consumption, and energy usage into architecture optimization. In this work, we focus on the accuracy–latency trade-off and formulate HW-NAS as the multi-objective prob-lem

The objective is to identify a Pareto set of architectures providing different accuracy–latency trade-offs. Multi-objective NAS has been addressed using evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning, and swarm intelligence. Methods such as NSGA-II (Kumar, 2025) provide effective Pareto-based search, while swarm-based methods offer alternative exploration–exploitation mechanisms, with shark-inspired optimizers such as the White Shark Optimizer (WSO) and its multi-objective extension MOWSO providing promising search mechanisms (Braik et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2025).However, adapting continuous shark-inspired optimization mechanisms to discrete architecture spaces remains a challenge, motivating the development of optimization strategies that operate directly on discrete architectures. In this work, we introduce MOSHO (Multi-Objective Shark Hunting Optimization), a discrete shark-inspired metaheuristic for hardware-aware NAS. MOSHO operates directly on integer-encoded architectures using discrete architecture-editing operators. It further incorporates archive-guided search, adaptive operator scheduling, energy-regulated restarts, and deferred archive truncation to balance exploration and exploitation while limiting multi-objective search overhead. 2. Related Work 

Multi-objective NAS can be viewed through two complementary components: an eval-uation strategy, which assigns each candidate architecture its accuracy and hardware cost, and a search mechanism, the outer loop that proposes candidates and steers the population 2toward the accuracy–latency trade-off. Section 2.1 reviews the former; Section 2.2 reviews the latter. 

2.1. Architecture Evaluation Methods for Hardware-Aware NAS 

Early NAS approaches based on reinforcement learning (Zoph and Le, 2017) and evo-lutionary search (Real et al., 2019) demonstrated that architecture design could be au-tomated, but at a prohibitive computational cost, since every candidate architecture was trained from scratch. Three complementary directions have since emerged to make this evaluation tractable, trading off fidelity, generality, and cost differently. The first relies on weight-sharing supernetworks, in which a single over-parameterized network embeds the en-tire search space and candidate architectures are extracted as subgraphs sharing a common set of trained weights, avoiding the need to train each candidate independently (Pham et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019). Once-for-All (Cai et al., 2020) extends this idea to hardware-aware deployment by decoupling supernet training from architecture search entirely, so that a specialized sub-network can be extracted for a given device and latency budget without additional training. This family amortizes training cost across many deployment scenarios but still requires training a single, carefully regularized supernet, and provides no explicit multi-objective search procedure for the accuracy–latency trade-off once sub-networks are available. The second direction replaces training-based evaluation with a learned surrogate model that predicts an architecture’s accuracy and/or hardware cost directly from its encoding. NAS-Bench-301 (Siems et al., 2021) demonstrates that surrogate models can support efficient evaluation over the DARTS search space, which contains approximately 10 18 architectures, using only a fraction of the architectures for training. BANANAS (White et al., 2021) further couples a path-based neural predictor with Bayesian optimization to guide the search itself. Surrogate models trade the exactness of ground-truth evaluation for scalability to arbitrarily large architecture spaces. The third direction relies on exhaustive tabular benchmarks, where every architecture in a search space is evaluated once and stored in a lookup table, so optimizer runs query exact, pre-computed values instead of training or predicting them. NAS-Bench-201 (Dong and Yang, 2020) and its hardware-oriented counterpart HW-NAS-Bench (Li et al., 2021), used throughout this paper, sacrifice the scale reachable by supernets or surrogates but remove training stochasticity and approximation error entirely, isolating optimizer behavior as the sole source of variability, which is essential for the controlled comparison in Section 5. All three directions address how an architecture is evaluated, not how the accuracy– latency trade-off space is explored once that evaluation is available: an outer-loop optimizer is still required to search the resulting objective landscape and maintain a representative Pareto front under a finite budget. Multi-objective NAS employs a variety of search paradigms that can be broadly grouped into three categories: recombination-based evolutionary methods, swarm-intelligence meth-ods, and Learning- and trajectory-based methods. 

Recombination-based evolutionary methods encode architectures as discrete rep-resentations and evolve populations through selection and variation. Classical approaches such as NSGA-II(Kumar, 2025) use non-dominated sorting to maintain Pareto-optimal so-lutions, while decomposition-based methods optimize scalarized subproblems (Zhang and Li, 2007). Subsequent NAS variants have incorporated Bayesian-network exploitation (Lu et al., 2019), co-evolutionary search with uniform-sampling initialization (Xue et al., 2026), differential evolution combined with Hyperband (Awad et al., 2021), and alternative archi-tecture encodings based on Cartesian genetic programming or trees (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2023; Michel et al., 2019). Although these approaches provide effective population-based ex-ploration, they often rely on relatively large populations and repeated objective evaluations, while adaptation to search history is generally limited. 

Learning- and trajectory-based methods include reinforcement learning, Monte Carlo tree search, trajectory-based local search, and simulated-annealing-based approaches. Reinforcement-learning methods generate architectures as sequences of actions via policy gradients (Zoph and Le, 2017), while Monte Carlo tree search explores architecture spaces through simulated rollouts (Lallouet et al., 2025). Trajectory-based methods can instead 4accept temporarily inferior candidates according to a decaying probability and may combine this mechanism with learned performance predictors (Liu et al., 2021). These approaches pro-vide alternative search mechanisms but can require substantial numbers of candidate evalua-tions, making their computational cost challenging under limited search budgets. Surrogate-assisted approaches, which combine learned performance models with an outer-loop search procedure, represent another class of methods and are discussed in Section 2.1. 

Swarm-intelligence methods provide an alternative population-based paradigm based on cooperative or collective search behaviors. Multi-objective particle swarm optimization (Coello Coello et al., 2004), variable-length PSO (Wang et al., 2018), artificial bee colony search (Shahawy and Benkhelifa, 2023), firefly optimization (Nguyen et al., 2025), monarch butterfly optimization (Bacanin et al., 2020), and Harris Hawks optimization (Basha et al., 2021) have been applied to NAS and related discrete optimization problems. However, most of these methods were originally formulated for continuous search spaces. Applying them to discrete architecture spaces therefore requires discretization or mapping mechanisms, which can introduce quantization effects and become less effective as architecture representations grow in complexity. Among swarm-intelligence approaches, shark-inspired methods are par-ticularly relevant to this work: • Shark-inspired methods. These constitute the direct lineage of MOSHO. The White Shark Optimizer (WSO) (Braik et al., 2022), its multi-objective extension MOWSO (Zheng et al., 2025), and Shark Smell Optimization (SSO) (Kamarzaman et al., 2021) employ predator–prey and smell-guided mechanisms to balance exploration and ex-ploitation. Detailed mathematical formulations of these methods are provided in Ap-pendix A of the Supplementary Materials. MOWSO extends WSO with a Pareto archive and True Distance-based leader selection but retains a continuous search for-mulation. Consequently, applying such methods to integer-encoded NAS architectures requires post-hoc rounding, which can introduce quantization effects (Zheng et al., 2025). Moreover, maintaining an external Pareto archive with diversity-preserving truncation can introduce additional computational overhead as the population and archive grow. These limitations motivate the native discrete formulation and archive-management mechanisms introduced in MOSHO. 

3. Problem Formulation 

We address the joint optimization of the predictive accuracy and the hardware-specific inference latency of CNN architectures, a bi-objective discrete combinatorial problem whose solution is not a single architecture but a Pareto front of trade-offs (Hsu et al., 2018). 

Search space. We adopt the cell-based space of NAS-Bench-201 (Dong and Yang, 2020), also covered by HW-NAS-Bench (Li et al., 2021), in which an architecture is en-tirely determined by a single cell stacked inside a fixed macro skeleton. Problem characteristics. The space is categorical: operation indices carry no order, so velocity–position arithmetic has no architectural meaning, and the natural neighborhood is the Hamming distance between encodings (Awad et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2025). The objectives are black-box (Dong and Yang, 2020), their trade-offs vary across devices (Li et al., 2021), and the landscape is multi-modal (Elsken et al., 2019), with plateaus created by functionally equivalent encodings (e.g., when zeroize disconnects an edge). These properties call for a natively discrete, archive-based metaheuristic. 

4. Proposed Method: MOSHO 

The shark-inspired optimizers from which MOSHO descends were not designed for the problem of Section 3. WSO (Braik et al., 2022) and SSO (Kamarzaman et al., 2021) are single-objective, and all three ancestors, including the multi-objective MOWSO (Zheng et al., 2025), move agents through continuous velocity–position or gradient-following equations. On the categorical encoding of X , these iterates must be rounded to integers: since oper-ation indices have no order, a rounded step maps onto an arbitrary operation, and many continuous positions collapse onto the same architecture, so that the search trajectory is distorted by quantization noise. Moreover, the archive of MOWSO plays a passive role, supplying only the leader of the velocity update, while its control parameters follow fixed exponential schedules that ignore whether the search is progressing or stagnating. Finally, 6MOWSO prunes its archive with the True Distance metric after every insertion, a cost of 

O(N · A3) per iteration that dominates the total runtime when objective evaluations are cheap lookups. MOSHO addresses these limitations through three contributions: (i) six native discrete operator kernels that edit integer architecture vectors directly, without relaxation or rounding (Section 4.2); (ii) an integrated adaptive search and archive-management strategy combining energy-regulated restarts, a dual-signal operator sched-uler, and deferred crowding-distance truncation to balance exploration and exploitation while reducing archive-maintenance overhead (Sections 4.3, 4.5, and 4.4); and (iii) a compre-hensive empirical evaluation (Section 5) across 18 dataset–device contexts using the merged NAS-Bench-201/HW-NAS-Bench benchmark, assessing Pareto-front quality, search efficiency, and scalability against established baselines. The results show that MOSHO re-duces search runtime by 96.4% compared with NSGA-II while producing competitive Pareto fronts. A detailed structural comparison between MOSHO and its ancestors is provided in Appendix B of the Supplementary Materials. 

4.1. Architecture of MOSHO 

MOSHO evolves a population Pt of N sharks, each characterized by an integer architec-ture vector xti ∈ X and an energy level eti, together with an external Pareto archive A of bounded capacity M that stores the best trade-offs found so far and constitutes the returned approximation set ˆP . Around this population and archive, the method is organized into four interacting modules: an operator-kernel engine , which generates candidate architec-tures with six discrete operators sampled from a probability vector πt; an energy manager ,which tracks the success of each shark and restarts depleted ones; a deferred truncation 

module, which bounds the archive while preserving its diversity; and a dual-signal adaptive scheduler , which updates πt from archive dynamics and search progress. As summarized in Figure 3, the archive is not a passive store but the hub of the search: it supplies genes to the operators, seeds to the restarts, and an improvement signal to the scheduler.

4.4. Archive Management and Selection 

The archive is maintained under a dominance-first policy. Initially, A0 is built from the non-dominated subset of the uniformly sampled initial population. Afterwards, a candidate is inserted whenever no archive member dominates it, and all archive members it dominates are removed at insertion time, whereas truncation to the capacity M is deferred to the end of the iteration. This decoupling of insertion from truncation is where MOSHO obtains most of its runtime advantage. MOWSO recomputes TD-based pruning after each candidate insertion, which costs O(A2) pairwise distances per candidate and O(N · A3) per iteration once the iterative removals are accounted for, where A = |A| . MOSHO instead performs two distinct steps: 1. Per candidate, inside the agent loop: a dominance check only, O(A). Non-dominated candidates are inserted without any sorting, and dominated members are dropped on the spot. 2. Per iteration, at the end of the loop: if |A| > M , a single crowding-distance-based sorting, O(A log A), ranks archive members by local density and prunes the most crowded ones until |A| = M . The extreme solutions of each objective are excluded from this pruning, so the boundaries of the front are never lost. The per-iteration cost therefore drops from O(N · A3) to O(N · A + A log A), roughly a factor A2, and this single policy is the main source of the runtime gap reported in Section 5. Crowding distance is also the more suitable diversity metric here, since it measures per-axis spacing and preserves extreme solutions by construction, which TD does not. Leader selection follows the same principle: when Scent or Burst requires an archive member, the draw is biased toward large crowding distances, so movement is guided toward the sparse regions of the front rather than its crowded center.

5. Comprehensive Experimental Evaluation 

This section presents an empirical evaluation of the proposed MOSHO algorithm across 18 dataset–device contexts derived from the merged NAS-Bench-201 (Dong and Yang, 2020) and HW-NAS-Bench (Li et al., 2021) lookup tables. The evaluation assesses the accuracy– latency trade-offs achieved by the search mechanisms and analyzes the impact of the proposed discrete algorithmic components. All experiments rely on deterministic tabular evaluation and are executed on a single Win-dows 11 Pro workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H processor, 32 GB DDR4 RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU with 6 GB VRAM. The evaluation benchmarks cover six distinct edge hardware devices (EdgeGPU, EdgeTPU, Eyeriss, FPGA, Pixel 3, and Raspberry Pi 4) across three representative vision datasets: CIFAR-10 (validation split), CIFAR-100, and ImageNet16-120. The evaluation protocol considers six evaluation budgets 

B ∈ { 1000 , 2000 , 5000 , 7000 , 10000 , 15000 }, with 30 independent runs per method per context 11 using deterministic random seeds defined by sn = 42 + ( n − 1) × 7, for n = 1 , . . . , 30 . Statis-tical significance is assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Wilcoxon, 1945) with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, complemented by Vargha–Delaney A12 effect sizes (Vargha and Delaney, 2000). To ensure full reproducibility, the ORAMA-NAS framework which implements the pro-posed MOSHO algorithm, all baseline methods, and the complete experimental bench-marking pipeline is made publicly available at https://github.com/Orama-team/orama-nas-framework. The remainder of this section is organized as follows: Section 5.1 defines the evaluation metrics; the baseline methods and hyperparameter configuration are then presented; Sec-tion 5.4 examines the contribution of each algorithmic component; Sections 5.5 and 5.6 assess MOSHO’s performance and compare it against baseline methods; Section 5.7 investigates sensitivity to population size and evaluation budget; and Section 5.8 examines scalability across an expanded search space and a three-objective formulation.

5.4. Ablation Analysis 

To isolate the contribution of individual components, we evaluate all single-unit removals and the grouped variants listed in Table 3. MOSHO denotes the final adopted configuration, while MOSHO-Full denotes the complete configuration including all development compo-nents. Three of the tested units (U02, U08, and U9) are auxiliary mechanisms evaluated during development but excluded from the final MOSHO configuration. U02 (explicit acceptance policy) replaces unconditional acceptance with a structured three-case rule: a candidate is accepted outright if it Pareto-dominates the agent’s current solution, accepted if it is non-dominated but improves archive coverage, and otherwise accepted only with a probability that decays as the search progresses, favoring free exploration early and selective conver-gence later; rejected candidates incur an energy penalty on the owning agent. U08 (K-means archive leader/seed selection) was a development-stage alternative to the final archive sampling rule. It partitions the archive in objective space using K-means and samples clus-ters inversely proportional to their size, thereby favoring sparse regions. This mechanism was excluded from the final MOSHO configuration after showing no consistent benefit. U9 (mono-objective fallback) monitors the spread of each objective across the archive and, if one objective’s range collapses below a small threshold (a degeneracy that can occur on hard-ware where many architectures share identical latency), temporarily suspends Pareto-based 13 comparison in favor of scalar comparison on the remaining objective, automatically reverting once the range recovers.

6. Conclusions and Future Work 

This paper presented MOSHO, a native discrete multi-objective metaheuristic for hardware-aware neural architecture search. MOSHO addresses the limitations of continuous shark-inspired optimization in discrete architecture spaces by replacing velocity–position dynamics and post-hoc discretization with native discrete architecture-editing operators. The result-ing framework combines discrete search operators, energy-regulated restarts, deferred archive truncation, and dual-signal adaptive scheduling to balance exploration and exploitation ef-ficiently. Experimental evaluation on the merged NAS-Bench-201/HW-NAS-Bench benchmark across 18 dataset–device contexts demonstrates that MOSHO achieves highly competitive Pareto-front approximations with substantially lower runtime. At 15,000 evaluations, MOSHO achieves a median nHV of 0.9987 and IGD + of 0.0064, with a median runtime of 0.64 s, corresponding to a 96.4% reduction compared with NSGA-II (17.72 s). Although NSGA-II achieves slightly stronger median Pareto-quality metrics, MOSHO provides a favorable trade-off between solution quality and computational cost. Scalability experiments further show that MOSHO remains effective in an expanded 510 search space and when extending the problem from two to three objectives. Despite these encouraging results, the current evaluation has several limitations. The ex-periments rely primarily on lookup-table benchmarks, which enable deterministic and repro-ducible comparisons but do not capture the full cost and variability of training architectures from scratch. Moreover, the evaluation is centered on NAS-Bench-201-derived search spaces and a fixed set of hardware targets. Further validation is therefore required on larger search spaces, training-based NAS, and real hardware platforms. Future work will focus on extending MOSHO to larger and more diverse NAS settings, in-cluding super-network-based and training-based search spaces. We also plan to evaluate the method on real embedded hardware and broader accelerator platforms, incorporating prac-tical constraints such as measurement variability and deployment cost. Finally, extending MOSHO to many-objective HW-NAS by incorporating objectives such as memory footprint, model size, and power consumption represents a promising direction.
