# Reactive Humanoid Multi-Contact Using Learned Stability Models 

Stephen McCrory 1,2, Beomyeong Park 1, Nicholas Kitchel 1, Nehar Poddar 1,2, Robert Griffin 1,2

Abstract — We present a planning and control approach to reactively use hand contacts to stabilize a humanoid in low stability scenarios, where only using feet contacts may result in a fall. Candidate contacts are sampled within the robot’s reachable workspace, and a preview is computed by rolling out the centroidal dynamics through pre-impact, impact and post-impact phases. Sampled points are scored based on the Center of Pressure (CoP) control authority at the post-impact phase. Central to our approach is a learned model of the robot’s CoP region during post-impact, which enables rapid evaluation of candidate contact points compared to traditional optimization-based methods. The presented planner has two stages: the first selects an optimal bracing region and the second computes an optimal bracing point within the region. Our simulation results demonstrate an average increase in impulse resilience of 89% over recovery without hand contacts and 17% over a naive planning strategy (closest reachable region). We validate our framework on hardware, performing push tests while standing and walking. The standing trials show an average 43% reduction in stabilization time compared to naive hand placement and the walking trials demonstrate a 18% reduction compared to baseline recovery (without hand contacts). 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

As humanoid robots become capable of performing tasks in real-world scenarios, reliability becomes increasingly im-portant. For this reason, push recovery has a rich history in legged robotics, with the broad goal of maximizing robustness [1–4]. However, in spite of numerous successful strategies for bipedal push recovery, existing approaches are largely restricted to quasi-flat terrain and feet contacts. Making full use of the humanoid form-factor means utiliz-ing upper body contacts to augment lower-body recovery techniques. Additionally, it requires models that go beyond quasi-flat terrain and handle arbitrary contact surfaces. In this work, we propose a sample-based hand contact planner that reactively braces in low stability scenarios where the use of only foot contacts may result in a fall, such as the scenario in Fig. 1. Sampled contact points are previewed by rolling out the robot’s centroidal dynamics through pre-impact, impact and post-impact phases. The pre-impact and impact phases can be rolled out using conventional closed-form models such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) [1]. In contrast, the post-impact contact state cannot be modeled as a basic LIPM, but instead using optimization-based models that are compatible with arbitrary contact sets [5, 6]. Each roll-out is scored based on the Center of Pressure (CoP) control authority at the post-impact phase.

A central part of our approach is a set of neural net-works that predict the feasible CoP placement at the post-impact rollout phase. We train these models using refer-ence CoP data computed through conventional optimization-based techniques. These networks enable rapid evaluation of the robot’s post-impact state, with around 40 × speed-up compared to optimization-based techniques. We apply these networks in a contact planner which performs two passes for each hand. The first pass selects the optimal bracing surface by scoring the closest reachable point on each candidate surface. The second pass samples within the optimal surface (if one exists) to compute the optimal bracing point. 

A. Related Work 

Reactive contact planning for legged robots has been widely studied, with many successful hardware demonstra-tions. Reduced-order models such as the Linear Inverted Pen-dulum Model [1], the Angular Linear Inverted Pendulum [7] and Single Rigid Body Model [8] enable efficient previews of the robot’s centroidal dynamics. In turn, these models have been leveraged in heuristics for step placement, such as Raib-ert’s heuristic [9] and the Capture Point [2, 10]. Since these heuristics have closed-form linear representations, they have been applied in mixed-integer frameworks that select optimal contact surfaces online [11–14]. Following this approach, Mason et al. [15] reactively plan hand contacts by first previewing a footstep sequence containing a slack variable for centroidal tracking. If this slack variable is sufficiently high, a solver activates which scores candidate hand contacts arXiv:2610.00823v1 [cs.RO] 30 Sep 2026 based on a recovery preview, given a heuristic maximum hand pushing force. Such applications of centroidal models have been successful, but have the caveat of requiring linear or quadratic functions to encode cost and constraints and often are applied only to quasi-flat terrains. A related line of research addresses motion feasibility under arbitrary contact conditions given unilateral, friction and actuation constraints. Seminal work by Bretl et al. [5] posed this as a Linear Program, enabling efficient quasi-static stability assessment. This approach has been extended towards improving solve times [16], addressing dynamic stability [17] and modeling actuation constraints [6]. Marucci et al. apply a similar model to multi-contact planning on a piecewise affine map [18]. Other centroidal-based reactive recovery strategies have explored adapting gait frequency and posture to achieve high stability [19, 20]. There have also been machine-learning based approaches that aim to model stability and assess contact sequences. One approach, and the one taken in this work, is training a network to mimic an expensive optimization-based stability model [21]. Alternatively, Lin et al. use simulation data to train a set of networks to predict zero- and one-step cap-turability, given a robot’s state and environment [4]. These networks are applied in a graph-search that optimizes for pre-dicted success in the presence of disturbances, such that the robot walks nearby potential bracing surfaces. This approach of using procedurally generated simulation environments has seen many applications in legged motion planning [22, 23]. Rouxel et al. use datasets from teleoperated hardware experiments to train a network to load/unload a humanoid’s hand based on predicted stability [24]. 

B. Contributions 

This work builds on the core concepts of reduced-order models to achieve reflexive response times, while main-taining accurate stability modeling under arbitrary contact conditions by applying learned stability models. Our work particularly builds on the MPC-style bracing planner by Mason et al. [15] and the concept of optimal region selection by Corb` eres et al. [14]. In building on these approaches, this work contains the following novel contributions.  

> •

A reactive ( ≤10 ms) contact planner that models recov-ery for arbitrary (e.g. tilted or vertical) surfaces.  

> •

A set of neural network models that predict the robot’s feasible CoP placement given arbitrary combinations of hand and feet contacts.  

> •

Simulation demonstrations of the planner’s capability to out-perform a naive heuristic through a comparison of the minimum unrejectable impulse.  

> •

Hardware demonstrations of the planner and controller’s capacity to use hand contacts to recover from high impulses. II. B ACKGROUND

B. Feasible CoP region neural networks 

This work aims to achieve reactive hand contact planning and real-time control to enable a humanoid to brace in the presence of a disturbance. The optimization-based calcula-tion of the feasible CoP region described in Sec. III-A has a number of drawbacks for use in a time-critical context. Notably, it is parameterized by whole-body configuration 

q, requiring Inverse-Kinematics to preview the multi-contact CoP region, which is computationally expensive. Computing the contact Jacobian SVD to accurately model singularities is additional overhead. Finally, computing the multi-contact CoP region requires iteratively solving Eq. (2) along multiple query directions. To reduce computational overhead during planning and control, we train a set of neural networks which model the multi-contact CoP region, given the robot’s contact state. A. Planar Region Mapping 

The Alex humanoid is equipped with a ZED X Mini depth camera on the head. This depth image is used to extract a set of convex planar regions , which serves as the robot’s map of bracing surfaces. Planar region extraction is GPU-accelerated and streams at 30 Hz, in the style of [26, 27]. To prevent contact near the edge of surface, regions are shrunk by a safety distance of 7 cm. 

B. Centroidal Dynamics Rollout 

Candidate contacts are evaluated by rolling out the robot’s centroidal dynamics through pre-impact, impact and post-impact phases. In aiming for fast ( ≤10 ms), reflexive plan-ning of hand contacts, we take two simplifying assumptions. (i) Hand contacts are planned separately per-side, with the assumption that often the optimal strategy for a given side is independent of the other side. (ii) During rollout, we chose to ignore recovery strategies other than bracing with the upper body and using the multi-contact CoP region. This includes step adjustment, upper-body angular momentum and CoP feedback within the nominal CoP region. Note that these strategies are used by the controller. However, we observe these strategies do not conflict with the use of hand contacts and add significant complexity to previewing the robot’s state at the planning phase. B. Simulation 

We tested our framework in simulation to determine the maximum sustainable impulse in three scenarios. Shown in Fig. 8, these include standing, walking sideways, and walking backwards. At a fixed time in the simulation, an impulse is applied to the pelvis link. We perform a binary search to determine the maximum impulse that can be sustained without the robot falling. To quantify the effectiveness of our approach, we compare against two baselines: (i) no bracing and (ii) a naive planner that braces using the closest reachable point. In Fig. 8, the naive contact plans are depicted. For the standing and backwards walking simulations, the naive plan selects different bracing regions, which may be difficult to determine using simple heuristics. The sideways walking simulation only has a single bracing surface available, which is reachable by the left hand. This highlights the value of contact placement, which adjusts the contact by 14.9 cm, yet it yields 16 % improvement in impulse resilience.

Subsequent tests will aim to test higher push forces, which will induce longer post-impact recovery times. VII. C ONCLUSION 

Bracing with the upper body is a core recovery strategy used when humans are in low stability scenarios [29]. It is crucial for humanoids to also posses this ability in order to maximize reliability and reduce falls. Towards this goal, we presented a reactive, sampling-based planning approach that is motivated by the preview-based recovery approach from Mason et al. [15] and the optimal region selection from Corb` eres et al. [14]. We began by approximating the optimization-based approach to compute feasible CoP placement by a set of neural networks, by using distinct networks for each contact permutation. These networks allow us to bypass both the Inverse Kinematics and Linear Pro-gramming steps required to traditionally compute this region. Our findings show this translates to approximately 50 ×

speedup in computation time. In turn, this allows for rapid assessment of candidate brace points, which we evaluate by rolling out the centroidal dynamics, yielding a total reaction time of ≈10 ms. We demonstrate our approach in both simulation and hardware, achieving substantial improvement in stability compared to baseline walking and significant (15-20 %) improvement compared to naive bracing strategies. Our presented approach, though using some data-driven techniques, relies heavily on reduced-order models. Future work could investigate alternative methods for scoring can-didate contacts, particularly with respect to the impact map. One of our unexpected findings is that the impact phase, as opposed to the post-impact phase, often accounted for the majority of recovery. Other avenues for incorporating data-driven techniques could explore training networks to directly predict the optimal contacts, given the robot’s state and planar region map. APPENDIX 

A. Reachability Model 

The Alex robot has 3 shoulder joints and 1 elbow joint. We use a spherical model centered on the shoulder with a minimum (32 cm) and maximum (72 cm) reachability, centered on the shoulder. We encode this spherical workspace as a set of linear constraints when cropping the bracing region, in a similar style as [14].
