Abstract 

Continuous-time reinforcement learning seeks an optimal policy under unknown dynamics driven by stochastic differential equations using observations collected at discrete times. We develop a policy gradient framework that combines rigorous convergence analysis with learning from discrete data. We first establish a performance difference lemma and derive policy gradient formulas for randomized and parametrized policies. We then prove exponential convergence of an entropy-regularized policy flow to the regularized optimum. For discrete parametrized updates, we derive a convergence bound that explicitly separates the contributions of critic error, policy-regression error, and the learning step size. To enable learning under unknown dynamics, we integrate this framework with PhiBE, which constructs a continuous-time surrogate generator from discrete transition data. The resulting on-policy and off-policy model-free actor–critic algorithms preserve the continuous-time structure of the control problem. Numerical experiments on an entropy-regularized linear–quadratic regulator and a nonlinear wheeled robot demonstrate the performance and stability of the proposed methods with both known and data-driven dynamics. 

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) in continuous time provides a natural framework for sequential decision making in systems governed by differential equations, with applications in robotics [21], autonomous systems [33], manufacturing [31], and finance [25]. In many of these settings, the system evolves continuously while data are recorded only at discrete times through sensors or simulations. We consider controlled stochastic systems modeled by stochastic differential equations (SDEs), with decisions represented by randomized feedback policies. Thus, the dynamics and objective remain continuous in time, while learning relies on discrete observations. Value-based methods face several difficulties in continuous-time RL. Under a fixed policy, the value function is characterized by a PDE involving the infinitesimal generator of the controlled diffusion rather than by a Bellman recursion [39, 7]. Moreover, the usual discrete-time state-action value function loses its action-dependent information in the continuous-time limit, as the Q-function converges to the state value function when the action duration tends to zero [12]. Even after policy evaluation, extracting a policy requires additional computation: a greedy construction requires optimization over the action space at every state [20], while a softmax construction requires approximating its normalization constant [37]. Thus, continuous-time value-based methods require suitable formulations of policy evaluation and advantage estimation, followed by a separate policy-extraction step. Policy gradient methods avoid this separate policy-extraction step by optimizing a parametrized policy directly [34]. Direct policy parametrization also makes it possible to control the size of each update: trust-region and proximal methods restrict the change between consecutive policies through a KL constraint or 1arXiv:2610.28580v1 [cs.LG] 20 Oct 2026 a clipped surrogate objective [28, 29]. Policy gradient methods still require policy evaluation, since the gradient depends on an advantage function, but the resulting estimate is used to update the policy directly rather than to recover it through an additional maximization problem. For continuous-time stochastic control with known dynamics, policy gradient formulas and convergence results have been established under suitable assumptions [46, 8, 16, 30]. In this setting, the advantage can be constructed from the drift, the diffusion, and the policy value function [12]. These results provide a rigorous foundation for continuous-time policy optimization, but their convergence analyses generally assume access to the system coefficients or to exact policy-evaluation quantities. When the dynamics are unknown, the value and advantage must instead be learned from observed transitions. Existing approaches use actor–critic or adaptive dynamic programming methods [40, 13, 43], while other work focuses specifically on continuous-time policy evaluation [11]. Another approach is to formulate a discrete-time MDP on the observation grid, but this discards the continuous-time structure and introduces a discretization error even when the discrete-time transition kernel is known exactly [50]. A remaining challenge is to develop data-driven continuous-time policy gradient methods with convergence guarantees that explicitly account for errors in both policy evaluation and policy parametrization. 

Our contribution 

We establish rigorous convergence guarantees for policy gradient algorithms and develop model-free actor– critic methods based on this analysis. Our contributions are threefold. First, we establish a policy gradient theorem for randomized and parametrized policies from a performance difference lemma, expressed in terms of the continuous-time advantage and discounted occupancy measure. Second, we establish convergence guarantees for continuous-time policy optimization and finite-step parametrized updates. The entropy regularization yields exponential convergence of the policy gradient flow, while the finite-step analysis explicitly separates critic error, policy-regression error, and the learning step size. Third, we develop model-free on- and off-policy actor-critic algorithms based on the PhiBE framework directly from discrete transition data, without identifying the dynamics or reducing the problem to a discrete-time MDP. The methods are demonstrated on an entropy-regularized LQ problem and a nonlinear differential wheeled robot. 

Related work 

Exploratory continuous-time RL. The entropy-regularized exploratory formulation was introduced in [39], connecting randomized policies with relaxed stochastic control. Subsequent work studied the exploratory HJB equation and the vanishing-regularization limit [36, 27]. We adopt this formulation but focus on policy gradient optimization and learning from discrete data. 

Policy gradient and actor–critic in continuous time. Advantage updating was introduced in [2] to learn state values and action-dependent advantage rates separately. The collapse of the conventional discrete-time Q-function towards the value function in the continuous-time limit was subsequently formalized in [35]. For controlled diffusions, [10] characterized policy evaluation through martingale identities and developed methods based on martingale losses or orthogonality conditions. This approach was extended to parametrized policy gradients and actor–critic algorithms in [11], and to the little-q function in [12], which represents an instantaneous action-dependent return rate and is jointly characterized with the value function through on-and off-policy martingale conditions. Closest to our theoretical analysis, [42] uses discounted occupation measures to derive performance-difference and local-approximation formulas, together with a parametric policy-gradient representation. We first characterize the directional derivative of the performance functional along general admissible policy perturbations, controlling the perturbed occupation measure through SDE stability estimates, and then establish the corresponding parametric score formula. At the algorithmic level, we develop model-free on- and off-policy actor–critic methods based on PhiBE [48, 50]. In contrast to the neural actor–critic solver for elliptic HJB equations in [44], our methods require neither known drift 2nor known diffusion coefficients. While the online actor updates in [11], the sampled advantage estimator in [42], and the control-randomization actor updates in [6] use differences of value-function estimates, our construction preserves the differential structure of both policy evaluation and advantage estimation: it retains the first- and second-order spatial derivatives of the critic and replaces the unknown generator by a PhiBE operator that uses discrete-time data. 

Relation to discrete-time policy gradient. Our analysis shares two central ingredients with policy gradient theory for discounted MDPs. First, the parametric policy gradient is expressed as an occupation-weighted expectation of the advantage multiplied by the policy score [34]. Second, the convergence argument relies on a performance difference lemma, as in discrete-time policy-improvement analyses [15]. The approxi-mation of the advantage-driven direction by a linear combination of policy scores is also related to compatible function approximation and natural policy gradient [14, 1]. The main difference is that the continuous-time advantage is an instantaneous rate determined by the generator of the controlled diffusion, and the occupa-tion measure accumulates rewards over continuous time. Accordingly, our finite-step update discretizes the policy-optimization dynamics rather than the controlled system. 

Convergence of policy gradient in continuous control. Existing convergence results can be distin-guished by whether they concern policy flows, discrete updates in function space, or in finite-dimensional parametrized policies. At the flow level, convergence guarantees have been established for regularized measure-valued controls [32], policy mirror descent for exit-time control [30], feedback policy gradients [46], and coupled actor–critic dynamics [45]. These results describe the zero-step-size limit of policy op-timization. We likewise establish exponential convergence of an entropy-regularized policy flow, but consider infinite-horizon stationary randomized policies and use this result as the foundation for analyzing discrete parametrized updates. For nonzero step sizes, [26] proves linear convergence of a proximal policy gradient method for finite-horizon feedback control, including stability under approximate gradients, while [16] establishes convergence rates for discrete mirror descent with measure-valued controls. These analyses optimize directly over suitable function spaces of feedback controls or adapted control processes, rather than over a prescribed finite-dimensional policy class. Consequently, they do not explicitly quantify the additional approximation error introduced by restricting policy updates to such a parametrized class. The existing convergence guarantees for parametrized updates among these works rely on LQ or closely related problem-specific structure [8, 41, 38]. To the best of our knowledge, our work provides the first convergence bound for discrete, finite-dimensional parametrized policy-gradient updates in a general nonlin-ear diffusion control setting that explicitly accounts for critic error, compatible score-regression error, and the actor step size. Under suitable regularity and approximation-error assumptions, the bound establishes geometric decay to an error neighborhood whose size is controlled by these quantities. 

Data-driven and PDE-based continuous-time RL. PhiBE [48] embeds discrete transition information into a continuous-time PDE for policy evaluation, while Optimal-PhiBE [50] extends this construction to control and establishes exact recovery of the continuous-time optimal policy in the undiscounted LQ setting. The state-action PhiBE formulation in [49] enables off-policy learning while retaining instantaneous action-dependent information that vanishes from the conventional discrete-time Q-function in the continuous-time limit. Earlier actor–critic and adaptive-dynamic-programming methods [40, 43, 13, 17] focus mainly on structured systems, with theoretical guarantees also available for learning unknown LQ dynamics from discrete observations [3]. We use PhiBE as the critic within policy gradient and treat the discrepancy between the estimated and true advantages as the critic error appearing in the discrete convergence bound.

6.1.1 Known dynamics 

Figure 1a compares the exact policy gradient with the Monte Carlo estimators obtained from discounted-occupancy and uniform state sampling. The discounted-occupancy estimator, which is unbiased by con-struction, is almost indistinguishable from the exact gradient. In contrast, uniform state sampling produces a systematically slower curve. Although it still converges in this scalar example, it uses a different state weighting and therefore gives a biased estimate of the policy gradient. Figure 1b compares the four policy optimization methods under the default configuration. All methods rapidly reduce the error, with differences in their long-run behavior becoming visible mainly in the logarithmic inset. Across the additional experiments, we observe that the stability of the policy update depends strongly on the effective step size relative to the scaling of the dynamics. Changes in A mainly affect the transient behavior, whereas larger values of B or σ can make a fixed vanilla-PG step substantially less stable. NPG, TRPO, and PPO are more robust under these changes. Smaller effective steps improve stability at the cost of slower convergence, while larger steps accelerate the initial decrease but can produce oscillations or leave the admissible region. In the tested PPO configurations, changing the clipping ratio has a comparatively small effect. We refer to Section A.4 for the corresponding parameter studies and their detailed analysis.

6.1.4 Data-driven: on-policy PhiBE 

In the on-policy experiment, every actor iteration uses a fresh batch from the current discounted occupancy measure. We sample actions from ai ∼ πωk (·| Si) and observe one local transition over ∆ t. Hence the critic and advantage use fresh current-policy data, and the actor has the state weighting required by (45). We 18 use the state basis ϕ(s) = (1 , s, s 2)⊤, n = 1 ,000 transitions per iteration, and ∆ t = 0 .1. The default update parameters are ηPG = ηNPG = 0 .05, δTRPO = 0 .005, and ηPPO = 0 .005, with five PPO epochs and ϵ = 0 .2. The implementation used in these experiments is detailed in Algorithm 3 in Section A.4. Figure 2c shows the behavior under the default configuration. All four methods rapidly reduce the coefficient error and enter a low-error region. In contrast to the known-dynamics experiments, however, the error does not continue toward numerical precision. Since a new finite-sample critic and advantage are estimated at every actor iteration, the curves fluctuate around a nonzero statistical error level. The additional experiments show that this behavior is robust to changes in A and B, while high diffusion is substantially more challenging because it increases the variability of the data-driven moment estimates. The effective update size controls the usual speed–stability trade-off: smaller steps give smoother but slower transients, whereas larger steps amplify the iteration-to-iteration fluctuations. Changing the PPO clipping ratio has a comparatively small effect in the tested range. Overall, these experiments indicate that the long-run variability is mainly associated with the finite-sample critic and advantage estimation rather than with a failure of policy improvement. We refer to Section A.4 for the corresponding parameter studies and their detailed discussion. 

Comparison across regimes. The experiments display a consistent progression. With exact dynamics, the remaining error is mainly controlled by the actor step and the choice of policy update. Replacing the dynamics by the drift-only Optimal-PhiBE surrogate preserves essentially the same optimization trends. With data-driven PhiBE estimates, the methods still reach a low-error region, but a nonzero statistical floor appears whose size depends on the amount and temporal resolution of the data and on the variability of the dynamics, as illustrated by the dependence on n, ∆ t, and σ. Trust-region and clipped updates become particularly useful when this estimation noise or the scaling of the dynamics makes a fixed Euclidean step unstable. These observations are consistent with the separation between policy-update and critic-approximation errors developed in the preceding sections.

6.2.3 Data-driven results 

The data-driven critic and actor use only rewards and observed increments in their PhiBE quantities. The analytical drift and diffusion in (47) are used only by the simulator that supplies the transitions and by the post-training Monte Carlo validation. 

Policy evaluation. We first evaluate a fixed policy using only one-step transition data. Figure 3c shows that the squared PhiBE residual decreases by several orders of magnitude before fluctuating around a small nonzero level. In contrast with the known-dynamics PDE residual, this statistical floor is expected because the reward and local PhiBE quantities are estimated from finitely many transitions and the squared one-sample residual is affected by the double-sampling effect described in Remark 6.1. Independent Monte Carlo validation shows that the fitted critic nevertheless recovers the main structure of the value function. Across the tested headings, the fitted values reproduce the location of the high-value region and its systematic change with the robot orientation, although some differences in contour geometry and value level remain. Thus, the nonzero residual floor does not prevent the data-driven critic from recovering a meaningful approximation of the policy value. The corresponding fitted–reference comparisons are reported in Section A.4.

## 7 Conclusion 

We developed a policy gradient framework for continuous-time reinforcement learning with theoretical con-vergence guarantees and data-driven implementations. Starting from a performance difference lemma, we derived policy gradient formulas for randomized and parametrized policies, established exponential conver-gence of the entropy-regularized policy flow, and obtained convergence bounds for discrete updates that account for critic and policy-regression errors. By incorporating PhiBE, we constructed on- and off-policy actor–critic methods that learn from discrete observations while preserving the continuous-time dynamics. Numerical experiments on an LQR problem and a nonlinear wheeled robot demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Figure 10: On-policy data-driven PhiBE: sensitivity to the update parameters. large excursions show that decreasing the actor step alone does not remove the variability introduced by the finite-sample estimates of the local moments. Figure 10 studies the sensitivity to the policy-update parameters. Dividing the effective steps by ten produces smoother but slower convergence, whereas multiplying them by ten reaches the low-error region faster but increases the iteration-to-iteration fluctuations. Thus, the update size controls the speed–stability trade-off, while the persistent long-run fluctuations remain associated with the resampled critic and advan-tage. The PPO curves remain close for the tested clipping ratios, indicating that clipping has a comparatively small effect on the long-run error in this configuration. In the robustness experiment, taking ϵ = 10 largely removes the effect of clipping. Combined with a larger learning rate, this leads to substantially less stable updates, illustrating the role of restricting the policy change when the critic itself is estimated from fresh finite-sample data.
