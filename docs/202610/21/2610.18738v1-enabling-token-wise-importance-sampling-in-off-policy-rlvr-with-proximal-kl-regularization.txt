# Enabling Token-wise Importance Sampling in Off-Policy RLVR with Proximal KL Regularization 

Hao Bai Jiarui Yao Tong Zhang 

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign 

Abstract 

In reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), asynchronous generation, dif-ferences between rollout and training policies, and response reuse across optimizer minibatch updates can all produce off-policy data. Token-wise importance weighting allows policy-gradient methods to reuse these responses without multiplying likelihood ratios across a long response, but it generally biases the gradient. We prove that, under coverage and local expressiveness con-ditions, adding full future KL credit makes the exact proximal optimizer the update’s unique stationary policy. The result holds even when responses come from a mixture of older poli-cies and requires neither full-response importance weights nor a fitted critic. We also prove convergence of continuous-time tabular population updates under persistent coverage and time-varying behavior policies, and give a finite-sample reward guarantee for linear logistic models under additional assumptions. These results motivate Proximal KL , a practical off-policy RLVR method that separates the proximal reference from the sampling policy and replaces ordinary PPO clipping with bounded future KL credit. Across all seven matched off-policy comparisons on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, Proximal KL achieves higher micro-averaged accuracy than GRPO over eight math benchmarks, spanning two learning rates, response reuse, and rollout staleness. The absolute improvement in micro-averaged accuracy reaches 2.58% . A separate on-policy comparison shows comparable aggregate accuracy for the two methods. These gains accompany stable optimization: in the periodic-refresh experiments, Proximal KL avoids the training collapses observed in PG and CISPO, attains higher training accuracy than GRPO, and reaches higher accuracy in fewer policy updates. To explain these gains, ablations first compare how off-policy sampling is corrected: token-wise weighting outperforms sequence-level and unweighted controls. With token-wise correction retained, future KL feedback reduces sen-sitivity to the tested baseline perturbation relative to PPO clipping. The extent of this feedback matters as well: a finite future cost horizon improves AIME24 mean@16 and pass@16 over the full tail, while fixed-policy measurements show that truncation reduces correction variance over the tested horizons. # 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) optimizes a policy from verifier-scored re-sponses, often generated by a behavior policy that differs from the policy being optimized. This off-policy mismatch has several sources. Asynchronous systems overlap generation and optimiza-tion, so responses may come from earlier policy versions (Fu et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025; RadixArk, 2026). Reusing collected trajectories across optimizer minibatch updates introduces mismatch as the current policy changes, even when collection is synchronous. We study the common problem of learning from these off-policy responses. 1arXiv:2610.18738v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 Policy-gradient methods such as GRPO and DAPO use token-wise importance ratios (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Exact importance correction of a response-level objective instead multiplies the ratios across the response, which can have high variance for long responses. Token-wise weighting avoids this product but generally biases the gradient. We ask when an update based on token-wise weights and sampled rewards, without a fitted value or Q function, still retains the proximal target. This target is the optimizer of a KL-regularized objective. We study a proximal objective that penalizes the reverse KL divergence from the current policy to a reference policy held fixed during each optimization round (Geist et al., 2019; Tomar et al., 2022). The reference defines the improvement target, while the behavior policy determines the sampling distribution. Following batch-size-invariant policy optimization (Hilton et al., 2022), we separate these policies so the target remains fixed even when the data-generating distribution differs from the current policy. For autoregressive responses, the regularized policy gradient assigns each token the current and all future token log-ratio costs, as expressed by Future-KL credit (Yao et al., 2026). We combine this full credit with a single importance ratio for the current token. Our main result shows that, under coverage and local expressiveness, the expected token-wise update with full future KL credit vanishes if and only if the learner equals the proximal optimizer. The result holds for any supported stale behavior policy or mixture of behavior-policy versions, although the expected update can differ from the exact proximal gradient away from the optimizer. The proof uses the soft path-consistency identity established by PCL and Trust-PCL (Nachum et al., 2017, 2018). With independent tabular logits and persistent coverage, we further prove convergence of continuous-time updates (Appendix E). To study learning from sampled responses, we also give a finite-sample reward guarantee after re-peated reference refreshes for a linear logistic model, whose token logits depend linearly on trainable parameters through fixed features (summarized in Theorem 2, with the precise statement in Ap-pendix F). Under its assumptions, the final policy approaches the comparator’s expected reward for a single response. Its mismatch condition bounds the sum of learner-weighted squared token log ratios along each response path, and the update never forms the response-level product of ratios. This analysis motivates a practical algorithm that can also be applied to nonlinear models such as Transformers. Guided by these results, we develop Proximal KL Regularization (Proximal KL ). The practical update records each token’s behavior probability, periodically refreshes the proximal reference, and subtracts bounded future KL costs from a group-relative reward advantage before applying the token-wise importance weight. A bound on each log-ratio cost limits individual contributions, while a tunable horizon limits how many future costs are included. The update omits ordinary PPO clipping. Section 4 discusses the trade-off between bias and variance when bounding the costs, and Section 6.4 evaluates these choices empirically. 

Contributions. To our knowledge, this is the first stationary-policy guarantee for critic-free token-wise importance weighting with full future KL credit under supported stale behavior mixtures. We also prove tabular convergence and, for a realizable linear logistic model, a finite-sample reward guarantee under correct-solution coverage using a full-credit group update with repeated reference refreshes (Theorem 2). Our empirical comparisons show that Proximal KL achieves higher micro-averaged held-out accuracy than GRPO in all seven matched off-policy settings in Section 6.3. These gains span eight math benchmarks, 1.7B and 4B models, and the tested learning rates and rollout staleness levels, including fresh rollouts reused across optimizer minibatches. In the separate on-policy comparison, Proximal KL attains aggregate accuracy comparable to GRPO. Comple-mentary periodic-refresh experiments show that Proximal KL sustains training gains through the collapses observed in PG and CISPO and attains higher training accuracy than GRPO at compara-2ble optimizer-update counts (Section 6.2). The ablations connect these outcomes to update design: current-token importance correction outperforms Sequence-level IS and IS-free settings, and future KL feedback is less sensitive to the tested baseline perturbation than PPO clipping. The tightest tested future-cost bound combines strong accuracy with smaller gradient-norm excursions, while a finite horizon outperforms both strong local and full-tail corrections. Fixed-policy measurements confirm that truncation reduces correction variance over the tested horizons (Section 6.4). 

# 2 Related Work 

Proximal updates. Proximal methods control policy changes relative to a reference policy, as in TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015). Regularized MDPs and mirror-descent policy optimization formalize KL-based updates (Geist et al., 2019; Tomar et al., 2022). Batch-size-invariant policy optimization (Hilton et al., 2022) distinguishes the proximal reference from the behavior policy, separating the optimization target from the sampling distribution. AReaL and AsyncWebRL apply this distinc-tion to asynchronous optimization (Fu et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2026). Slime and Miles support asynchronous rollout and training pipelines that reduce bubble time (Zhu et al., 2025; RadixArk, 2026). FRPO derives the on-policy Future-KL gradient identity for autoregressive regularization (Yao et al., 2026). We use this identity for expected reward regularized against a fixed proximal reference (Appendix D.2), then characterize the stationary policy when full future credit is com-bined with a single current-token importance ratio under behavior-policy mismatch, including stale behavior mixtures. PCL establishes soft path consistency and minimizes the corresponding errors on off-policy trajec-tories (Nachum et al., 2017). Trust-PCL extends this approach to relative-entropy regularization against an updated reference (Nachum et al., 2018). Soft Q-learning learns soft Bellman values and an associated sampling policy (Haarnoja et al., 2017); Unified PCL represents policy and value with one Q model (Nachum et al., 2017). Schulman et al. (2017a) derive regularized Monte Carlo policy gradients and relate them to soft Q-learning with value fitting. Our analysis concerns the stationary policy of an update that combines policy scores with token-wise importance weights, sampled rewards, and full future credit. The update requires no fitted values or optimization of a consistency residual (Section 5). 

Policy-gradient methods. REINFORCE uses sampled policy scores (Williams, 1992), while PPO uses a clipped policy-gradient surrogate (Schulman et al., 2017b). For language-model reason-ing, GRPO constructs group-relative advantages (Shao et al., 2024), and DAPO develops clipping and sampling choices (Yu et al., 2025). V-trace and Retrace correct bootstrapped value or Q targets for off-policy data using truncated importance traces (Espeholt et al., 2018; Munos et al., 2016). V-trace also weights its policy update by a current-action ratio. Their fixed-point guarantees concern corrected value learning. We establish the unique stationary policy of the actor update itself, using sampled rewards, full future KL credit, and current-token importance weighting without a fitted critic. Compatible approximation and policy-improvement closure provide structural conditions for policy-gradient convergence (Agarwal et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023; Bhandari and Russo, 2024). Ap-pendix F relates these conditions to our bounded Gibbs-target representation assumption and its concrete examples. # 5 Theoretical Guarantees 

Full future KL credit gives token-wise importance weighting a common proximal target, even when the responses come from stale policies. We first state this mechanism for population updates. We then summarize a finite-sample reward guarantee for a projected group-gradient variant; Appendix F gives its full specification and proof. The key observation is that, at the proximal target, the reward minus the full future KL cost depends only on the current history. The sampled continuation no longer changes this coefficient. Current-token importance weighting then makes its expected score contribution zero, even when later tokens were generated by an older policy. The first result shows that, under the stated conditions, this cancellation also identifies the target uniquely.

Here local expressiveness means that parameter changes span every independent token log-odds direction. The proof and precise assumptions are in Appendix E. Local expressiveness lets us translate a zero parameter update into a condition at each history, while behavior coverage ensures that every relevant history contributes to that condition. Different behavior policies can therefore change the update direction without changing its station-ary policy. Convergence additionally requires progress toward that target. For independent tabular logits and history visit probabilities uniformly bounded away from zero, global trajectories of the continuous-time population update converge under time-varying behavior policies (Theorem E.2). This establishes convergence toward a fixed reference’s target when the expected update is available exactly. To account for sampled responses and repeated reference refreshes, we next study a linear logistic model with bounded fixed features. A frozen-feature linear output head is one example; ap-plying a linearized model to a nonlinear Transformer additionally requires control of approximation errors. In this setting, the finite-sample result below shows how repeatedly fitting and refreshing the reference can improve the final policy’s reward.

δ can require tighter token-ratio control and increase cδ. On policy, Vlog = 0 and the direct history-transfer bound yields 1/ε dependence, apart from the logarithmic round-count factors. The guarantee shows that repeated proximal updates can turn initial correct-solution coverage into a policy whose single-response success probability approaches the comparator’s. Each round ap-proximately fits the current reference’s proximal target; refreshing the reference then allows further reward improvement. Appendix G gives examples of the representation bound; Remark F.11 gives the uniform-coverage case. 

Single-response reward compared with best-of-N . The comparator can also describe a procedure that samples several responses at inference time. This gives a concrete interpretation of the single-response guarantee. Let p0(x) = Pr π0 (R = 1 | x). Drawing N responses from the initial policy and selecting a correct one whenever available gives oracle success probability 

1 − (1 − p0(x)) N ≤ N p 0(x). Thus the guarantee applies with C⋆ = N : one response from the trained policy approaches the reward of oracle best-of-N selection at initialization, under the theo-rem’s conditions. The training budget and the oracle’s inference budget are accounted for separately. These results explain the roles of the two policies in the practical algorithm. For implementation, the reference determines the improvement target, while the logged behavior policy determines the importance correction. Full future KL credit preserves the target in the analyzed population update. We evaluate Proximal KL in two off-policy training settings. Periodic rollout-policy refresh tests stability across model sizes. 6.1 Setup 

All compared methods omit the KL penalty to the initial model. The reward-advantage normaliza-tion follows the training setting below and is shared across methods within each setting. 

Working Default. We recommend H = full , β = 0 .30 , and ϵF = 0 .05 as a general starting configuration for Proximal KL . This choice is shared across all four model sizes in the periodic-refresh experiments. A shorter horizon provides a model-specific adjustment; for 4B, we suggest 

H = 32 as a starting point with the same coefficient and cost bound. The ablations examine how horizon tuning can further improve accuracy. The actual configurations used for the reported results are listed in Appendices B.2 and C.2. 

Training with Periodic Policy Refresh. At each training iteration, we collect a fresh batch of responses and use that batch once for a single optimizer update. The training-policy weights therefore change every iteration, whereas the rollout-policy weights are synchronized with the train-ing policy only once every 64 optimizer updates and remain fixed between refreshes. Successive batches can thus be generated by the same older policy while training progresses. This provides a controlled simulation of delayed policy synchronization in asynchronous RL, where response genera-tion uses earlier policy versions while optimization continues. We train Qwen3 models (Yang et al., 2025) on verifiable math tasks using Slime (Zhu et al., 2025), comparing PG, CISPO, GRPO, and 

Proximal KL across model sizes under this shared refresh schedule. Reward advantages subtract the mean reward within each prompt group, without standard-deviation normalization. Figure 3 reports training accuracy, defined in Appendix B.1; all configurations are in Appendix B.2. 

Training with Controlled Rollout Staleness. Following AReaL (Fu et al., 2025), we combine asynchronous rollout generation with multiple optimizer minibatches per batch on filtered math prompts. We explicitly control the permitted rollout staleness and verify the realized policy-version gaps from training logs. Reward advantages standardize rewards within each prompt group, as defined in Equation (A.2). We compare GRPO and Proximal KL , adding PG for the stability test, and include a separate comparison with fresh rollouts and one optimizer minibatch. Held-out evaluation covers MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024), MinervaMath (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), Gaokao2023MathEn, AIME24/25 (Mathe-matical Association of America, n.d.), and AMC23/24. A finite horizon can outperform the full-tail working default. Starting from the full-tail working default, we ask whether truncating future credit can further improve accuracy. Figure 9 compares the full tail with intermediate windows and a one-token correction. The comparison includes both a fixed KL coefficient and a matched product βH , allowing us to examine whether increasing the local coefficient can compensate for removing future terms. Both intermediate-window settings outperform the full tail in AIME24 mean@16 and pass@16 near the end of their shared observation range, including with the default KL coefficient held fixed. With βH matched, the one-token correction improves mean@16 more slowly than the intermediate window. Thus, shortening the tail can improve on the working default, while increasing the local coefficient does not replace future-token credit. This is the practical trade-off suggested by the theory: truncation omits part of the ideal future credit, but can reduce fluctuations in the correction. The fixed-policy diagnostic below measures this second effect independently of training. 

Truncating the future sum reduces correction variance at fixed policies. To test the variance argument for horizon truncation in Section 4, we measure how extending the future sum changes correction variability at fixed policies. For each retained response, we accumulate the scalar correction at its first token, compute its sample variance across responses to the same prompt, and 17 average these variances across prompts. Policies, responses, the correction coefficient, and the cost bound are held fixed across horizons. Appendix C.3 specifies the calculation; this paired comparison isolates the effect of extending the sum on the same data. Figure 9(c) shows that, with policies and responses held fixed, the average variance of the accumu-lated correction increases over the tested horizons. Truncating the tail therefore reduces correction variability in this diagnostic. Longer sums fluctuate more across responses to the same prompt, on average, even without any change in the policies. This supports horizon truncation as a way to control fluctuations in future KL feedback. The measurement quantifies variation in the scalar correction; the training curves show how horizon choice affects held-out accuracy. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Proximal KL combines token-wise importance weighting with future KL credit for off-policy RLVR, where the behavior policy differs from the policy being optimized. Under the stated coverage and expressiveness conditions, we prove that the full-tail critic-free update has the exact proximal optimizer as its unique stationary policy and that tabular population flow converges to this target. Repeated reference refreshes allow further reward improvement: for linear logistic models, we give a finite-sample reward guarantee for a projected group update under the stated representation, sampling, and correct-solution coverage assumptions. The bound depends on an aggregate token-wise log-ratio second moment. The practical algorithm uses clipped costs and an optional finite window. The held-out evaluations in Section 6.3 show higher micro-averaged accuracy for Proximal KL than GRPO in all seven matched off-policy settings, while the separate on-policy comparison yields comparable aggregate accuracy. Periodic-refresh experiments complement these gains with stable training where PG and CISPO can collapse, and higher training accuracy than GRPO at comparable optimizer-update counts. The ablations favor retaining current-token correction and future credit together, while bounding individual costs and truncating the horizon control correction fluctuations. An adaptive choice of H could improve this balance by retaining useful future credit without accumulating unnecessary variability.

Rollout generation overlaps with policy optimization, and collected batches are reused across suc-cessive optimizer minibatches. Evaluation metrics. Mean@16 measures correctness per response, while pass@16 measures the fraction of prompts solved at least once. For independent attempts with per-prompt success prob-24 ability p, expected pass@16 is 1 − (1 − p)16 .
