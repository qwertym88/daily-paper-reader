# Synthesis of the Transient Behaviour of Optimization Algorithms for Strongly Convex Objective Functions 

Adel Abdelsamed 1, Dennis Gramlich 1, Christian Ebenbauer 1 and Carsten W. Scherer 2

Abstract — We study the transient behavior of first-order op-timization methods for unconstrained minimization of smooth and strongly convex objective functions. These methods are modelled as linear systems over finite-time intervals embedded in feedback with the gradient nonlinearity. By characterizing the function class through discrete-time Zames–Falb integral quadratic constraints, we employ a robust H2 control frame-work to obtain convex semidefinite programs certifying tight worst-case bounds on the terminal distance to the optimizer. Building on the same formulation, we derive a convex synthesis procedure for a transient-optimal method that achieves the optimal finite-horizon bounds and guarantees error reduction over any prescribed number of steps. The framework naturally extends to delayed gradient measurements, enabling both tran-sient performance analysis and synthesis of optimal algorithms under feedback delays. 

I. INTRODUCTION Classical analyses of convex optimization algorithms empha-size asymptotic convergence, whereas many applications are dominated by finite-time, or transient, performance. Model predictive control is a prominent example: the optimization problem must be solved within a fixed sampling interval, meaning only a limited number of iterations are available before a decision must be made. In such settings, the tran-sient behaviour of the algorithm — how quickly and reliably it approaches the optimizer in the first few iterations — is of greater practical relevance than its eventual asymptotic rate. To address this gap in transient analysis, this paper aims to unify two complementary research streams. The first stream was initiated by the seminal work of Drori and Teboulle, who introduced the Performance Estimation Prob-lem (PEP) framework for analyzing worst-case behaviour of first-order methods applied to smooth convex functions and provided approximate solutions [1]. Subsequent work by Taylor et al. delivered exact analytical PEP solutions and the Performance Estimation Toolbox (PESTO), enabling systematic semidefinite programming–based analysis over a broad range of performance criteria [2], [3]. The second research stream, closer in spirit to this pa-per, adopts a control-theoretic framework for analyzing and designing optimization algorithms. In this view, optimiza-tion methods are treated as dynamical systems and studied with tools from control theory. Earlier work has designed continuous-time optimization algorithms as smooth feedback systems, establishing convergence through passivity-based arguments [6], and has extended classical momentum meth-ods, which are naturally described as second-order systems, to higher-order integrator dynamics with convergence guar-antees provided by robust control techniques [7]. Robust con-trol techniques such as integral quadratic constraints (IQCs) have proven particularly powerful in this context, as also demonstrated by Lessard et al., who exploit Lur´ e systems and IQCs to analyze the worst-case asymptotic convergence of first-order methods [8]. More recently, convex synthesis approaches based on dynamic IQCs have been developed to automatically design accelerated first-order methods with optimal guaranteed asymptotic rates, even in the presence of nontrivial dynamics such as delays [9], [10]. Within this control-theoretic stream, the transient be-haviour of accelerated methods has also been studied in [11]. Mohammadi et al. attribute the transient growth of Nesterov’s and the heavy-ball method on convex quadratic objectives to nonnormal dynamics and, using IQCs, establish analytical upper and lower bounds on the peak deviation from the optimizer for the general class of smooth and strongly convex objectives. These results quantify the transient cost of acceleration for given algorithms using a specific notion of transient growth, but does not address algorithm synthesis or additional dynamics such as delayed gradient measurements. The main contributions of this paper are two-fold. First, we propose a control-theoretic framework, based on discrete-time Zames–Falb IQCs, to analyze the worst-case transient 

performance of first-order algorithms in the input–output domain, for general finite-horizon quadratic performance ob-jectives. The resulting convex semidefinite programs (SDPs) provide tight finite-horizon upper bounds that coincide with those obtained from exact PEP formulations. Second, we use Zames–Falb multipliers to synthesize a transient-optimal lin-ear time-varying first-order method. Notably, the synthesized parameters recover the information-theoretic exact method (ITEM) as a special case, demonstrating that optimal finite-arXiv:2610.23206v1 [math.OC] 16 Oct 2026 horizon performance can also be realized via IQC-based synthesis. A key feature of our synthesis is the ability to explicitly model delays in the gradient measurements, which are unavoidable in practical applications due to communica-tion latencies or computation times and can severely degrade the critical first few iterations of an algorithm. The paper is organized as follows. Section II first in-troduces the class of smooth strongly convex objective functions, then establishes the finite-time horizon systems framework and thereby the considered class of first-order optimization algorithms from a control-theoretic perspective. To characterize the gradient nonlinearities, the notion of IQCs is also introduced. In Section III, the formal problem is introduced. Section IV then presents the analysis prob-lem and a convexification of the synthesis problem, while Section V numerically validates the obtained upper bounds for different well-established first-order algorithms. Lastly, in Section VI a summary and an outlook are provided.

Classical analyses evaluate the performance of optimization algorithms through the worst-case convergence rate ρwc (κ) ∈

(0 , 1) with the associated bound 

∥y(t) − z⋆∥2 ≤ c(κ)2 ρ2twc (κ) ∥ξ − z⋆∥2, (7) which must be satisfied for every f ∈ Sμ,L and every initial condition ξ at each stage t. This asymptotic bound separates two factors with fundamentally different roles. While ρ2twc (κ)

dominates for large t, finite-horizon performance is strongly influenced by the generally unknown constant c(κ), which can be large. In this paper, we propose a new bound on the terminal distance, which merges the asymptotic and transient effects. The considered finite-horizon bound is of the form 

∥y(τ ) − z⋆∥2 ≤ γ(κ, τ )2 ∥ξ − z⋆∥2, (8) where γ(κ, τ )2 is the worst-case squared amplification factor over a horizon of τ iterations. Unlike ρ2twc (κ), a convergent algorithm might have γ(κ, τ )2 ≥ 1 for small τ . In general, transient performance of an algorithm depends on the al-gorithm parameters, the objective function, and the initial condition. For optimization algorithms representable by the class (4), modelling their linear dynamics over finite-time intervals renders both the analysis of γ(κ, τ )2 for a given algorithm and the synthesis problem tractable via convex SDPs. In particular, in the synthesis problem, we seek algorithm parameters that minimize a worst-case performance criterion over all f ∈ Sμ,L , while measuring the sensitivity to the initial condition through an average quadratic criterion. To this end, the initial iterate ξ is modelled as an exogenous random vector with finite first and second moments satisfying 

E[ξ] = z⋆, Cov( ξ) = Σ0 = I. A quadratic performance output is used to capture objectives such as the terminal distance to the optimizer. In control theory, the expected value of such a quadratic output, when the system is driven by a random exogenous input with prescribed second-order moments, defines an H2-type performance criterion. The resulting synthesis problem is therefore a robust H2 problem, in which the unknown gradient map ∇f plays the role of the uncertainty. We formally define the problem.

The synthesis Problem ( H2-cvx) is solved for τ ∈ [1 , 30] 

and κ ∈ { 10 , 100 , 1000 } using MATLAB/YALMIP [15] and MOSEK [16], with r = 1 . This yields a distinct time-varying algorithm for every horizon; each point on the OPT-SYN curve, therefore, corresponds to a different synthesized method. The synthesized algorithms (OPT-SYN) are bench-marked against the gradient descent method (GDM), NAGM, the triple momentum method (TMM) [17], and ITEM, where all benchmark methods are parameterized for optimal asymp-totic convergence on the function class Sμ,L [8]. The upper bounds for the benchmark methods are obtained by solving the corresponding analysis Problem ( H2-rel). The resulting upper bounds γ2(τ, κ ) are shown in Fig. 2. A key distinction between the methods is their transient behaviour. GDM guarantees γ2 < 1 for all τ ≥ 1, albeit at a slower asymptotic convergence rate. Momentum methods such as NAGM and TMM achieve superior asymptotic rates, but at the cost of transient amplification: both exhibit γ2 > 1

for small τ , indicating that the worst-case iterate distance to the optimum increases before eventually decreasing. This transient cost grows more pronounced with increasing κ as the amplification region extends significantly, which is espe-cially visible in the middle plot ( κ = 100 ) of Figure 2. Even TMM, which achieves the optimal asymptotic convergence rate, incurs a non-negligible transient cost. In contrast to the quadratic case studied in [11], balanced initialization does not eliminate worst-case transient amplification for NAGM over the class Sμ,L .The synthesized algorithms maintain γ2 < 1 for all tested 

τ and κ. For any fixed finite-time horizon τ , the synthesized algorithms guarantee a reduction of the error after τ steps ,even in the worst case. Moreover, larger horizons yield stronger error reduction, i.e., the achieved decrease over a horizon τ2 is greater than that obtained with any shorter horizon τ1 < τ 2. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2, the upper bounds of OPT-SYN coincide with those of ITEM and the synthesized parameters recover the ITEM parameters, numerically confirming that the proposed method attains the information-theoretic complexity lower bound of [4]. Therefore, no algorithm within the considered class can achieve tighter worst-case guarantees. This paper developed an input–output framework for analyz-ing and synthesizing first-order methods for smooth strongly convex problems over a finite horizon, focusing on the terminal distance (although other objectives are possible). The analysis reduces to solving a convex semidefinite pro-gram that certifies worst-case transient performance. The same IQC-based formulation enables convex synthesis of a transient-optimal algorithm. Using time-varying Zames– Falb multipliers, the synthesized method guarantees descent after any horizon length and recovers the ITEM parameters, thereby achieving the information-theoretic complexity lower bound. This shows that finite-horizon optimal performance, previously obtained via Performance Estimation Problems, can equally be realized through Zames–Falb IQC synthesis. Moreover, the framework seamlessly extends to problems with feedback delay, where the optimal algorithm respects the inherent latency and achieves the best possible finite-horizon performance. Future work includes extending the framework to state-space realizations to address two practical limitations of the current input-output approach. First, the SDPs involve matrix variables of dimension τ ×τ , making computation prohibitive for large horizon lengths. Second, the synthesized algorithm requires access to the complete gradient history through the dense matrix G1, incurring a memory footprint that grows with dτ and hence is impractical in high dimensions.
