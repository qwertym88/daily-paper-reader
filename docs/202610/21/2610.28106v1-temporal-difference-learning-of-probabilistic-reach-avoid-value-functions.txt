# Temporal Difference Learning of Probabilistic Reach-Avoid Value Functions 

Elias Fontanari 1, Pietro Noah Crestaz 1,2, Matteo Saveriano 1 and Andrea Del Prete 1

Abstract — Reach-avoid sets, defining the states from which a task can be completed while avoiding unsafe regions, are fundamental tools for deploying robots in environments subject to disturbances and uncertainty, as well as for runtime task-feasibility monitoring, allowing prediction of whether a state is safe and will lead to successful task completion. Classical Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability (HJR) and its recent neural extensions (e.g., Lipschitz Continuous Reachability Learning, DeepReach) treat disturbances adversarially, computing the reach-avoid set under worst-case disturbances from a bounded set. This guarantees safety but can yield overly conservative sets when disturbances are stochastic rather than adversarial, and additionally requires the disturbance set to be known or specified in advance. We propose Probabilistic reach-avoid Value Function (PVF), a self-supervised method, applicable to black-box systems, for computing reach-avoid sets as the states with a certain reach-avoid probability under stochastic, potentially unbounded disturbances. We evaluate our approach against HJR-based baselines on systems of increasing complexity, up to a Hopper locomotion task, and show that our probabilistic sets are less conservative and more accurate than adversarial HJR sets when disturbances are non-adversarial. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous robotic systems are increasingly deployed to perform complex tasks in environments subjected to different sources of noise and uncertainty. Determining, from a given state, whether a task of interest can be completed while satisfying critical safety constraints can often be framed in terms of the Reach-Avoid set : the set of states from which the system can reach a target region while avoiding an unsafe one. Avoid and Reach-Avoid sets are essential in runtime safety monitoring, as done in model predictive shielding [1]. Reach-avoid sets are also a useful tool for computing Control Invariant Sets (CIS) [2]: if the target set used to compute a reach-avoid set is itself control invariant, the resulting reach-avoid set is also control invariant, and typically larger. CISs are essential tools in safe control methods such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) [3], since constraining the terminal state of the associated optimal control problem to lie in a CIS ensures recursive feasibility and constraint satisfaction. Therefore, the accurate computation of reach-avoid sets is extremely useful for deploying robots in safety-critical settings. 

A. Related works 

Existing approaches to computing reach-avoid sets are predominantly grounded in Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability

This comes with two main drawbacks: i) these methods cannot be applied to disturbances with unbounded support, such as a Gaussian distribution, and ii) when the disturbance is random they tend to be extremely conservative. Classic HJR methods rely on grid-based dynamic programming over the state space, which scales exponentially with the system’s dimensionality, rendering them intractable for high-dimensional systems. For this reason, the computation of reach-avoid sets has been addressed mainly by two lines of work, alleviating the curse of dimensionality: physics-informed neural networks such as DeepReach [5], and reinforcement learning [6], [7]. DeepReach learns the reach-avoid set through a curriculum learning procedure that enforces the conditions of the as-sociated Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs partial differential equation (PDE). RL-based methods, instead, learn a critic that en-codes the safe set, replacing the PDE-based Hamilton-Jacobi formulation with a Bellman recursion that can be solved via RL algorithms. However, in the adversarial setting the disturbance policy must be learned jointly with the control policy, turning training into a min-max optimization problem that is notoriously unstable and slow to converge. In addi-tion, the theoretical guarantees typically rely on regularity assumptions on the dynamics, most commonly Lipschitz continuity, which may not hold for hybrid or contact-rich systems. DeepReach has been tested on large nonlinear systems such as quadrotors, but, unlike RL methods, it cannot be applied to non-differentiable dynamical systems, such as legged robots or black-box systems, since the dynamics is needed in the computation of the Hamiltonian. A separate line of work, stochastic reachability anal-ysis [8], [9], addresses reach-avoid problems directly as probabilities of success under a stochastic disturbance model, formulated via dynamic programming over the system’s tran-sition kernel in a measure-theoretic setting. While closer in spirit to our probabilistic formulation, these approaches rely on the numerical solution of the associated Bellman recursion over a gridded or otherwise explicitly represented state space, which precludes their use with the high-dimensional, black-box dynamics typical of robotic simulators. In another line of work [10] the authors used actor-critic RL to learn a safe policy, where the critic expresses a pseudo-probability of future failure. However, several aspects of this formulation limit its use as a set-computation method. B. Contribution 

We introduce PVF (Probabilistic Reach-Avoid Value 

Functions), addressing the typical drawbacks of HJR meth-ods in settings where there is no adversary, but simply non-adversarial stochastic noise, as is often the case in robotics scenarios, wind disturbances acting on an aerial vehicle being a representative example. By learning a value function that expresses, for each state, the probability of reaching the target set without ever entering the failure set, we are able to assess safe target reaching in a graded fashion: states that succeed under most disturbance realizations, and fail only under a highly unfavourable sequence of disturbances, are distinguished from states that fail under typical conditions. Crucially, the entire procedure treats the system as a black box: it requires only the ability to sample transitions from the environment, with no access to an analytical model, no differentiability of the dynamics, and no knowledge of the disturbance distribution. This makes the approach directly applicable to contact-rich and hybrid systems, such as legged robots, for which a closed-form model is either unavailable or non-differentiable, and where PDE-based methods cannot be applied. Fig. 1 provides an overview of the proposed method. Additionally, our value function provides interpretable probabilities, as opposed to those computed by HJR methods, which provide either the worst-case constraint violation the trajectory will incur, or the value of the target-set member-ship function at the time the target is reached. Summarizing, the contributions are the following: (i) a novel self-supervised method for computing probabilistic reach-avoid sets under stochastic disturbances with poten-tially unbounded support, without assuming worst-case be-havior; (ii) a comparison against HJR-based baselines on systems of increasing complexity, up to a Hopper locomotion task, showing that the resulting sets are more interpretable, less conservative and more accurate under non-adversarial disturbances. A. Problem formulation 

We consider stochastic discrete-time dynamical systems of this form: 

x′ = f (x, u, w ),x ∈ X ⊆ Rn, u ∈ U ⊆ Rm, w ∼ pW , (1) where x is the current state, x′ the state reached applying the control u, and the stochastic disturbance w, drawn from a possibly unbounded distribution pW , to the system. D. Comparison with related methods 

We compare our approach with two representative learning-based safety methods: Lipschitz Continuous Reach-ability Learning (LCRL) [7], which learns robust reach-avoid sets, and Safety Q-functions for Reinforcement Learning (SQRL) [10], which estimate the future failure risk of a safety-constrained policy. Both methods additionally synthe-size or adapt a controller. In contrast, our method focuses on estimating the reach-avoid probability of a given closed-loop system. We therefore restrict the following discussion to the value functions learned by the three approaches. LCRL belongs to the family of Hamilton–Jacobi reach-ability methods and adopts a robust formulation: its value function determines whether the target can be reached safely for every disturbance realization within a prespeci-fied bounded set. Our method instead models disturbances stochastically and estimates the probability of reaching the target before entering the constraint set. Consequently, the two value functions encode different notions of feasibil-ity. LCRL provides a worst-case, binary characterization through the sign of its value function, whereas our value function quantifies the likelihood of task success under the disturbance distribution. The latter can distinguish states having different success probabilities even when neither state admits a worst-case guarantee. Moreover, our formulation does not require a bounded disturbance set and therefore also accommodates distributions with unbounded support. To enable a direct empirical comparison, we consider bounded stochastic disturbances, for which LCRL can be applied by treating every disturbance in the support as adversarial. Such a comparison highlights the conservatism introduced when disturbance likelihoods are discarded. SQRL is closer to our approach because its safety critic is also defined through an expectation over stochastic transitions. Its objective, however, differs from reach-avoid feasibility. SQRL estimates, for each state–action pair, the discounted risk of eventually entering a failure set, while task completion is handled separately through the policy reward. It therefore does not include an explicit target set in the safety-critic formulation. This distinction is particularly relevant over long or in-finite horizons. If a system remains exposed to a nonzero failure risk indefinitely, its eventual failure probability may equal one over a large portion of the state space, providing little information about task feasibility. SQRL mitigates this issue through a finite horizon and a safety discount factor 

γsafe < 1, which emphasize near-term failures but cause the resulting critic to represent discounted risk rather than an undiscounted hitting probability. Our target set instead defines successful termination explicitly. Together with the constraint set, it supplies the two boundary conditions needed to distinguish successful and unsuccessful task completion. Under the standard Assumption 1, these boundary conditions uniquely determine the reach-avoid probability and provide terminal supervision for TD learning. IV. C ONCLUSIONS 

In this article we presented PVF, a novel method for com-puting probabilistic reach-avoid sets. We compared it against LCRL, a recent and promising HJ-reachability method for computing robust reach-avoid sets. Under random distur-bances, PVF achieved better classification accuracy when evaluated against a ground truth certified via Wilson con-fidence intervals at a high confidence level. Despite these encouraging results, the proposed approach still presents some limitations that would require further attention. Most importantly, our method currently assumes that a policy is available for controlling the system. We plan to extend our method to jointly learn the probabilistic value function and a policy to maximize the associated success probability. Another critical point is computational efficiency, which is currently a weakness of the method. In this respect we want to investigate ways to intensify training on state-space regions where the probability estimate is more uncertain. Finally, we plan to apply and validate our method on larger systems, such as quadruped robots navigating obstacle-rich environments, and investigate the use of PVF for shielding in safety critical applications.
