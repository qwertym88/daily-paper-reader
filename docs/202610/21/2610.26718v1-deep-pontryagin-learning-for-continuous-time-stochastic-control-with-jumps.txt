# Deep Pontryagin Learning for Continuous-Time Stochastic Control with Jumps 

Jean-Loup Dupret a, ∗, Patrick Cheridito a

> aDepartment of Mathematics and RiskLab, ETH Zurich, Rämistrasse 101, Zurich, 8092, Switzerland

Abstract 

We develop a deep Pontryagin learning framework for high-dimensional continuous-time stochastic control problems that naturally accommodates jump-diffusions. In contrast to direct policy optimization, which repeatedly differentiates the objective through the entire time-discretized dynamics, our method separates adjoint learning from policy improvement by explicitly incorporating state-sensitivity information from the Pontryagin stochastic maximum principle. For a fixed Markov control, we first compute pathwise adjoint labels by backpropagating the reward through time along simulated state trajectories, and project these labels onto the Markov state through a supervised regression step. The control is then updated by enforcing the pointwise Hamiltonian stationarity condition. We prove that the resulting projected adjoint process satisfies a discrete-time Pontryagin principle and converges, as the time step tends to zero, to the solution of the corresponding continuous-time adjoint backward stochastic differential equation. Numerical experiments illustrate the accuracy, stability, and scalability of the approach. 

Keywords: Stochastic optimal control, Pontryagin stochastic maximum principle, Policy improvement, Deep learning, Jump diffusions, Optimal investment-consumption 

1. Introduction 

Stochastic control problems are central in many areas of applied mathematics, finance, insurance, en-gineering and operations research. In continuous time, they are classically characterized either by the dynamic programming principle (Fleming and Soner, 2006), which leads to a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) partial differential equation (PDE), or by the Pontryagin stochastic maximum principle (PMP) (Peng, 1990), which leads to a system of forward–backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDE). While these characterizations are theoretically powerful, their numerical implementation remains challenging in high dimension. Grid-based methods for HJB equations (Bonnans and Zidani, 2003; Oberman, 2006; Beard et al., 1997; Boulbrachene and Haiour, 2001) suffer from the curse of dimensionality, and classical tree or regression-based BSDE methods (Ma et al., 2002; Gobet et al., 2005) often become prohibitively expensive in the PMP framework when the state dimension or the number of time steps is large. Recent progress in deep learning has opened new possibilities for solving high-dimensional stochastic control problems (Hu and Lauriere, 2023). A first class of methods is based on the HJB–PDE and approx-imates the value function globally, for instance through physics-informed neural networks (Raissi et al., 2017; Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2018; Duarte et al., 2024; Dupret and Hainaut, 2026), continuous-time Bellman updating (Cheridito et al., 2026; Dupret et al., 2026) or constrained Gaussian process regressions (Chen et al., 2021; Hainaut and Dupret, 2026). These methods are attractive because they can provide a  

> ∗Corresponding author, Email address: jeanloup.dupret@math.ethz.ch arXiv:2610.26718v1 [cs.LG] 19 Oct 2026

global approximation of the value function and the optimal feedback control over the entire space-time do-main. This is useful when one needs a reusable solution over many initial time-space conditions. However, when the goal is to solve the control problem for a fixed initial state or along the optimal state trajectory, computing a global space-time solution may be unnecessarily expensive. In contrast to global PDE-based approaches, another stream of literature adopts a simulation-based view-point. The controlled dynamics are discretized in time, and neural networks are trained on simulated state trajectories to approximate the feedback control, the value function, or related adjoint quantities (Han and E, 2016; Han et al., 2017, 2018; Han and Hu, 2021; Huré et al., 2021; Beck et al., 2021; Bachouch et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2022; van Staden et al., 2024; Alasseur et al., 2024). Particularly popular is the direct policy optimization (DPO) of Han and E (2016), which extends ideas from the earlier neural-network control literature for deterministic nonlinear systems (Psaltis et al., 1988; Hunt et al., 1992). In DPO, the control is parameterized directly by a neural network and trained by differentiating an empirical approximation of the objective functional. This avoids the explicit solution of the HJB equation and is therefore simple to implement. However, DPO has important limitations. Since the loss is defined through long simulated trajectories, gradients have to be propagated through the full time-discretized dynamics. This can lead to memory-intensive training, accumulation of discretization and approximation errors over time and van-ishing/exploding gradients on long time horizons (Bachouch et al., 2022). As a consequence, DPO and related simulation-based time-discretized methods may be unstable and may converge to poor local minima, especially in high-dimensional or strongly nonlinear problems. To avoid the aforementioned difficulty that a local change in the control affects the entire future state trajec-tory, adjoint sensitivity methods (Pontryagin et al., 1962; Peng, 1990) instead introduce auxiliary adjoint state that propagates the state sensitivities of the objective backwards in time. Once the adjoint is known, optimality reduces to a pointwise maximization of the Hamiltonian with respect to the control, along the state trajectory; see Theorem 2.2. In the PDE-constrained optimization literature (Plessix, 2006; Hinze et al., 2009), adjoint equations are widely used in a deterministic setting to compute gradients efficiently, since their computational cost scales linearly with the problem dimension and have low memory cost. Closely related ideas have also been used in scientific machine learning to improve the training of neural ODEs and neural PDE solvers (Chen et al., 2018; Mowlavi and Nabi, 2023; Nzoyem Ngueguin et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024; Hintermüller and Korolev, 2026). Recent work has extended these adjoint-based techniques to continuous-time stochastic portfolio optimization via backpropagation through time (Huh et al., 2025a,b). However, the resulting Pontryagin-Guided Direct Policy Optimization method remains computationally expensive because they rely on a preliminary DPO stage and require nested Monte Carlo simulations to estimate state-dependent adjoint quantities. Nevertheless, adjoint sensitivity methods demonstrate that incorporating state sensitivity information can substantially improve learning by providing first-order infor-mation about how future rewards depend on current states. In this paper, we propose a deep stochastic Pontryagin maximum principle (deep PMP) framework that avoids the nested simulation bottleneck of Huh et al. (2025a,b) and which contrasts with existing determin-istic Pontryagin-based methods (Jin et al., 2020; Böttcher et al., 2022). The idea is to compute pathwise adjoint labels by backpropagation through time (BPTT) of the objective along simulated state trajectories. For each simulated trajectory, differentiating the realized future payoff with respect to intermediate states yields noisy but cheap estimates of the discrete-time adjoint variables. These labels are then projected onto 1the Markov state through a supervised regression step, producing a neural approximation of the discrete-time adjoint process. The control network is subsequently trained by enforcing the pointwise Hamiltonian stationarity condition from the PMP. In this way, the method separates the learning of adjoint sensitivities from the learning of the control and avoids repeated backpropagation through the full empirical objective as in Han and E (2016); Ji et al. (2022); Alasseur et al. (2024). The proposed approach therefore combines several advantages. First, the control is updated through a pointwise Hamiltonian optimization, thereby avoiding the stability issues associated with end-to-end pol-icy training by backpropagation through the full time-discretized dynamics. Second, it incorporates the adjoint/sensitivity structure of the continuous-time stochastic control problem through PMP, improving convergence of the scheme. Third, the BPTT adjoint labels are obtained from a single set of simulated trajectories and do not require nested Monte Carlo simulations; only a supervised regression step is needed instead. Fourth, the proposed framework naturally accommodates controlled jump-diffusions, where the adjoint BSDE contains both Brownian and jump martingale components and for which deep-learning meth-ods remain comparatively scarce. Finally, we prove that the projected BPTT discrete adjoint process satisfy a discrete-time analogue of the PMP and converge, as time step tends to zero, to the continuous-time adjoint BSDE associated with the limiting controlled jump-diffusion.

Establishing strong convergence in Proposition 3.4 would require additional and restrictive grid-consistency assumptions that are generally difficult to verify in practice, see Huh et al. (2025b). More precisely, this requires that the terminal error converges strongly, E|pN − pT |2 → 0, and that the continuous-time adjoint admits a time-discrete recursion consistent with the discrete BPTT–PMP scheme, with accumulated truncation error converging to zero in L2. Moreover, the convergence result should be understood as a time-discretization result for the ideal projected adjoint process. It does not directly imply convergence of the fully trained neural-network algorithm, since the latter also involves statistical error, neural approximation error, and optimization error. A complete convergence theory for full neural jump-FBSDE solvers still remains largely open (Alasseur et al., 2024). From these theoretical results, we now introduce our numerical algorithm which relies on a continuous-time policy-improvement principle (Jacka and Mijatović, 2017). Starting from a current Markov feedback control αk, one first evaluates the corresponding adjoint quantities of the value function induced by αk,and then defines an improved policy αk+1 by maximizing the Hamiltonian pointwise with respect to the control. Our method can be viewed as an approximate, data-driven implementation of this principle. In the first step, the current adjoint field p∆ is estimated from the L2-projection (3.7). In the second step, the Hamiltonian maximization condition (2.7) is enforced through a pointwise stationarity loss. 94. Remark 3. The proposed Deep PMP algorithm differs from DPO methods (Han and E, 2016) in the way gradient information is generated and used. In DPO, the control network is trained by minimizing the empirical rollout cost bJ∆,(m) directly. Hence, every gradient update requires differentiating through the full time-discretized dynamics X0 → X1 → · · · → XN . With M simulated trajectories and N time steps, each update has a computational graph of depth N and memory requirements proportional to the full collection of simulated states and network activations. By contrast, Deep PMP separates adjoint estimation from control optimization, which has several practical advantages: (i) The pathwise adjoint targets  bp(m)

> i

Ni=0 are obtained from a single reverse-mode BPTT pass along each simulated trajectory. The Markov adjoint field is then learned through a standard supervised regression problem based on these targets, which is more stable than DPO on long-horizon objectives. (ii) The repeated control updates do not require backpropagation through the full time-discretized state dynamics. This reduces memory requirements, shortens the depth of the computational graph, and improves optimization stability when the number of time steps N is large, mitigating error accumulation as well as vanishing or exploding gradients over long horizons. (iii) Our method explicitly incorporates the PMP into the learning procedure through the conditional 

L2-projection in Proposition 3.1 and the discrete adjoint recursion in Proposition 3.3. This allows the neural networks to learn adjoint information that is consistent with the physical and variational structure of the controlled process, and to improve convergence and stability of the numerical scheme.

6. Conclusion 

This paper introduces a Deep Pontryagin learning framework for continuous-time stochastic control prob-lems with jump-diffusion dynamics. The method exploits the adjoint structure of the Pontryagin stochastic maximum principle while avoiding nested Monte Carlo estimation of value-function gradients. More precisely, the deep PMP algorithm is based on a two-step policy-improvement scheme. First, for a fixed feedback control, pathwise BPTT derivatives provide noisy adjoint labels, which are then projected onto the Markov state by supervised regression. The learned adjoint field is then used, together with the Markov representation (4.2)–(4.3), to update the control through a pointwise Hamiltonian stationarity loss. This separation between adjoint estimation and control improvement reduces the learning problem to su-pervised regression and pointwise Hamiltonian maximization. It therefore avoids repeated end-to-end backpropagation through the full time-discretized objective, which is a key limitation of DPO methods. On the theoretical side, we showed that the projected BPTT adjoint satisfies a discrete-time Pontryagin principle and converges, as the time step tends to zero, to the continuous-time adjoint BSDE. The numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed method accurately recovers reference controls and values across several jump-diffusion benchmarks, including high-dimensional and nonlinear examples. They also indicate improved stability and accuracy compared with direct policy optimization. Future work 25 includes extensions to path-dependent problems, state- or control-dependent jump intensities, and a more detailed analysis of the neural approximation error.
