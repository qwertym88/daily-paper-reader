## Abstract 

Although Graph Neural Networks are widely used in vari-ous domains, they remained mostly unexplored in computer vision. Convolutional Neural Networks and Vision Trans-formers were leading the field, until the quite recent in-troduction of Vision Graphs (ViG) that treat images as a set of nodes and apply graph convolution on their k-NN graphs. ViGs show impressive accuracy on image classifi-cation but their computational cost remains relatively high. Inspired by optimization methods for GNNs, we present CoViG, a novel approach combining graph coarsening and ViGs. Our method introduces an additional MinCUT pool-ing step within the Grapher component of intermediate ViG blocks. By simplifying the intermediate graphs and thus de-creasing the number of nodes of specific blocks, our ap-proach considerably reduces the computational cost of ViG during inference. We evaluated our approach on ImageNet with several ViG architectures, and we obtained accuracies equivalent to the original ViG models with only about 51% of FLOPs. 

## 1. Introduction 

Although Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vi-sion Transformer (ViTs) are the predominant neural net-work architectures of state-of-the-art computer vision mod-els, a new type of architecture called Vision Graphs (ViGs) has been introduced recently [14] showing excellent per-formances on the standard ImageNet image classification benchmark. Similar to ViT, ViGs divide the input image into non-overlapping patches, and then compute embed-dings that are processed further by the backbone. But, in-stead of the Transformer blocks that implicitly integrate the contextual information using attention, ViGs model these spatial relations and visual similarities more explicitly us-ing graphs encoded with a Graph Neural Network (GNN) based architecture. Although the costly attention mechanism is avoided in ViGs, computational complexity remains still relatively high due to the graph generation process and GCN at each

block of the model. Much effort has been spent in the last decade to increase the efficiency of CNNs and ViTs (and Transformers in general), both in terms of computation and memory. However, only a few recent works have specifi-cally tackled the reduction of ViG complexity [29–31]. The main computational bottleneck is the k-Nearest-Neighbor-based graph construction in each ViG block, where the sim-ilarity between each pair of patch embeddings needs to be computed, and a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) is applied on this graph. Our proposed method directly addresses this by in-troducing a graph coarsening approach at pre-determined stages (blocks) of the model. More specifically, we replace one or several of the Grapher (GN) components with, what we call a Coarsened Grapher that simplifies the graph into a reduced version with much fewer nodes. This process is represented in Figure 1. This reduction then propagates to all subsequent blocks of the model and has a direct impact on the computational efficiency. Our coarsening approach is based on the MinCUT Pooling algorithm [2] that learns the clustering jointly with the rest of the model and a task-specific loss, as opposed to other GNN or ViG optimisa-tion methods where this clustering is performed indepen-dently [5, 31]. Our contributions can be summarised as follows: 1arXiv:2610.21321v1 [cs.CV] 15 Oct 2026 • We propose a new Vision Graph-based architecture with reduced computational complexity thanks to an integrated and differentiable graph coarsening approach based on MinCUT Pooling. • By introducing a new Coarsened Grapher module, the proposed approach is flexible and adaptable to different (pre-trained) ViG models. Before training or fine-tuning, one simply has to choose one or several blocks that should be reduced and determine the desired compression rate for each coarsening step. • We extensively evaluated our approach on the ImageNet benchmark, with different coarsening configurations and rates, and with different ViG models, and we were able to reduce the FLOPs up to a factor of 2 without decreasing classification accuracy. 

## 2. Related Work 

This section provides a brief overview of the different archi-tectures used for image classification and the presentation of the compression methods that are applied to them. 

2.1. CNN and ViT 

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been the dominant paradigm in computer vision, driving remarkable advances in image classification [22], object detection [34], and semantic segmentation [37]. LeNet [24] established the fundamental principles of local receptive fields, weight sharing, and hierarchical feature extraction. It was followed by AlexNet [22], a breakthrough on the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC). This work paved the way for the well known VGGNet [38], ResNet [19], and MobileNet [21]. The generalized use of CNNs led to the need of com-pressing the networks to alleviate their memory footprint and computational complexity [4, 16]. Many methods have been developed such as Pruning [15, 39], Knowledge Dis-tillation [1, 20, 35], Early-Exit [32, 40], and Quantization [11, 13]. CNNs have long dominated the field of computer vision, primarily due to their ability to capture spatial hi-erarchies through convolutional layers compared to classic non-convolutional Deep Neural Networks (DNN). After the rise of attention in the field of NLP [42], Doso-vitsky et al. [6] have developed ViT for image recogni-tion. Compared to CNNs, ViTs capture global image con-text more effectively especially where long-range depen-dencies are crucial. However, it usually needs to be trained on very large-scale datasets. ViTs are also computationally expensive due to the self-attention mechanism, which scales quadratically with the number of image patches, leading to high memory and computation resource requirements. These computational costs have motivated the research of compression techniques for ViT. Some CNN compression techniques have been success-fully applied to ViT. DeiT [41] have applied knowledge dis-tillation to ViT by adding a specific token. Liu et al . [27] also achieved post-training quantization on ViT. Other methods also try to reduce the complexity of the self-attention mechanism, which scales quadratically with the patch number. For example, Wang et al . proposed pyra-mid architecture [43] to improve ViT performances while reducing the network cost. Liu et al . proposed the Swin Transformer [26], which has a linear computational com-plexity with respect to the image size. Finally, some compression methods leverage ViT-specific architecture. For example, Rao et al . introduce Dy-namicViT [33], to prune unnecessary tokens. Bolya et al .explore token merging in ToMe [3]. 

2.2. From GNN to Vision GNN 

In many applications, objects and relationships between ex-amples in a dataset can be represented as a graph. However, common machine learning algorithms rely on the assump-tion that instances are independent of each other. This is invalid for graph data. This leads to ever-increasing use of graph-based methods in different fields. Neural networks that operate on graphs (GNN) have been introduced by Gori 

et al . [12]. Convolution-like propagation in GNN was first introduced by Duvenaud et al . [7] and later improved to be-came the modern GCN by Micheli [28]. GCN have been used widely in computer vision applications and specific vi-sual tasks with naturally constructed graphs, such as point cloud classification and segmentation [23, 44], scene graph generation [45], and action recognition [46]. The idea behind Vision Graphs (ViG) [14] is to model the semantic spatial relationship of image parts (i.e. patches) together with their content in a graph. This approach intro-duces a GCN-based backbone that directly processes image data. It uses patches as nodes and builds a graph with the top first-order similarity as neighbors. This model outper-formed state-of-the-art CNN and ViT-based architectures on standard image classification benchmarks. They pro-posed two versions of their network: an isotropic version and a pyramid version (PViG). Inspired by this work, Mu-nir et al . have proposed an hybrid CNN-GNN architecture MobileViG [29] based on PViG, that uses Sparse Vision Graph Attention instead of the previously used k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) graph attention. Munir et al . also pro-posed GreedyViG [30] which uses Dynamic Axial Graph to replace k-NN graph construction on PViG. Parikh et al .have proposed ClusterViG [31], introducing Dynamic Effi-cient Graph Convolution. It reduces the computational ex-pense of image graph construction by partitioning the im-age patch feature grid into several partitions and performing graph construction in parallel within each partition. K-NN is performed locally within each partition in parallel, reduc-2(a) (b)

2.3. Graph compression 

Classic compression methods like pruning [51], quantiza-tion [50] or knowledge distillation [47] have been success-fully applied to GNN. Graphs can be quite complex and this has led to the exploration of graph condensation to replace wide graphs with smaller versions that are easier to manipu-late in term of computations [18]. There are three main cat-egories of graph condensations: graph sparsification, coars-ening and condensation. Graph sparsification can be seen as a form of pruning, it aims to selects existing nodes or edges and remove the rest of the graph. Graph coarsening is the surjective mapping of a graph that results in a smaller version of the graph made of ”super nodes” that aggregate original nodes. Finally, graph condensation is the creation of a small synthetic graph that aims to provide similar per-formances as the original graph. There are different graph coarsening techniques. We can cite DiffPool [48], that learns a soft assignment matrix S

via a full GNN pass, costing O(N 2) memory for the as-signment matrix. The Top-K method [10] select a subset of existing nodes and drops the rest, retaining the subgraph in-duced by selected nodes. This works for sparse graphs but in ViG’s dense k-NN setting it creates coverage gaps. SAG-Pool [25] is quite similar to Top-K but the score is computed differently using a GCN meaning that this method in con-trast to the other is graph-aware. Graclus [5] is a determin-istic graph algorithm that matches neighboring nodes. For each node, it selects the node that maximizes a criterion and matches those nodes together. This process can be applied iteratively to coarsen even more. 

## 3. Method 

In this section, we will first describe the basic ViG archi-tecture and the MinCUT Pooling algorithm. Then, we will introduce our proposed CoViG approach. 

3.1. Vision GNN 

Han et al . [14] proposed the first ViG; this method can be considered a replacement for the self-attention mechanism of a Vision Transformer, using graph message passing in-stead of attention. The image is first divided into visual nodes. Each node corresponds to the receptive field of one feature map location.

The network is composed of several blocks that repeat-edly follow the following path shown in Figs. 2a and 3. For each node, ViG computes the k nearest neighbors, produc-ing a graph. This graph is based on feature similarity, not spatial adjacency. It is then passed through a GCN, which gathers neighbour features, aggregates the information and updates the node representation. Each node is then pro-cessed by a feed-forward network (FFN). After the final block, global pooling is applied to all nodes, and the resulting feature vector is fed to a linear clas-sifier. It means that strongly con-nected nodes (nodes sharing similar features, close in fea-ture space) are encouraged to be merged together. This loss prevents degenerate solutions where all nodes collapse to the same cluster. However, when all nodes are assigned to the same cluster, the numerator and denominator are equal and Lc is minimized. To avoid such local minimum, Lo encourages the MLP to find clusters that are orthogonal between each other ( i.e .avoiding assigning all nodes to all clusters). Ensuring that each node is assigned to at least one super-node avoids any coverage gaps that can occur with Top-K or SAGPool. The fact that MinCUT performs soft assignment - con-trary to Top-K, SAGPool, Graclus, and ClusterViG - allows patches at boundaries between semantic regions to be as-signed to several super-nodes. It preserves boundary infor-mation that would be lost by hard methods. Comparing to DiffPool, MinCUT computes S via an MLP on node features costing O(N × K) memory for the assignment matrix. DiffPool also requires a separate GNN to compute S, it effectively doubles the graph computation at the coarsening block. For all these reasons, we have chosen to use MinCUT over other coarsening methods to coarsen ViG. 

3.3. CoViG architecture 

We will now describe how the CoViG architecture inte-grates MinCUT Pooling in ViG. While the original ViG keeps the same number of nodes through all blocks, CoViG adds a coarsened Grapher to re-place the original Grapher. This new Grapher reduces the number of nodes according to different parameters. Several coarsened Grapher can be placed along the model to reduce multiple time the nodes number. The position of the differ-ent pooling blocks is defined using two parameters: coarsen start ( Cstart ) is the index of the first block where coarsening is applied; coarsen step ( Cstep ) is the distance between two coarsening blocks. The coarsening strength (the number of coarsened nodes) is defined by two parameters: coarsening ratio ( Cr ) is the fraction of nodes kept after each coarsen-4ing blocks; node threshold ( NT ) is the minimum number of nodes. For example, using a ViG-Ti as base model (with 196 nodes and 12 blocks) if the coarsening start on the third block ( Cstart = 3) with coarsening applied each sixth blocks ( Cstep = 6 ), 50% of the nodes pruned each time (Cr = 0.5) and a minimum of 49 nodes ( NT = 49 ). The final model will apply coarsening on the third block (196 → 98 nodes) and the ninth block ( 98 → 49 nodes). MinCUT is applied to the graph using the existing k-NN graph and computing the soft assignment matrix S us-ing a trained MLP. The node features X is pooled using 

Xcoarse = XS . The graph convolutions are then applied to the newly created Xcoarse . To train the MinCUT Pooling, the auxiliary loss Lu is added to the cross-entropy:

## 4. Experiments 

This section provides details on the setup used to perform the experiments. 

Dataset: We choose to use ImageNet [36] a widely used image classification dataset, for our experiments. It con-tains 1.2M training images and 50K validation images, dis-tributed across 1000 classes. 

Experimental settings: Using the pre-trained models from the original paper as base, we trained the coarsened versions for 150 epochs using a batch size of 128. We kept most of the original parameters, the depth and dimension D remain the same for the 2 models. We keep a k k-NN in range 9 to 18, the graph convolution used is Max-Relative. We used a learning rate of 10 −4 with AdamW as an optimizer and cosine (with restarts) as learning rate scheduler. 

Metrics: For all experiments, we report top-1 accuracy as the primary evaluation metric (noted accuracy in the follow-ing section). Top-1 accuracy corresponds to the percentage of samples for which the model’s highest-confidence pre-diction matches the ground-truth class. The computational cost of a model is calculated using fvcore FlopCountAnalysis. This library provides a com-putational cost in MACs but since most works [14] used 1 FLOPs = 1 MACs, we have chosen to present our results in FLOPs with the same conversion ratio instead of the classic 2 FLOPs = 1 MACs. The model latency is measured using a batch size of 1. A warm-up of 20 images is used. Then, the latency is aver-aged over 200 images. The latency is calculated on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. 

Ablations: We tested several positions Cstart for the coars-ening block with a fixed coarsening ratio Cr and changed the coarsening ratio while keeping the coarsening block on the same position. We also explored the possibility to coarsen multiple times the same model with different gaps between two coarsenings and changing the coarsening rate. We also explore the impact of different ωu values in range 0 to 2. For all other experiments, we used ωu = 0 .1.

Hardware: Our experiments have been performed on 3 dif-ferent hardware settings. To train the CoViG-Ti, we used 8 NVIDIA A100 GPU. For the CoViG-s training, we used 16 NVIDIA A100 GPU on 4 nodes. Finally, we evaluated our models on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

Several observations emerge from these visualizations: First, the adjacency matrices of both configurations ex-hibit a clear block-diagonal structure, confirming that the MinCUT loss successfully encourages the pooling to pre-serve intra-cluster connectivity. Clusters are well-separated and super-nodes are connected primarily to other super-nodes within the same semantic group, as theoretically pre-scribed by the MinCUT objective. However, the adjacency matrices exhibit an important difference: at Block 0, the connectivity is strongly concentrated around the diagonal and a few nearby bands, whereas at Block 6 the connectivity is more dispersed across the graph, with substantially more off-diagonal structure. This indicates that deeper ViG rep-resentations contain richer non-local relationships between visual nodes. Second, the soft assignment matrices reveal a marked qualitative difference between the two configurations. At block 0, assignments are near-hard and high-contrast, with individual nodes assigning almost their entire probability mass to a single cluster with confidence values reaching 0.9. This behavior indicates that early-block features are spa-tially discriminative enough for the MLP to produce con-fident, near-deterministic cluster memberships. However, such near-hard assignments provide little benefit over hard partitioning methods and produce super-nodes that aggre-gate only a handful of original nodes, limiting the seman-tic richness of the coarsened representation. In contrast, at block 6, assignments are considerably softer and more dif-fuse, with probability mass distributed across multiple clus-ters and peak values limited to 0.35. This diffuse structure reflects the feature homogenization that occurs at deeper network layers. As node representations converge toward more uniform embeddings, the assignment MLP naturally distributes contributions across clusters, producing super-nodes that are genuine weighted aggregations of semanti-cally related patches rather than near-singleton partitions. The corresponding pooled adjacency at block 6 is denser and exhibits richer inter-cluster connectivity, indicating that the coarsened graph retains meaningful global structure for the downstream Grapher blocks to operate on. To conclude, coarsening at Block 6 can aggregate nodes after the network has established more meaningful global relationships, whereas coarsening at Block 0 operates on a less developed representation. This qualitative observation is consistent with the quantitative results: delaying coarsen-ing generally provides a better accuracy–efficiency trade-off than applying it at the beginning of the network. ## 6. Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce CoViG, a graph coarsening ap-proach to reduce the computational cost of isotropic Vision GNNs while preserving their classification performances. CoViG integrates MinCUT Pooling into the ViG architec-ture to progressively reduce the number of visual tokens processed by subsequent graph convolutional blocks. This reduces the computational cost of graph reasoning while keeping the original depth and feature dimensionality of the ViG architecture. Our experiments on ImageNet demonstrate that the ef-fectiveness of coarsening strongly depends on the coars-ened Grapher position in the network. Applying coarsening too early can discard spatial information before sufficiently high-level representations have been formed, whereas de-laying coarsening allows the network to perform graph rea-soning at full resolution before aggregating visual tokens. In particular, for ViG-Ti, CoViG can reduce the compu-tational cost by approximately half while maintaining the baseline accuracy, and a less aggressive configuration even improves the accuracy from 74.4% to 75.5% while reduc-ing FLOPs from 1.31G to 1.03G. These results indicate that graph coarsening can provide computational savings with-out necessarily requiring a proportional loss in recognition performance. We further showed that repeatedly coarsening the graph is less effective than applying a single coarsening opera-tion at an appropriate depth. Although multiple coarsen-ing stages can further reduce the number of visual nodes, they can progressively discard discriminative information and result in substantial accuracy degradation. Experiments with the larger ViG-S model confirm the generality of the approach, while also showing that larger models can be more sensitive to aggressive node reduction. Overall, these results demonstrate that visual tokens in graph-based vision architectures do not necessarily need to be processed at full resolution throughout the entire net-work. Carefully positioned graph coarsening provides a simple mechanism for controlling the computational cost of graph reasoning while preserving, and in some configu-rations improving, recognition accuracy. Future work could investigate adaptive coarsening strategies that determine the number of visual nodes dynamically based on the input or the semantic content of intermediate representations, as well as extending CoViG to other graph-based vision archi-tectures ( i.e . pyramid ViG). 7References
