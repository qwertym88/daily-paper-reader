# Reinforcement Learning via Advantage Guidance 

Zhiyuan Zhou, Sergey Levine 

UC Berkeley 

Abstract — Reinforcement learning (RL) holds the promise of learning a policy that can outperform what is shown in the data. However, in practice, such systems suffer from instability and hyperparameter sensitivity. In contrast, supervised learning provides a stable and scalable training objective, but is fundamentally limited by the quality of the demonstrated behavior. In this paper, we ask whether we can perform policy optimization with something as simple and stable as supervised learning but as performant as RL. Unfortunately, naïvely training the policy with supervised learning and conditioning on future rewards or advantages typically underperform dedicated RL methods. We introduce a new policy extraction method, Advantage Guidance , that retains the simple supervised learning structure with advantage conditioning while outperforming prior RL methods through effective conditional training and contrastive diffusion guidance during sampling. We show that Advantage Guidance can significantly improve a model’s ability to respond to conditional advantage signals, and the contrastive guidance during sampling can use the learned advantage-conditioned policy to target the KL-regularized optimal policy. Empirically, we find that Advantage Guidance significantly outperforms prior state-of-the-art RL methods on 26 long-horizon, sparse reward tasks across datasets with varying degrees of optimality. 1. Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) holds the promise of improving policy performance beyond what is shown in the data, and can in theory outperform imitation learning under suitable conditions [46, 30]. However, RL algorithms suffer from lots of instability and hyperparameter sensitivity issues in practice [49, 17, 22], especially over off-policy or offline data. Imitation learning, on the other hand, has been shown to be stable and scalable for policy training with modern generative modeling techniques arXiv:2610.19783v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 Reinforcement Learning via Advantage Guidance 

like diffusion and flow matching. This raises the question: can we perform policy training with something as simple and stable as imitation learning, but as performant as RL? 

To achieve the stability of imitation learning, one might be tempted to directly apply the supervised-learning loss function, training the policy to maximize the likelihood of actions seen in the data. Previous works have explored accomplishing this with conditional, filtered, or weighted supervised learning [13]. One set of methods, which we will refer to as advantage-conditioned methods [28, 2, 18, 42], resorts to training a policy conditioned on advantage values, which estimate how “good” actions are. During inference, the policy is queried with a high advantage to generate “good” actions. Unfortunately, such naïve advantage-conditioned policies typically underperform dedicated RL methods (see Figure 1). This is in part because advantage-conditioning does not generally result in a policy that always favors actions with higher advantages as RL policies do: the resulting policy is restricted to the queried advantage level, and this restricts the degree of policy improvement. Moreover, we find it difficult to train a policy to respond to advantage conditioning in the first place: the policy often cannot match the requested advantage (see Figure 1), and can ignore the conditioning altogether and revert to unconditional imitation. In this work, our goal is to train a policy with the simple and stable supervised learning objective conditional on continuous advantage estimates, while enabling the policy to generate actions that maximize advantage. To accomplish this, we first derive a diffusion guidance formulation we call 

contrastive guidance for sampling at test time, which can target actions that maximize the advantage under a KL behavior constraint. Then, to effectively train a flow-matching conditional policy that responds to the conditional values, we re-parameterize the conditional policy as a residual flow on top of a reference unconditional flow field. We also propose an action imagination scheme: we use “imagined actions” sampled from the unconditional policy, and their advantage estimates, to train the advantage-conditioned policy under a broader action distribution for each state. The resulting algorithm, Advantage Guidance , significantly improves the policy’s ability to respond to advantage inputs, and can target the KL-regularized optimal policy during sampling. Across 

26 long-horizon, sparse reward tasks with both play-style and demonstration datasets in the offline RL setting, Advantage Guidance outperforms prior strong RL algorithms. 

2. Related Work 

Offline RL and policy extraction. Offline RL aims to learn policies that outperform the behavior represented in a fixed dataset. Prior works focus on learning value functions that mitigate erroneous extrapolation [3, 19, 27, 29, 54], as well as policy extraction from that value function [5, 16, 20, 34, 39, 47, 53]. However, many prior policy extraction methods can be difficult to apply in practice due to instability and hyperparameter sensitivity. Another line of work, which closely relates to ours, circumvents this instability by converting the policy training objective to an imitation learning loss via advantage, reward, or return conditioning [28, 6, 13, 45], data filtering [6, 52, 9], and loss weighting [39, 41, 35]. We specifically study advantage conditioning, which avoids instability of loss-weighting, uses the full dataset for generalization, and has lower variance than return conditioning. Existing advantage conditioning methods condition on continuous values [28, 18, 42] or binarized labels [2, 48], and during inference query with a fixed high advantage value, which may fall outside the training distribution, or learn an extra model to choose a high in-support query [28, 18]. In contrast, our method uses a form of contrastive diffusion guidance to maximize advantage without risking out-of-support advantage queries or needing additional models. In addition, we find that previous methods struggle to respond to advantage inputs, and we introduce residual parameterization and action imagination training schemes to improve the policy’s responsiveness to advantages. 

Flow and diffusion policies for RL. Diffusion and flow matching have been widely studied for parameterizing the policy in RL [43, 11, 56, 8, 26, 7, 58]. Using these iterative denoising methods is challenging because leveraging the action gradient requires backpropagating through a multi-step

denoising process [51, 21, 12, 57, 50, 33], which can be expensive and unstable in practice [58, 38]. Previous works avoid this unstable backpropagation process by treating each denoising step as an action in the environment [4, 43], which blows up the problem horizon, or approximating the multi-step policy with single-step models [38, 7, 24], which suffers from approximation errors and limited expressivity. Another line of work avoids backpropagation entirely, instead directly guiding the iterative sampling process using critic gradient [58] or classifier-free guidance (CFG) [15, 14]. Like the latter, our approach avoids critic gradients, which are difficult to estimate for high-dimensional or partially noised actions. Most similar to our work is CFGRL [15], which applies CFG between an unconditional policy trained on the entire dataset and another “filtered policy” trained on the positive-advantage subset. Our approach instead trains on the entire dataset conditioned on continuous advantage values, and uses a form of contrastive guidance during sampling. Empirically, we find that CFGRL usually underperforms in practice because its binary filtering criterion discards information on action quality.

5. Advantage Alignment Problem of Naïve Conditioned Policies 

While sampling with CFG does not necessarily produce actions that maximize the advantage, we might imagine that conditioning on a sufficiently high target advantage can still yield a good policy that is an improvement over ˆπ(a | s). By corollary, advantage conditioned policies should also enjoy this benefit because they equate to the special case of CFG sampling with guidance weight w = 1 . For simplicity, we focus our analysis on this special case, analyzing advantage-conditioned policies to understand whether they can effectively adapt to conditioning and generate high-advantage actions. To measure how well a policy responds to the advantage signal, we define advantage alignment as the extent to which the achieved advantage of a policy action matches the advantage used to query the conditional policy ( i.e. , Aquery vs. A(s, π (· | s, A query )) . Intuitively, if a conditional policy responds properly to advantage inputs, it should be advantage aligned: actions generated conditioned on high advantage should have high advantages, and vice versa. 6.2. Residual Parameterization for Conditional Policy 

While contrastive guidance can target the KL-regularized optimal policy in theory, it makes an implicit assumption that the conditional policy is advantage-aligned with y+ and y−. Therefore, we focus next on training techniques that can improve advantage alignment. Recall that we found in Section 5 that naïve advantage conditioned policies often ignore advantage inputs and largely optimize the policy loss based on state and initial noise alone. In other words, the policy is minimizing the loss for the advantage-conditional policy by learning to minimize the unconditional loss. This motivates an approach that separates out the components that are not conditioned on advantage, and leaves the conditional policy to focus on the advantage input. In Advantage Guidance, we propose to parameterize the conditional policy as a residual flow vθ to the flow field of an unconditional reference policy 

vˆπ(a | s). Specifically, both policies are instantiated as flow-matching networks, where the model learns a velocity field conditioned on a partially noised action xt, along with the noise scale t, state s,and conditioning variable y. The residual parameterization in velocity space is:

Experimental Setup. We evaluate all methods in the offline RL setting with 20 tasks from OG-Bench [37] and 6 tasks from OCBench [36]. OGBench offers a suite of long-horizon challenging manipulation tasks, and OCBench is a recently proposed extension of OGBench that provides a more realistic action distribution meant to emulate the statistical properties of real-world human demon-strations. In particular, the two benchmarks differ substantially in their offline dataset composition. OGBench is a standard offline RL benchmark with play-style datasets generated by scripted behaviors pursuing random goals, often yielding highly suboptimal trajectories for the task objective. OCBench offers demonstration-based datasets, consisting of trajectories generated by near-expert policies with high success rates on the task objective. Compared to play-style datasets, these demonstrations cover narrower states and predominantly near-optimal actions, making it more challenging for offline RL because extrapolation errors are harder to avoid. However, such data more closely resembles real-world robotics datasets, and OCBench’s design to emulate human demonstration statistics makes it particularly relevant. We use cube-single-T{1-5} , cube-double-T{1-5} , scene-T{1-5} ,

Baselines. We compare Advantage Guidance against a representative set of strong flow-matching RL baselines, grouped into three categories: (1) actor critic methods train a parameterized actor to maximize the critic Q(s, a ) subject to behavioral constraints. FQL [38] distills a multi-step flow policy into a single-step policy to avoid backpropagation through time, while EDP [24] approximates the multi-step integration with a single large Euler step. (2) Supervised-learning-based methods 

formulate policy optimization as a conditional, filtered, or weighted supervised learning problem. 

CRR [52] trains a behavior cloning policy on the subset of data with positive advantage; FAWR is an extension of AWR [39] to flow policies by weighting the flow-matching loss with exponentiated advantages. RCP-R and RCP-A [28] train a policy conditioned on the return-to-go and advantage estimate, respectively. (3) Diffusion guidance methods steer the denoising process toward high-value actions at inference time. QGF [58] guides a behavior cloning policy using an approximation of the critic gradient ∇aQ(s, a ) at partially noised actions. CFGRL [15] applies CFG between a policy trained on the full dataset and a policy trained on the positive-advantage subset. Section A.6 provides a more detailed description of each method. To ensure fair comparison among all the policy extraction methods, we use the same value function for all. For simplicity, use the IQL [27] Q and V functions, and use A(s, a ) = Q(s, a ) − V (s).

8. Discussions 

We demonstrated in this paper that Advantage Guidance can outperform more complex RL methods with a simple supervised learning objective with advantage conditioning and contrastive diffusion guidance during inference. Our work serves as a step towards simple, stable, yet performant off-policy RL algorithms. There are several promising directions for future research. First, our implementation uses a straightforward advantage estimate. While we find this to be empirically effective, a natural next step is to explore whether more intricate advantage estimates improve policy performance, and how policy extraction can be robust in case of noisy advantage estimates. Second, our approach uses contrastive guidance to maximize advantage during inference time, but too large a guidance weight usually lead to a decline in performance. Developing techniques to stably utilize larger guidance weights can further improve the performance of Advantage Guidance. Finally, it would be interesting to explore Advantage Guidance in (offline-to-)online RL settings.

A.4. Does A More Expressive Architecture Help with Advantage Alignment? 

In this section, we investigate whether we can improve advantage alignment, and in turn policy performance, of naïve advantage conditioning by using a more expressive conditional policy architecture. Specifically, we experiment with using a transformer architecture to ingest the conditional advantage. We compare RCP-A, which uses a FiLM-like architecture that uses the advantage input to modulate the hidden states at every layer of the policy network, to a variant that instead uses a transformer to handle conditional inputs. We represent each action position in the H = 5 action chunk with a separate token. These action tokens, together with an advantage token, an observation token, and a flow-time token, are processed jointly through self-attention. Each contextualized action token is then projected directly to the velocity vector for its corresponding action position. We use four transformer blocks with model width 256, eight attention heads (32 dimensions per head), and feed-forward width 1024. The FiLM RCP-A actor has approximately 1.39 M parameters, while the transformer actor has approximately 

3.23 M parameters. We tune the learning rate smaller ( 1e − 4) for the transformer version to prevent overfitting. We plot the performance comparison between RCP-A and its transformer variant, as well as Advantage Guidance, in Figure 14. Figure 14 (left) shows that using a transformer is not significantly better in terms of advantage alignment: while the transformer policy output varies more when advantage input is changed, the policy gradient is still invariant to advantage inputs. In comparison, Advantage Guidance is much more responsive to different advantage inputs. Figure 14 (right) plots the policy performance on OGBench. Overall, using a transformer does not affect policy performance much, and even slightly underperforms the original RCP-A agent. This shows that there is no free lunch: we cannot expect to improve advantage alignment by simply swapping in a transformer policy backBFNe, and the residual parameterization and action imagination training schemes are critical.

Hyperparameter tunning. For each method, including Advantage Guidance and all baseline methods, we tune the task-specific hyperparameter per domain in the ranges specified in Table 4.
