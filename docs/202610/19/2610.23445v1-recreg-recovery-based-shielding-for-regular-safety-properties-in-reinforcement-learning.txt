## Abstract 

In many real-world scenarios we need to consider formal requirements, such as safety and constraint compliance, to deploy Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents. In this paper we introduce R EC REG , a safe RL framework capable of enforcing non-Markovian regular safety properties via recovery-based shielding. R EC REG is a unified approach that supports different levels of a priori knowledge and safety guarantees. The paper primarily focuses on building the theoretical foundations for R EC REG in the tabular setting, providing rigorous safety and convergence guarantees that are not present in related work. We provide an extensive set of empirical results using the MASA-Safe-RL library, and we outline an extension of REC REG to deep RL domains using SOTA model-based RL. Our empirical results demonstrate that R EC REG achieves efficient learning and matches the theory in this paper. 

## 1 Introduction 

Safe Reinforcement Learning (RL) is an active area of research focusing on the training of agents with RL while maintaining some standard of safe behaviour at deployment and possibly during training as well [5]. There exist many competing paradigms in safe RL, the most common being Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) [4], which frame safe RL as a constrained optimization problem, enforcing safety through cost functions and expected cumulative cost thresholds; although this approach is often inappropriate in highly safety-critical domains, as it only enforces safety in expectation and often lacks rigorous guarantees [53]. An alternative view comes from formal methods . Methods from this class often leverage some form of Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) [42] to express formal safety constraints and task objectives, enabling expressive and interpretable specifications [14, 25, 26, 40, 47, 53, 54]. Following this line of work, shielding has emerged as a powerful approach for enforcing LTL safety properties in RL [3]. Predicated on ideas like the Simplex Architecture [46], shielding works by monitoring the agent’s actions and overriding them with a safe alternative if they propose an action that could lead to a safety violation. Classical approaches to shielding leverage reactive synthesis to build correct-by-construction monitors from formal safety specification and a formal model of the environment called the safety abstraction [3, 10]. The safety abstraction is designed to encode the minimum amount of environment dynamics required to guarantee safety. As such, it is often built from a pessimistic view of the environment derived from prior knowledge, thus classical shielding approaches can only reasonably be applied to known environments or simple safety specifications. This fundamentally departs from the usual RL framework (unknown environment), but does provide strong safety guarantees by construction. More recent shielding approaches consider probabilistic safety constraints [24, 32]. While these are less pessimistic, they still require knowledge of the transition kernel, using probabilistic model

There has however been a shift to lift these assumptions, in particular, by leveraging model-based RL to estimate the unknown environment dynamics and verify actions via forward simulation in the latent space of a recurrent state space model [17, 18, 27, 57], with Gaussian Process dynamics models [19] or ensembles of probabilistic neural networks [20]. R EC REG 

addresses this same question: how can we obtain meaningful safety guarantees when the environment dynamics are unknown? . We summarize our contributions hereafter. 

Contributions. To bridge the gap between the strong empirical results achievable by data driven methods (e.g., CMDP, model-based RL) and formally guaranteed safety (classical shielding) we propose R EC REG , a unified framework for enforcing regular safety properties that spans multiple levels of a priori knowledge. In a similar fashion to Recovery RL [49], R EC REG learns a backup policy trained to maximize safety probability and action replacement (shielding) is implemented by identifying probabilistic safe sets (or “recovery zones”). A central contribution of this paper is in the development of explicit safety guarantees, convergence and optimality results which are missing in prior work [49] . In particular, we identify and formalize three complementary operation modes. In the E XACT mode, R EC REG assumes full knowledge of the transition kernel and guarantees safety from the beginning of training. In the M ODEL BASED mode, R EC REG estimates the transition kernel from observed data, then performs on-the-fly verification via finite-horizon reachability analysis, enabling principled shielding under partial model knowledge with statistical guarantees. In the MODEL FREE mode, R EC REG dispenses with explicit model learning altogether and instead directly estimates the safety critic, yielding a purely model-free shielding mechanism with convergence guarantees. We also use D REAMER V3 [23] to develop a deep RL variant of R EC REG that uses latent rollout models and safety critics to provide safety guarantees under correct model calibration. In the tabular setting, we validate that R EC REG matches the outlined theory and that comparable approaches cannot reliably enforce safety constraints while maximising task performance. In deep RL we also observe that R EC REG is able to reliably reduce safety violations while achieving good returns compared to similar approaches based on the D REAMER V3 architecture. Together these results establish R EC REG as a practical and theoretically-grounded approach to safe RL 

Related work. Existing methods for safe RL can roughly be dividing into two camps: ( A) methods that incorporate safety into the learning objective, encouraging safe behaviour as an outcome of learning [2, 6, 13, 25, 38, 45, 47, 48, 60], and ( B) methods that enforce safety via external mechanisms that directly modify the agent’s actions [3, 9, 20, 32, 35, 36, 49]. Methods from camp ( B) make up a large portion of “provably safe“ RL algorithms [37]. They can also be further divided into hard constraints [3] and probabilistic constraints [9, 24, 32]. Methods based on CMDPs are from camp (A) – they use a cost function, that often represents safety violations, in the learning objective to reduce the probability of unsafe behaviour. CMDPs themselves can be solved via linear programming in the tabular setting [4]. For more complex environments, methods based on Lagrange relaxations 

and dual-gradient descent [6, 13, 38, 45, 48], and local policy search with gradient projections [2, 60] are popular approaches. While CMDPs provide a convenient framework for learning, they have several limitations. In particular, safety constraints are only enforced in expectation, which do not guarantee that unsafe events will never occur. Furthermore, the discounting accumulated costs also poses issues with constraining limiting behaviour [53]. In contrast, our approach R EC REG fits neatly into camp ( B), enforcing regular safety properties (more expressive than LTL safety properties) with high probability. LTL constraints have been considered in RL for both task specification [14, 25, 26] and constraint specification [47, 53, 54]. LTL constraints are PAC learnable under specific conditions [53, 59], but optimality under LTL constraints can only be achieved subject to optimal discount factors and Lagrange multipliers [47, 54]. Earlier theoretical work assumed access to a perfect generative model of the environment which can be queried from any state, breaking standard RL assumptions [53]. Recent work on Automata Constrained Q-learning 

uses reward shaping and penalties to ensure the optimal policy is also safe at convergence although does not provide explicit safety guarantees during learning unlike our approach [40]. For RL with LTL safety constraints, Shielding can be used to enforce constraint compliance [3]. In partially observable settings (e.g., deep RL) safety can be guaranteed if the underlying transition structure is known [12]. R EC REG , by contrast, offers a unified framework for safety guarantees even when the transition dynamics or structure are truly unknown. Another related line of research is Reward Machines [29, 30]; these are closely related to our work as simple reward machines can encode reward functions for learning regular safety properties. While 2the Reward Machines literature is primarily focused on task specification, there are many key insights such as counterfactual experiences and reward shaping [41] that are used to speed up learning over 

Reward Machines , which can applied in our framework too.

Regular safety property. A linear-time property Psafe ⊆ Σω is a safety property if every violating trace has a finite bad prefix , i.e., a finite word that cannot be extended to any trace in Psafe . A safety property is regular if its set of bad prefixes forms a regular language. Equivalently, every regular safety property can be represented by a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) D = ( Q, Σ, ∆, Q0, F)

that accepts exactly its bad prefixes. Here Q is a finite set of states with initial state Q0 ∈ Q; Σ is a finite alphabet; ∆ : Q × Σ → Q is the deterministic transition function; and F ⊆ Q is the set of accepting states. A trace satisfies the safety property iff no finite prefix of the trace is accepted by 

D. It is well know that for any LTL safety property there is a regular language that contains its bad prefixes [7]. Therefore, R EC REG can be straightforwardly applied to LTL safety constraints.

## 3 REC REG : Recovery-based Shielding

construction; defining memoryless policies on an expanded state space that preserves optimality. We then continue by formalizing probabilistic safe sets and how the backup policy is trained, followed by details regarding how the task policy is trained including a safety-aware Bellman operator that guarantees convergence. We then detail each of the three operation modes and corresponding guarantees (safety, convergence, optimality), and finally we conclude this section by providing a high level overview of R EC REG applied to deep RL.

Probabilistic safe set. Even if a state s⊗ is not labelled “unsafe” it does not mean that it is operationally safe, since there could be some unavoidable high-probability of eventually reaching an “unsafe” state under the current policy. 3.2 REC REG with Deep RL 

While the tabular case provides a convenient framework for obtaining concrete guarantees, many interesting applications of RL consider continuous or high-dimensional environments. Results from the tabular setting still hold under the assumption of a suitably accurate dynamics (analogous to the conditions in Proposition 3) [17, 18]. To utilize R EC REG in these scenarios we adopt SOTA model-based RL, leveraging D REAMER V3’s Recurrent State Space Model (RSSM) for joint dynamics learning and policy optimization [21]. The RSSM is a sequential variational auto-encoder type model that learns latent states and transition dynamics from observations. The RSSM includes a recurrent model ht = fθ (ht−1, z t−1, a t−1), an encoder zt ∼ qθ (zt | ot, h t), a prior/transition predictor 

ˆzt ∼ pθ (ˆ zt | ht), an observation decoder ˆot ∼ pθ (ˆ ot | ht, z t), a reward head ˆrt ∼ pθ (ˆ rt | ht, z t), and a termination head ˆγt ∼ pθ (ˆ γt | ht, z t). Similar to approximate model-based shielding (AMBS) [17] we augment the RSSM with a cost head ˆct ∼ pθ (ˆ ct | ht, z t) to model the cost function introduced in (10). Since the RSSM summarizes history in (ht, z t) we can accurately learn the cost function in (10) by back-propagating its gradients through the RSSM to capture the temporal dependencies of the DFA without needing to build the product MDP or synchronize states. Both the task policy bπ and safe policy πsafe are implemented as neural networks, with inputs derived from the RSSM latent state.

## 4 Experimental Evaluation 

Tabular RL. We consider a suite of tabular environments provided by MASA-Safe-RL [16], spanning navigation and control tasks, e.g., grid worlds and Pacman. For complete environment descriptions we refer the reader to Appendix D. The environments chosen cover a range of safety properties, from simple invariants to more complex regular safety constraints specified via automata. The difficulty varies both in terms of state-space size and the complexity of the underlying safety specification. We compare R EC REG against several representative safe RL approaches. Logically-Constrained RL (LCRL) [25] incorporates LTL constraints via reward shaping and maximal end components. QL-λ

follows Lagrangian-based approaches to LTL-constrained RL, treating the penalty coefficient as a hyperparameter [47, 53, 54]. Safe Exploration Mask (SEM) [58] learns auxiliary value functions to estimate proximity to unsafe states and controllability, suppressing risky actions by renormalizing the Q function. The conceptually closest baseline is R ECOVERY RL [49], which learns a recovery policy to guide the agent back to safe regions although these are specified via safety critics with respect to the task policy, not the safe policy, leading to sub-optimal and overly conservative action replacement strategies. We also include three representative CMDP-based methods: PPO-L AG , TRPO-L AG [45] and CPO [2], adapted to the tabular setting, which enforce constraints in expectation via Lagrangian and trust-region updates respectively. The main results are presented in Fig. 1, with corresponding tabular summaries and full learning curves provided in Appendix C. Across environments, both modes of R EC REG consistently achieve the desired safety probability, matching the theoretical constraint in almost all cases. Deviations primarily occur in larger state spaces, where estimation or exploration challenges arise. In contrast, baseline methods exhibit more variable safety performance, reflecting their heuristic nature or reliance on asymptotic guarantees. Even when satisfying the constraint, this often comes at the cost of reduced returns. Overall, R EC REG provides a more reliable trade-off between safety and performance. Additional analyses, including runtime (across modes) and ablations (e.g., reward shaping [41] and counter factual experiences [30]), are in Appendix C.3.

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced R EC REG , a unified approach for enforcing high-probability satisfaction of regular safety properties in RL which, compared to the most closely related literature (e.g., Recovery RL [49]), provides formal safety and convergence guarantees, with optimality under step-wise constraints. The experimental results presented in this paper validate our theoretical claims, and provide further evidence that the separation of reward and safety objectives is an effective strategy towards safety-aware decision making. C.3 Ablation Studies 

We conduct a simple ablation study for Colour Bomb Gridworld V2 with Property 2 . The DFA for Property 2 has 22 states, meaning the product MDP has 22 times more states than the original MDP. In particular, we ablate the use of counter factual experiences [30] for training the task and safe policy. We also investigate the effect of two reward shaping mechanisms based on the idea of potential-based reward shaping [41]. First we provide a primer. 

Potential-based reward shaping. Potential-based reward shaping is an approach used to typi-cally deal with sparse or delayed reward environments. Potential-based reward shaping provides intermediate rewards of the following form, 

r′(s′, s, a ) = r(s′, s, a ) + γΦ( s′) − Φ( s) (33) where Φ : S → R is the potential function.Intuitively this provides the agent with a dense reward signal for moving to ‘more promising’ states with higher potential values. Note that by discounting the potential values with the original discount factor γ this keeps the set of optimal policies that maximize the original reward function unchanged [41]. Then if the potential function is useful in some sense, this allows us to learn optimal policies more quickly. 

Automated reward shaping. Automated reward shaping (RS) for reward machines (RM) was proposed in [30]. We adopt the same methodology here. The potential function Φ : S⊗ → R is defined over product state space S⊗, each automaton state q ∈ Q is assigned a potential value v(q).The potential value for each automaton state is then computed by running value iteration with a different discount factor γ′ < γ . The cost function for the safe policy πsafe is then redefined as, 

C′(s⊗, a, s ⊗′) = C(s⊗, a, s ⊗′) + γv (q′) − v(q) where s⊗ = ( s, q ) and s⊗′ = ( s′, q ′) (34) Intuitively the value function v : Q → R essentially assigns automaton states that are closer to the accepting state with higher values. This in turn penalized the agent for moving closer to the accepting state in the automaton. 

Cycle-based reward shaping. This approach is inspired by cycle experience replay [47] which was used for full LTL constraints. The approach is as follows, we first compute the length shortest path through the DFA D, from each automaton state q ∈ Q to the initial state Q0, e.g., via breadth-first search. Each automaton state q ∈ Q is assigned a value v(q) which corresponds to the length of the shortest path from q to Q0, which will be used for potential-based reward shaping. This provides an incentive to the agent to return to the initial state Q0 as quickly as possible (short cycles), while avoiding the accepting states of the DFA D, which are sink nodes, i.e. v(q) = ∞ ∀ q ∈ F . Again, the cost function is redefined as in (34). 

Results for R EC REG -M ODEL FREE . Figure 22 provides the learning curves for R EC REG -MODEL FREE with the two different potential-based reward shaping approaches. First we see that without counter-factual experiences (CFE) the algorithm struggles to converge quickly and 39 achieves a low safety probability due to slower convergence of the safe policy. Automated (Potential) reward shaping destabilizes learning slightly and the algorithm converges suboptimally, whereas cycle-based reward shaping improves learning stability and converges optimally. 

Figure 22: Reward Shaping for Colour Bomb Gridworld V2 with R EC REG -M ODEL FREE .

Results for QL-λ. Figure 23 provides the learning curves for QL-λ with the two different potential-based reward shaping approaches. Again we see that without counter-factual experiences (CFE) the algorithm struggles to converge quickly. However, in contrast to before both automated (Potential) and reward shaping fully destabilizes learning collapsing the policy to the safest possibly policy (no reward). Whereas cycle-based reward shaping improves both safety and task performance uniformly finding the right balance. This highlights the difficulty with tuning Lagrangian approaches like QL-λ, subtle shifts in the cost function and penalty magnitudes can break the balance between task performance and safety.
