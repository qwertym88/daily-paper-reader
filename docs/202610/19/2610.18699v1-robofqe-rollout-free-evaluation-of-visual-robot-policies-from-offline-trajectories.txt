# RoboFQE: Rollout-Free Evaluation of Visual Robot Policies from Offline Trajectories 

Yunfu Deng Sangwoo Shin Brahma S. Pavse Josiah P. Hanna 

Department of Computer Sciences University of Wisconsin–Madison 

yunfu.deng@wisc.edu sshin237@cs.wisc.edu pavse@wisc.edu jphanna@cs.wisc.edu 

Abstract: Estimating the performance of a robot’s control policy before real-world deployment typically relies on rollout-based approaches, which execute the policy in a simulator or in a learned world model. Both approaches are costly to con-struct: simulators require extensive domain expertise, while world models require large-scale training infrastructure and additionally suffer from prediction errors that compound over the evaluation horizon. To address these challenges, we leverage off-policy evaluation (OPE), a framework that estimates the return of a target policy from a fixed dataset of offline trajectories. OPE has been studied primarily on low-dimensional, state-based tasks, and its effectiveness for visuomotor robot control remains largely unexplored. To make OPE practical in this setting, we introduce RoboFQE, which fits a Q-function to offline trajectories, without ever rolling out the policy. RoboFQE leverages a pretrained vision foundation model to handle high-dimensional inputs and a learned terminal success detector to supply reward signals without labels. Through experiments on simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, we demonstrate that RoboFQE produces reliable return estimates, signifi-cantly outperforming world-model evaluators by a growing margin as the evaluation horizon increases. We further show that the pretrained vision foundation model is critical for RoboFQE on long-horizon tasks, while the learned success detector eliminates the need for ground-truth reward labels with minimal loss in accuracy. 

Keywords: Off-Policy Evaluation 

# 1 Introduction 

Generalist robot policies are advancing rapidly. Recent vision-language-action models, diffusion-based policies, and large-scale robot learning systems increasingly aim to operate across tasks, objects, scenes, and embodiments [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. This progress creates a fundamental but unresolved evaluation problem: how should such policies be evaluated? The most direct approach is repeated real-world testing. A policy can be executed across many tasks, initial states, objects, and environments, with enough repeated trials to obtain statistically meaningful success estimates. However, this gold-standard procedure is expensive and difficult to scale. Each trial may require scene setup, resets, failure recovery, hardware operation, and human supervision. Because these costs multiply with the number of candidate policies and development iterations, statistically meaningful evaluation becomes a bottleneck during policy development. Recent work has made substantial progress by scaling how evaluation rollouts are obtained. Auto-mated real-world evaluation systems reduce human labor by automating scene resets and success detection [8], and distributed evaluation protocols pool real-robot trials from multiple laboratories into unified policy rankings [9]. Simulator-based and real-to-sim methods evaluate policies in re-constructed environments, sometimes with statistical calibration to real-world outcomes [10, 11].

More recently, action-conditioned video world models have been used to simulate the evaluation environment, where generated policy rollouts can be scored automatically [12, 13, 14, 15]. These approaches share a common structure in which policy performance is estimated by producing new trajectories, whether in the real world, in a simulator, or in a learned model. However, as shown in Figure 1, each of these rollout-based paradigms imposes its own requirements in visual robot manipulation. Real-world rollouts require physical access and repeated interaction with the deployment environment under human supervision. Simulator-based evaluation requires environment reconstruction, system identification, or calibration, and remains affected by sim-to-real mismatch. World-model-based evaluation avoids hand-built simulators but relies on autoregressive trajectory generation, and prediction errors can compound over long manipulation sequences [16, 17]. These costs motivate evaluation methods that avoid rollouts of the evaluated policy entirely. Value-based off-policy evaluation (OPE) addresses this need by estimating the expected return of a target policy by iteratively fitting a value function to one-step Bellman targets constructed from offline transitions, recovering long-horizon returns without any additional environment interactions [18]. The offline data OPE requires is abundant in robotics: teleoperation demonstrations, policy rollouts, and open-source datasets accumulate as policies are trained. OPE reuses this data, whereas simulators and world models must be built before a policy can be evaluated. However, OPE has been studied primarily on low-dimensional, state-based benchmarks [19, 20], and its effectiveness in visual robot manipulation remains an open empirical question. In this paper, we address this question by introducing RoboFQE, a rollout-free OPE evaluator augmented with pretrained visual representations and learned reward annotation. Among different categories of OPE, RoboFQE builds on fitted Q-evaluation (FQE) [21, 22], a value-based OPE method that learns the target policy’s Q-function by regression. By avoiding both a dynamics model and estimation of the data-collection policy’s action distribution, FQE is simpler than other OPE methods and ranks among the most accurate estimators in prior empirical comparisons [19, 20]. To further adapt FQE to the visual robot domain, we introduce two design choices. First, a pretrained vision foundation model 2serves as the Q-function’s representation backbone, efficiently handling the high-dimensional visual observations that existing OPE benchmarks rarely cover. Second, a learned terminal success detector supplies reward annotations for trajectories without ground-truth labels. As offline robotics data often lacks reward signals or outcome labels, conflicting with the standard OPE assumption that rewards are given in the data, we introduce this learned success detector to bridge the gap. Our experiments on the LIBERO and CALVIN benchmarks [23, 24] show that RoboFQE achieves the lowest absolute return error across four manipulation task suites, particularly on long-horizon tasks where rollout-based world-model evaluators degrade. Specifically, RoboFQE reaches only 5.63% return error on the longest-horizon suite while the strongest world-model evaluator exhibits 54.4%, as autoregressive prediction errors compound over horizons. This result suggests that our rollout-free OPE evaluator will become increasingly relevant as foundation policies tackle longer and more complex task sequences. Beyond simulation, RoboFQE also achieves lower return-estimation error than world-model evaluators on a real robot, even with its success detector applied zero-shot. We summarize our contributions as follows: (i) To the best of our knowledge, we present the first systematic study of value-based OPE for visual robot manipulation, and introduce RoboFQE as a practical instantiation of FQE for this domain by incorporating a vision foundation model and a learned success detector into FQE. (ii) Through experiments on the LIBERO and CALVIN benchmarks and on real-world manipulation tasks, we show that RoboFQE achieves lower return-estimation error than rollout-based evaluators. 

# 2 Related Work: Evaluation Paradigms and Data Constraints 

Rollout-based robot policy evaluation. While direct real-world execution remains the gold stan-dard for evaluating robot policies, its cost has motivated alternative paradigms. One class of alter-natives replaces real-world execution with simulator-based proxies. Since their evaluation accuracy depends on how closely the simulator matches the real deployment environment, the central challenge lies in faithfully reconstructing real evaluation scenes within the physics simulator. Li et al. [10] address this challenge through system identification and texture-baked rendering, while Jain et al. [25] use 3D Gaussian splatting. To account for the inevitable mismatch between simulator and real-world outcomes, Badithela et al. [11] combine imperfect simulators with limited real-world data through a prediction-powered inference framework [26]. More recently, action-conditioned video world models have been proposed as potential evaluation protocols. The core idea is to roll out the target policy inside a generative video model rather than the real world or a hand-built physics simulator. For example, Quevedo et al. [12] train an autoregressive, diffusion-based video generation model on Open X-Embodiment real robot data and score rollouts with a vision-language model. Subsequent work scales this approach by leveraging foundation-model pretraining: Tseng et al. [13] initialize from the Cosmos-Predict model [27] and augment with policy rollouts, while Gemini robotics team [14] build on the frontier Veo [28] model with multi-view con-ditioning, validated across over 1,600 real-world evaluation episodes. Recent designs explicitly target consistency over long rollouts: Guo et al. [15] use pose-conditioned memory retrieval, and concurrent work proposes multi-view latent world models [29] and self-consistent video generation [30]. Although both paradigms offer promising alternatives to real-world hardware execution, the engineer-ing overhead and sim-to-real mismatch of simulation-based approaches and the compounding errors of world-model evaluators remain key bottlenecks for scalable policy evaluation. Our work instead reuses offline trajectories through value-based off-policy evaluation, avoiding both environment construction and trajectory generation. 

Offline policy evaluation. Off-policy evaluation (OPE) estimates the expected return of a target policy from a fixed dataset of offline trajectories, without new environment interactions. Existing methods broadly fall into three families. Importance-sampling and doubly robust estimators [31, 32] reweight offline trajectories by the target-to-behavior policy ratio, but require access to behavior policy action probabilities, which are rarely logged in robot datasets. Stationary distribution correction 

3methods [33, 34] avoid explicit behavior-policy access but require estimating density-correction ratios through minimax optimization over the joint state-action space, a step that has not been validated in high-dimensional visual observation spaces. Value-based estimators [21, 22] learn the target policy’s Q-function directly from offline transitions, requiring neither behavior-policy access nor distribution-ratio estimation. The DOPE benchmark [20] finds value-based approaches among the most accurate OPE estimators on continuous control tasks. Based on this observation, we adopt fitted Q-evaluation [22], a representative value-based estimator, as the base method for studying OPE in visual robot policy evaluation. Despite extensive study on low-dimensional benchmarks, OPE has seen limited adoption as a stan-dard evaluation tool in robotics. Irpan et al. [35] apply OPE to image-based robotic grasping tasks, observing that importance-sampling and learned-dynamics OPE both struggle in visual settings and proposing a classification-based metric for policy ranking. Voloshin et al. [19] extend OPE benchmarking to vision-based domains, but these environments consist of rendered Atari and grid-world tasks with less visual complexity than real manipulation scenes. Pavse and Hanna [36] and Pavse et al. [37] develop OPE-specific representation learning to stabilize value estimation under visual observations, but evaluate primarily in state-based domains. Our work bridges these gaps by augmenting classical FQE with modern pre-trained visual representations and VLM-based reward annotation, providing accurate value estimation beyond policy ranking while removing the standard OPE assumption of given rewards. The reward annotation can draw on learned robotic reward models that judge task success from observations, such as LRM [38] or Robometer [39]. 

# 3 Rollout-Free Evaluation from Offline Robot Trajectories 

This section formulates the problem of rollout-free policy evaluation from offline robot trajectories and introduces RoboFQE, the instantiation of FQE that we study in this work. 

3.1 Problem Setup 

We consider language-conditioned visual robotic manipulation tasks, where the agent receives image-based (or multimodal) observations, outputs continuous actions, and receives a sparse terminal reward indicating task success. The task is modeled as a finite-horizon partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) with latent state st ∈ S , observation ot ∈ O , action at ∈ A , natural-language task description l ∈ L , horizon H, discount factor γ ∈ (0 , 1] , and terminal success function 

Rl : S → { 0, 1}. The latent state is not directly observed; instead, the agent receives observations generated from the underlying state. In practice, robot policies condition on a task-specific subset of the available information at each timestep, which may include a single image, a fixed window of recent frames, proprioceptive state, multiple camera views, or multimodal combinations thereof. 3.4 Instantiation for Visual Robot Policies 

FQE cannot be applied to visual robot policies without resolving two issues: encoding high-dimensional observations, and obtaining rewards when success labels are absent. RoboFQE addresses both through its choice of visual representation and success detector. The action-value function processes image observations through DINOv3 [42], a self-supervised vision foundation model. Non-visual inputs (proprioceptive states, gripper status) and the continuous action are projected through learned embedding layers and concatenated with the visual features. The language instruc-tion is encoded via a pretrained text encoder and fused with the observation–action representation to produce the scalar action-value estimate Qθ (xt, a t, l ). When ground-truth terminal success labels are unavailable, terminal success is inferred using the Large Reward Model [LRM; 38], a vision-language model, used off the shelf or adapted on demonstration data, that produces a binary success judgment from the terminal observation and task instruction. The success detector labels D as a preprocessing step before FQE training begins. The pretrained representation supports value learning from high-dimensional observations, while the success detector supplies terminal success labels in place of ground-truth annotation. Together, these two adaptations make value-based OPE applicable to visuomotor policy evaluation without altering the FQE objective itself. 

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate RoboFQE on two benchmarks chosen for complementary properties. LIBERO [23] provides a comparison of how estimation error grows with horizon. CALVIN [24] adds a longer-horizon sequential regime in which value estimates correspond to the number of tasks completed. A real-world study on an SO-101 arm then tests RoboFQE with its success detector applied zero-shot. 

4.1 Setup Benchmark and data. The four LIBERO [23] suites are Spatial ( H=220 ), Object ( H=280 ), Goal (H=300 ), and Long ( H=520 ), each containing 10 language-conditioned manipulation tasks with sparse binary terminal reward. This progression in horizon length enables controlled comparison of how estimation accuracy degrades as the evaluation horizon grows. Terminal success labels for augmented rollouts are annotated by LRM [38] fine-tuned on the official LIBERO demonstration data, achieving 97.4% accuracy against simulator ground-truth on the augmented rollouts. The offline dataset D comprises 50 human demonstrations and 25 additional rollouts per task, totaling 750 trajectories per suite across 10 tasks (102k, 121k, 114k, and 232k transitions for Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, respectively). The additional rollouts are collected from partially trained behavioral cloning policies using the ResNet-T architecture and the official LIBERO training pipeline. On CALVIN [24], policies are trained on environments A–C and evaluated on the held-out environ-ment D, following the ABC →D split. All methods use the same 20 fixed task chains, each a sequence 6of five subtasks executed consecutively in D. The offline dataset is drawn from the A–C data, which, unlike the LIBERO demonstrations, contains both successful and failed trajectories; no additional rollout augmentation is performed. 

Target policies and ground truth. On LIBERO, the target policies use the ResNet-T architecture (ResNet encoder with transformer temporal decoder) provided by LIBERO [23], trained via behavioral cloning on the benchmark’s demonstration data. For absolute value estimation, a single policy is evaluated per suite across 5 seeds. For ranking, four policies from the same training run, checkpointed at varying stages of training, are compared per suite, producing a range of separated success rates. On CALVIN, the target policy is X-VLA [43], using the official checkpoint fine-tuned on the ABC split. Ground-truth returns are computed by Monte Carlo rollouts in the simulator: on LIBERO with 

γ = 0 .99 , averaging γTi−1 · Rl(sTi ) over 200 episodes per policy; on CALVIN with γ=1 , executing the policy over the same 20 fixed task sequences, each consisting of five subtasks to be performed sequentially in environment D. 

Baselines. SIMULATOR ROLLOUT executes the target policy directly in the LIBERO simulator, producing the empirical success rate (SR). WorldGym [12] rolls out the target policy in an action-conditioned video diffusion model and classifies terminal success via a VLM. Cosmos 2.5-AC adapts the pretrained Cosmos Video2World diffusion transformer into an action-conditional world model following Tseng et al. [13] and applies the same VLM-based success classification. Ctrl-World [15], evaluated on the four LIBERO suites, is a multi-view world model that maintains long-horizon consistency through pose-conditioned memory retrieval and uses the same success classification. All three world models generate rollouts chunk by chunk, conditioning each chunk on previously generated frames and the policy’s actions, and are post-trained on D to adapt to the LIBERO observations. RoboFQE uses a DINOv3 encoder fine-tuned on the offline dataset D via the FQE loss with LRM annotated terminal labels, instantiated as in Section 3.4. Value estimation accuracy is measured by the absolute relative error (ARE) and mean squared error (MSE) of the estimated discounted return, with lower values indicating more accurate estimates. Ranking accuracy is measured by the Pearson correlation r and the mean maximum rank violation (MMRV), with higher r and lower MMRV indicating more faithful rankings.

4.4 Real-World Evaluation 

Figure 2: Real-world setup: an SO-101 arm with a top camera and a wrist camera stacks colored blocks. The real-world study (Figure 2) uses an SO-101 arm with a top camera and a wrist camera on three block-stacking tasks, which share the same red, yellow, and blue blocks and differ only in the source and target block; Appendix A.2 gives the full setup. The offline dataset combines 150 teleoperated demon-strations, collected under five initial-position settings per task, with 100 rollouts of a behavioral cloning data-collection pol-icy. The target policies are SmolVLA [6], π0.5 [44], and a multitask behavioral cloning policy, none of which contributes trajectories to the offline dataset; over 100 robot episodes per task with γ=1 , their ground-truth success rates are 58.5%, 81.0%, and 33.0%, respectively. LRM is applied zero-shot and labels terminal success with 83.3% accuracy against manual labels, compared with 97.4% after fine-tuning on LIBERO. WorldGym and Cosmos 2.5-AC are post-trained on the same offline dataset. As Table 3 shows, RoboFQE achieves the lowest ARE and the highest correlation despite the noisier la-bels. Both world-model evaluators incur higher estimation error, and Cosmos 2.5-AC again combines moderate ranking accuracy with high estimation error, as on LIBERO. Real-world trajectories average approximately 500 steps, comparable to LIBERO-Long, where world-model evaluators degrade most. 

# 5 Conclusion and Limitations 

RoboFQE demonstrates that fitted Q-evaluation with a pretrained vision encoder produces more accurate return estimates than video world-model evaluators, especially as the manipulation horizon increases. By reporting absolute error on value estimation alongside ranking metrics, RoboFQE reveals that strong rank correlation does not imply calibrated estimates, a distinction that matters when deployment decisions depend on absolute performance thresholds. We note three limitations of our study. First, value-based OPE accuracy depends on the offline dataset’s coverage of state-action regions visited by the target policy; quantifying this dependence under visual observations remains an open question. Our study does not establish whether RoboFQE can reliably rank policies on tasks absent from the offline dataset, or how its generalization compares with that of large-scale pretrained world-model evaluators. Second, when ground-truth rewards are unavailable, errors in the learned success detector’s anno-tations can lead to inaccurate value estimates, and vision-language reward models are expected to degrade on out-of-distribution real-world scenes. Third, RoboFQE conditions the Q-function on the policy input rather than full history or a Markov state, which could lead to less accurate prediction in partially observable environments. 9Acknowledgments

A.1 Multimodal Policy Inputs 

The policies in the main experiments condition on visual observations and the language instruction. Pixel-space world-model evaluators instead generate image observations and cannot produce the proprioceptive channel that a proprioception-conditioned policy reads, so they cannot evaluate such a policy. This subsection evaluates RoboFQE on a policy that consumes proprioceptive state in addition to images across the four LIBERO suites. It is trained by behavioral cloning under the same pipeline as the vision-only policies. A single policy per suite is evaluated for value estimation, and three policies with separated success rates, taken at separate training stages, are compared for ranking. The Q-function follows Section 3.4: the proprioceptive state is projected through a learned embedding layer and concatenated with the DINOv3 visual features and the action embedding before language fusion. All FQE hyperparameters match the main LIBERO configuration, including γ=0 .99 and the target-network synchronization period, so no separate search is performed for this setting. This subsection details the real-world study reported in Section 4.4. The hardware is an SO-101 arm with two RGB cameras, a top camera and a wrist camera (Figure 2); the policies condition on the two camera views and the language instruction without proprioceptive input. Three tabletop tasks are considered, each stacking one colored block on another: red on blue, blue on yellow, and yellow on red. The three tasks share the same red, yellow, and blue blocks and differ only in the source and target block. Each task is instantiated under five initial-position settings, and 10 teleoperated demonstrations are collected per setting, yielding 150 demonstrations in total with an average length of approximately 500 steps. To broaden the state-action coverage of the offline dataset, 100 additional trajectories are collected by rolling out a behavioral cloning checkpoint that is not among the target policies; their terminal success labels are annotated by LRM [38]. The target policies are SmolVLA [6] and π0.5 [44], both fine-tuned on the demonstration data with LeRobot, together with a multitask behavioral cloning policy trained on the same data. Ground-truth returns are obtained by rolling out each policy on the robot for 100 episodes per task and recording task success manually, with the discount factor set to γ=1 so that the return of a policy equals its task success rate. The baselines are WorldGym [12] and Cosmos 2.5-AC, both post-trained on the real-world offline dataset and applied as in the LIBERO experiments. RoboFQE is instantiated as in Section 3.4, using the DINOv3 encoder fine-tuned on the offline dataset with LRM-annotated terminal labels. Results are averaged over three seeds and reported as mean ± standard deviation in Table 3; with only three seeds, bootstrapped confidence intervals as in the LIBERO tables are not reported. Unlike the LIBERO experiments, in which LRM is fine-tuned on the official demonstration data and achieves 97.4% accuracy, the real-world setting applies LRM without task-specific fine-tuning. On the 250 real-world trajectories (150 demonstrations and 100 policy rollouts), zero-shot LRM achieves 83.3% terminal annotation accuracy against manual labels. This lower accuracy increases the reward-inference error in the FQE training targets, making the real-world setting a stricter test of RoboFQE’s robustness to imperfect reward annotation. 

A.3 Training Data Composition 

The main experiments train a separate Q-function per LIBERO suite, using only the 750 trajectories collected for that suite. Because the four suites share a similar visual environment, trajectories from other suites provide additional state-action coverage in the same visual domain, although under different task instructions and success predicates. This subsection tests whether pooling off-task data from the remaining three suites into the FQE training set improves or degrades value estimation, and how this effect interacts with encoder initialization. Two data conditions are compared. In the on-task condition, FQE is trained on the 750 trajectories of the suite being evaluated, matching the main experiments. In the all-LIBERO condition, FQE is trained on the combined dataset from all four suites (3 000 trajectories, approximately 569k transitions). Each condition is run with both the DINOv3 encoder and the from-scratch ResNet-18 encoder, yielding a 

2 × 2 comparison. The language instruction identifies each task, so the Q-function can discriminate across tasks through its language-conditioned architecture. All other hyperparameters match the main LIBERO configuration. Figure 3 reports the results. With the DINOv3 encoder, pooling off-task data produces small ARE reductions on Spatial, Object, and Long (0.4–1.0 percentage points) but increases Goal’s ARE from 2.34% to 4.20%. A.4 Failure Modes of RoboFQE 

RoboFQE estimates a policy’s value from a fixed offline dataset, so the dataset determines which policies the estimator can evaluate accurately. This subsection characterizes two conditions under which RoboFQE produces unreliable estimates. The first condition arises from the composition of the offline dataset; the second arises from the relationship between the evaluated policy and the data-collection policy. Both conditions are demonstrated on the LIBERO suites used in the main experiments. 

Insufficient data coverage. Offline robot datasets are often built from teleoperated demonstrations, which are predominantly successful trajectories. When any data-driven evaluator is trained on such a dataset, it observes near-optimal behavior but few transitions that end in failure, limiting its ability to assess policies that fail. To measure this effect, RoboFQE is trained on the 50 human demonstrations per task alone and compared against the augmented dataset of the main experiments, which adds 25 rollouts per task from partially trained policies (Section 4.1). Table 5 reports value estimation accuracy under the two conditions. Across all four suites, demonstration-only training increases ARE by 5–10 × compared to the augmented dataset, with demonstration-only ARE highest on Long (30 .85% ) and Object ( 29 .05% ). Without failure transitions the Bellman targets at non-terminal steps are computed exclusively from high-value successor states, which biases the Q-function upward and prevents it from distinguishing between policies that succeed at different rates. Both world-model baselines also degrade under demonstration-only training, with ARE exceeding 100% on multiple suites, consistent with the finding that video world models trained on success-skewed data cannot render plausible failure outcomes without rollout augmentation [13]. Out-of-support policy actions. RoboFQE evaluates a policy by querying the Q-function on the actions that policy selects. When those actions resemble the behavior recorded in the offline dataset, the Q-function interpolates within its training support and the estimate is reliable. When the policy selects actions the dataset does not cover, the Q-function extrapolates and can assign values with no basis in observed outcomes. To demonstrate this failure mode, the ranking set from Appendix A.1 is augmented with a random policy whose true success rate is zero, and the ranking metrics are recomputed. Table 6 reports the result. The random policy’s actions are absent from the offline dataset, so the Q-function assigns it a positive value rather than a value near zero, placing it among the trained policies in estimated value and inverting pairwise orderings. The degradation is largest on Spatial: the Pearson correlation falls from +0 .93 to +0 .54 and the mean maximum rank violation rises from 0.02 to 0.27 . This extrapolation error reflects a structural property of value-based OPE: 17 the Q-function is trained under the offline data distribution, and its predictions carry no guarantee at state-action pairs at which that distribution assigns negligible density. This property bounds the intended use of RoboFQE: it produces reliable estimates for policies whose actions stay within the dataset’s coverage, such as checkpoints from a shared training pipeline, and not for policies whose behavior departs sharply from the recorded data. This dependence on the overlap between the offline data and the target policy is a known property of off-policy evaluation, in which estimation error grows as the behavior and target policies diverge [19, 20]. The trained checkpoints lie close to the diagonal across the full range of tasks, so their estimates remain accurate for policies whose actions are covered by the offline data. The random policy has a ground-truth return of zero on every task, yet the Q-function assigns it positive estimates that fall within or above the band of trained-checkpoint estimates: roughly 0.18 to 0.25 on Spatial, 0.36 to 0.55 on Object and Goal, and a similar pattern at the smaller value scale of Long. The spread across tasks is the central observation: the random policy’s actions are absent from the offline data, so the Q-function extrapolates without constraint and assigns a different value on each task rather than one consistent estimate.
