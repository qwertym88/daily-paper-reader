## ABSTRACT 

Traditional time-series classification methods often operate as black boxes, provid-ing a class prediction without indicating which time point or time interval within the series led to that prediction. To improve temporal interpretability, MILLET (Multiple Instance Learning with Temporal Explanation), which combines an In-ceptionTime encoder with Conjunctive Pooling, provides both classification and temporal explanations. However, MILLET contains approximately 424 K parame-ters, making it unsuitable for deployment on resource-constrained micro-controllers, and it achieves an overall time-series accuracy of 0.888 and an explanation score of 0.681 NDCG@ n. We therefore propose SEA-Net, a lightweight architecture using small multi-scale depthwise-separable convolutional TSC encoders and im-proved Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) pooling heads. Our SEA-Net bottleneck encoder with Top-k pooling achieves 0.931 accuracy and 0.765 NDCG@ n with only 41 K parameters. Another variant, using a gated encoder with Top-k pool-ing, achieves 0.955 accuracy and 0.750 NDCG@ n with 62 K parameters. These results demonstrate that lightweight architectures can maintain strong classification performance and temporal explanations while substantially reducing model size. The proposed variants address specific limitations of the pooling strategy used by MILLET, providing a practical direction for interpretable time-series classification on resource-constrained edge devices. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Time Series Classification (TSC) assigns a label y ∈ { 1, . . . , C } to a univariate time series X =(x1, x 2, . . . , x t), for example distinguishing a normal from an abnormal heartbeat recording, or a healthy from a faulty machine. Deep learning (DL) models solve this task well, and convolutional architectures in particular are its standard baselines. Accuracy alone is not enough in the applications that motivate this work, which impose three requirements at the same time . The first is accurate classification : the model must remain competitive with the strong convolutional baselines used in the TSC literature. The second is interpretable predictions : the model must show which time steps drove its decision, and this explanation must be part of the computation that produces the prediction itself, not a post-hoc approximation produced afterward by a separate tool. The third is a small parameter budget : the model must be small enough to be a realistic candidate for deployment on an edge device or a micro-controller unit (MCU), where memory is measured in hundreds of kilobytes. We build on MILLET (Early et al., 2024; Dietterich et al., 1997), which performs TSC as a Multiple Instance Learning problem. In MIL a label is attached to a bag (Ilse et al., 2018) of instances , not to each instance. The whole time series and each time step are treated as the bag and the instances respectively, and only the bag-level label y is observed. This is exactly the structure of time series classification: the series as a whole is labeled, but only a few time steps carry the evidence; the abnormal beat in a long recording, the spike in a traffic trace. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Deep learning for time series classification: In Wang et al. (2017), the “Fully Convolutional Network (FCN)” and the “Residual Network (ResNet)” are established as strong baselines for TSC. The InceptionTime (Ismail Fawaz et al., 2020) architecture extends this baseline by applying filters of several kernel sizes in parallel, capturing patterns of different durations. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) (Bai et al., 2018) have demonstrated their effectiveness for time-series analysis, and we take inspiration from them for our proposed model. TCNs use dilated convolutions , which enable the network to capture information over a wider temporal context. This enlarges the receptive field and allows the model to capture both short-term and long-term temporal dependencies without significantly increasing the number of model parameters. The comprehensive review by Ismail Fawaz et al. (2019) shows that convolutional architectures are an effective family of approaches for TSC. Despite their architectural differences, these models typically conclude with a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer, which removes the explicit temporal resolution of the learned representations. 

Interpretability and multiple instance learning: Interpretability methods can be broadly divided into post-hoc approaches and models that incorporate explanations directly into the prediction process. Post-hoc attribution methods, such as LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), and Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), explain a trained model after prediction, whereas Rudin (2019) advocates incorporating interpretability directly into the model architecture. For time series, two main directions follow this principle. The first direction uses shapelets , i.e., short representative subsequences whose presence can explain a prediction. VQShape (Wen et al., 2024) learns a codebook of representative shapes, while InterpGN (Wen et al., 2025) combines a shapelet expert with a deep expert through a confidence gate. The second direction, which we follow, is based on MIL (Dietterich et al., 1997). Attention-based MIL (Ilse et al., 2018) introduced learnable instance-level pooling, later extended by models such as DSMIL (Li et al., 2021) for histopathology. MILLET (Early et al., 2024) adapted this idea to TSC. 

Small architectures and deployment budgets: Depthwise separable convolutions (Chollet, 2017; Howard et al., 2017) and squeeze-and-expand bottlenecks (He et al., 2016; Iandola et al., 2016; Sandler et al., 2018) are standard techniques for reducing the parameter count and computational cost. For time series, ATCN (Baharani & Tabkhi, 2022) combines residual connections with separable convolutions for efficient inference, while the method in Risso et al. (2023) optimizes the dilation and channel structure of TCNs under edge-device constraints. However, these approaches focus on efficiency rather than interpretability: they produce a prediction without explicitly indicating which temporal regions contributed to it. For deployment on resource-constrained devices, MCUNet (Lin et al., 2020), MicroNets (Banbury et al., 2021b), and MLPerf Tiny (Banbury et al., 2021a) provide benchmarks and design principles based on constraints such as flash memory, SRAM, and computational cost. These works demonstrate how neural networks can be made suitable for micro-controller-class hardware, but they do not address the additional challenge of maintaining interpretability under such resource constraints.

The encoder module begins with a stem convolution layer , that extracts local temporal features from the input time series while simultaneously increasing its representation from a single input channel of a time point to d feature channels. ## 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND RESULTS 

Research questions and datasets: We ask whether the small model retains classification accuracy, retains a useful interpretation, and how much cost it actually removes; two further questions, RQ4 

what the bottleneck contributes and RQ5 what Top-k pooling contributes, are answered by the ablation of Section 4.1. We answer them on two datasets (Table 15). We use the same WebTraffic dataset as MILLET (Early et al., 2024): a synthetic set of 1,000 series of length 1,008 over 10 classes, split 500 /500 , in which each class signature is injected inside a known window (Figure 14). It is the only dataset with a ground truth per time point, hence the interpretation can be scored rather than merely inspected. For wider coverage we use the UCR archive (Dau et al., 2019), reporting the 85 -dataset subset used by MILLET so that the numbers are comparable; we also report the performance on the full 128 -dataset archive in Section E.5. Class signatures are not annotated there, so on UCR the interpretation can only be evaluated with AOPCR. We use the test accuracy for the classification, and AOPCR (Samek et al., 2017) together with 

NDCG@ n (Järvelin & Kekäläinen, 2002) for the interpretation; both metrics are defined in Sec-tion D.4. AOPCR progressively removes time points according to their estimated importance and accumulates the resulting decrease in the score of the predicted class. Computing AOPCR does not require ground truth, so we can report this metric on both datasets. AOPCR averages the drop in the predicted-class logit as the top-ranked time points are removed (Section D.4), and logits have no fixed scale: a model trained longer is more confident, so every removal drops the logit further, whether or not its ranking improved. ## 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Our trade-off depends on where the model runs: saving 10 .3× in parameters for 1.8 accuracy points on UCR buys nothing on a workstation, but for the micro-controller class of MCUNet (Lin et al., 2020) and MicroNets (Banbury et al., 2021b) a 41 K model is a candidate where a 424 K one is not, and the per-time-step interpretation is kept. The price is paid for the size and not the design, since widening the same encoder recovers the UCR gap (Section 4.1). Our costs are parameter counts, analytic FLOPs and GPU timings; nothing was quantized or run on a micro-controller (Table 22), so deployment fitness is argued from size rather than shown. Activation memory does not shrink, because the encoder preserves length: peak memory exceeds the baseline’s, and where 9Preprint SRAM rather than flash binds, streaming or strided variants are the next step. Interpretation is scored only on WebTraffic, which is also our design set, and its backup metric AOPCR is budget-sensitive (Section 4.1). Seeds are uneven: three models use three seeds, each ablation one, read against those spreads (Section 4). Training is slower, since GPU kernels favor dense over depthwise convolutions; the saving is at inference only. In summary, the cost of MIL lies in the encoder and not in the interpretability: our model keeps the per-time-step explanation and beats the equally-trained MILLET baseline on WebTraffic at a tenth of the parameters. Built-in interpretability is therefore not what makes these models large, and need not be given up to make them small. 10 Preprint

## A WHY MIL M AKES SEA-N ET INTERPRETABLE 

Section 1 and Section 3.1 treat time series classification as a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) problem (Dietterich et al., 1997; Early et al., 2024): the series Xi is the bag, each time point xji is an instance, and only the bag label Yi is observed. MIL has one main role in SEA-Net. The instance labels yji are never annotated, yet the model must still say which time points carry the evidence for its prediction. MIL recovers this from the bag label alone: the pooling head first gives every time point its own class evidence and only then aggregates it into one prediction, so the same per-time-point evidence that forms the prediction is also the interpretation ˆIi ∈ Rt×C . The interpretation is therefore part of the forward pass and not a post-hoc approximation. The main text states this view in words. This appendix adds two details: Figure 3 draws the full pipeline from the bag to the prediction and the interpretation, and Equation (11) writes out the embedded pooling that this view replaces, so that the order of its two steps can be compared with the order of our heads.

E.2 MODEL COST 

The main text states that our model is 10 .3× smaller and needs 11 .1× less computation than MILLET. Table 22 gives the full cost and separates what improved from what did not. At 32-bit precision the weights go from about 1.6 MB to 161 KB, which moves the model from above to well below the roughly 1 MB flash budget of the micro-controller class described by Lin et al. (2020) and Banbury et al. (2021b), and standard int8 quantization (Jacob et al., 2018) would reduce it to about 40 KB. In this sense, the model becomes a candidate for such a device. The other two columns do not improve. Latency improves by only 1.6× even though the FLOPs fall 11 .1×, because depthwise convolutions are limited by memory speed, and a GPU at batch 32 is the wrong tool for a claim about small hardware; the 269 K wide model is in fact the slowest row. Peak memory is 179 .0 MB against 112 .3 MB for the baseline, which is worse, and this comes from the design: the encoder keeps the length unchanged, so the activations have the shape (d, t ) at every stage, however few weights produce them, and Equation (3) reduces the weights, not the activations. On micro-controllers, the SRAM needed for the activations is the limit at least as often as the flash needed for the weights, so this is a real limitation of the design and not a measurement error (Section 5). Figure 16 draws the compute column of Table 22 as a function of the size of the problem. The left panel varies the length of the series at 10 classes, and the right panel varies the number of classes at length 463 , and the two dashed lines mark the mean length and the mean number of classes over the 

85 UCR datasets. The cost of both models grows linearly with the length and hardly depends on the number of classes, and our model needs about ten times fewer operations at every length. Table 23 completes the picture on the pooling side, by counting the real multiplications of each head as a function of t, C and d. No head adds a term that grows faster than those of Conjunctive 

pooling, so the saving of Table 22 comes from the encoder and is not lost again in the head. E.3 ABLATION STUDIES
