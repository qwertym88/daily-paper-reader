## Abstract 

One way to tackle reinforcement learning for continuous control is to discretise the action space and apply value-based Q-learning, where the resolution of that discretisation shapes the trade-off between exploration and exploitation. Coarse discretisations facilitate early exploration but limit final precision, while fine discretisations enable precise control but slow convergence. Growing Q-Networks (GQN) address this by growing the resolution uniformly from coarse to fine. We introduce Selective Growing Q-Networks (SGQN) , which keeps the decoupled Q-learning framework and replaces uniform growth with an adaptive action grid: bins are added next to bins whose estimated Q-value is high, and bins whose Q-value is low are pruned, with ε-greedy action selection. On eight tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite (DMControl) with 2 to 12 action dimensions, SGQN reaches the highest mean final return among the compared methods on four of them, is within 1.1% of the best method on three more, and is 3.4% below Bang-Bang MPO on the remaining one (Walker Run), while using on average 7.5 of the 9 available bins per dimension at the end of training. We then test whether the adaptive grid is responsible for these results, and find that it is not. In our runs, growth activates every candidate bin and behaves like uniform growth, and removing pruning changes the return by at most 1.1%. Within SGQN, two other design choices matter instead: starting from a coarse grid, since starting with all 9 bins produces individual seeds that collapse, and ε-greedy selection, since an annealed softmax lowers the return by 23% to 100%. SGQN is insensitive to its growth and pruning hyperparameters. SGQN also differs from the GQN and DecQN baselines in other respects, notably one gradient update per environment step instead of one per eight, and we did not control for these, so we do not attribute its advantage to a single component. We therefore present SGQN as a competitive value-based method and, above all, as an analysis of which ingredients of a growing action grid matter and which do not. 

Keywords: reinforcement learning, continuous control, action space discretisation, Q-learning, adaptive resolution, value decomposition 

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning for continuous control is commonly approached through actor-critic methods, which directly parameterise a policy over a continuous action space (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Haarnoja et al., 2018; Fujimoto et al., 2018; Schulman et al., 2017). While effective, these methods introduce substantial complexity: 1arXiv:2610.20463v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 heuristics for training stabilisation, increased compute requirements, and wide hyperparameter search spaces (Seyde et al., 2022). An alternative is action space discretisation, where Q-learning methods, which avoid an explicit policy parameterisation, are equipped with a suitable discretisation of the action space. Recent work has shown that this is possible. Seyde et al. (2021) showed that Bernoulli (bang-bang) policies are competitive on many locomotion tasks, drawing on Pontryagin’s maximum principle to explain why boundary actions are frequently near-optimal. Building on this, Seyde et al. (2022) demonstrated that Decoupled Q-Networks ( DecQN ), which decompose the Q-function across action dimensions and apply bang-bang two-bin discretisation, can match state-of-the-art actor-critic performance on standard benchmarks. Seyde et al. (2024) extended this line of work with Growing Q-Networks ( GQN ), which progressively refine a uniform discretisation from coarse to fine resolution over the course of training. Despite this progress, GQN activates new bins uniformly across the action range, regardless of which regions have been visited or which the learned Q-function considers promising. This motivates the question of whether the resolution of the grid should instead follow the learned value function. We propose Selective Growing Q-Networks (SGQN) , which is designed to make the active action grid follow the learned values through two mechanisms and uses ε-greedy action selection: 1. Value-guided selective growth. At each growth step, SGQN identifies bins whose running mean Q-value is close to the best bin of the same dimension and activates their neighbours. 2. Lazy pruning. At each pruning step, bins whose running mean Q-value lies below a quantile of the mature bins of that dimension are deactivated, subject to eligibility conditions that protect new bins, the boundary and the centre of the range, and a minimum number of active bins. SGQN retains full compatibility with the decoupled Q-learning framework of DecQN and GQN (Seyde et al., 2022; 2024), benefiting from its linear-time complexity in the number of action dimensions and bins, and uses standard off-policy training with prioritised experience replay, double Q-learning and twin critics. We evaluate SGQN on eight tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite (Tassa et al., 2018) with 2 to 12 action dimensions, against five baselines. SGQN reaches the highest mean final return on four of the eight tasks, is within 1.1% of the best method on three more, and trails Bang-Bang MPO by 3.4% on Walker Run. Because the central design claim is that resolution is placed where it matters, we also examine directly where SGQN places resolution (Section 5.3) and test each component in ablations (Section 5.4). We report what we find, including results that weaken the original motivation: the adaptive grid is not what drives the performance. 

Contributions. This paper makes the following contributions: 1. We propose SGQN, an adaptive-grid variant of decoupled Q-learning with selective growth, lazy pruning and ε-greedy selection, and evaluate it on eight DMControl tasks against five baselines. It has the highest mean return on four tasks and is close to the best method on three more. 2. We analyse the learned action grids: which bins are active over training, how the Q-values of active bins compare to those of inactive bins, and which bins are pruned. In our runs the growth rule activates all candidate bins and the retained and removed bins have nearly identical Q-values, while pruning is weakly but consistently biased towards low-Q bins. 3. We study the design through component ablations and a hyperparameter sensitivity study (Section 5.4). Within SGQN, the results attribute the performance to the coarse start and to ε-greedy selection, not to value-guided growth or pruning, and show that the growth and pruning hyperparameters can be varied widely without changing the return. 22 Related Work 

2.1 Value-Based Methods for Continuous Control 

Q-learning (Watkins & Dayan, 1992) and its deep extension DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) are natively suited to discrete action spaces. Extending them to continuous actions requires either approximating the argmax over a continuous domain or discretising the space. Gu et al. (2016) proposed Normalised Advantage Functions (NAF), which represent the Q-function in a form that admits closed-form continuous maximisation. Tavakoli et al. (2018) introduced action branching, treating each action dimension as an independent head of a shared network, enabling Q-learning in factored multi-dimensional spaces with linear rather than exponential complexity. Tang & Agrawal (2020) showed that naive uniform discretisation of continuous action spaces can match policy gradient methods on several tasks when combined with standard Q-learning improvements, challenging the assumption that continuous action representations are necessary. Building on this, Seyde et al. (2022) proposed 

DecQN , combining bang-bang (two-bin) discretisation with full value decomposition across dimensions. By framing each action dimension as a cooperative agent in a multi-agent RL formulation (Sunehag et al., 2018; Rashid et al., 2018), DecQN achieves linear-time complexity in the number of action dimensions d and bins 

n per dimension, and competitive performance with SAC and TD3 on standard benchmarks. 

2.2 Adaptive Action Space Resolution 

The trade-off between exploration efficiency and precision is well documented in continuous control. Coarse discretisations reduce coordination complexity and facilitate exploration (Seyde et al., 2022), while fine discretisations enable precise steady-state behaviour. Several approaches have aimed to get the best of both worlds. Seyde et al. (2024) proposed GQN , which grows the action space resolution from coarse to fine based on a stagnation criterion: growth is triggered when the moving-average return falls below a threshold 

Gthreshold ,t = (1 − 0.05 sign( μMA )) μMA + 0 .90 σMA , where μMA and σMA are the moving-average mean and standard deviation of recent returns. Growth follows a predefined sequence (e.g., 2 → 3 → 5 → 9 bins), activating new bins uniformly and warm-starting their Q-values through the shared network trunk. Seo et al. (2024) proposed Coarse-to-Fine Q-Networks (CQN), which perform hierarchical refinement within each action selection : at each of L levels, the agent selects a bin from a B-bin discretisation of the current interval, then zooms into that bin for the next level. CQN achieves resolution BL with only L × B Q-value evaluations, but errors compound across levels and early mistakes cannot be corrected. Our work differs from both GQN and CQN. Relative to GQN , SGQN replaces uniform growth with a value-guided expansion rule and adds lazy pruning, with the aim of concentrating resources on promising regions rather than distributing them uniformly. Section 5.3 examines to what extent this happens in practice. Relative to CQN, SGQN maintains a persistent global discretisation that is refined over training, rather than performing per-step hierarchical refinement. 

2.3 Adaptive Discretisation in Other Contexts 

Adaptive discretisation has a long history in function approximation and Monte Carlo methods. In state space, adaptive tile coding (Sutton & Barto, 2018) allocates resolution where the value function varies most rapidly. In tree search, progressive widening (Coulom, 2007) grows the branching factor adaptively. In control, curriculum learning approaches (Czarnecki et al., 2018) start with easier task formulations and progressively increase difficulty. SGQN applies this philosophy specifically to the action space discretisation 

in deep Q-learning. 

2.4 Bang-Bang and Boundary-Seeking Policies 

Seyde et al. (2021) investigated the empirical observation that trained continuous control agents frequently saturate at action boundaries, drawing theoretical support from optimal control theory: Pontryagin’s maximum 3principle implies that minimum-time controllers are bang-bang. This motivates the use of binary (Bernoulli) action discretisation, which they show achieves competitive performance on locomotion tasks despite its extreme coarseness. SGQN begins from this insight and is designed to let resolution grow selectively where the Q-function indicates that finer discrimination is valuable.

3.3 Growing Q-Networks 

GQN (Seyde et al., 2024) maintains a binary active mask m ∈ { 0, 1}d×N over a full grid of N bins per dimension, initially activating only n0 ≪ N uniformly-spaced bins. Growth is triggered by a stagnation criterion and advances the active set through a predefined sequence n0 → n1 → · · · → N . All newly activated bins are warm-started via the shared network trunk, exploiting the implicit structure learned at coarser resolution. At each step, Q-values are computed and actions selected only over the currently active bins.

4.2 Action Selection 

With probability ε, each dimension independently selects an active bin uniformly at random. Otherwise it selects arg max j:mij =1 Qi(s, b ij ). The exploration rate starts at ε = 0 .1 and is multiplied by 0 .995 after every episode down to 0 .01. An earlier version of SGQN used a temperature-annealed softmax over active bins instead. We replaced it by ε-greedy selection, which gave more stable learning curves on tasks such as Fish Swim and Hopper Hop in our runs, and we keep it as an ablation (Section 5.4).

4.5 Training 

SGQN uses the same off-policy training infrastructure as DecQN and GQN . A prioritised experience replay buffer (Schaul et al., 2016) samples transitions with probability proportional to their TD error magnitude, with a constant importance-sampling exponent β = 0 .4 (full details in Appendix A). SGQN performs one 5gradient update per environment step. The Q-network uses the decoupled architecture of DecQN : a shared trunk with layer normalisation (Ba et al., 2016) feeds per-dimension Q-value heads, each outputting N values, one per bin, and SGQN uses twin critics of this form. Only the outputs of active bins are used for selection and for the maximisation in the target. n-step returns with n = 3 provide temporal smoothing of the TD target, and gradient clipping with norm 40 stabilises training. The full procedure is given in Algorithm 1. 

4.6 Computational Complexity 

Like GQN and DecQN , SGQN maintains O(d × N ) Q-values in the network output layer. The growth and pruning operations scan the mask in O(d × N ) time per check, which is negligible relative to the neural network forward pass.

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate SGQN on eight tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite (Tassa et al., 2018) and compare it against five baselines. Every run uses 1M environment steps, and we target 7 random seeds per method and task. We report the mean episode return and the standard deviation across seeds. A few baseline runs produced no data and are excluded; the affected table entries are marked and listed with Table 2. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Tasks. We select tasks covering a range of action dimensionalities and control difficulties: Reacher Easy 

and Reacher Hard (2 action dimensions), Hopper Hop (4), Fish Swim (5), Cheetah Run , Walker Walk and Walker Run (6), and Quadruped Walk (12). Hopper Hop and Walker Run are hard exploration and locomotion tasks on which several baselines fail. 

Baselines. 

• Bang-Bang PPO and MPO : Bernoulli policies (Seyde et al., 2021) trained with Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) and Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimisation (MPO) (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), using binary {− 1, +1 } discretisation. • DecQN : Fixed 3-bin (bang-off-bang) decoupled Q-learning (Seyde et al., 2022), the setting of that paper for state-based tasks. 6• GQN : Uniform adaptive growth from 2 to 9 bins (Seyde et al., 2024). • Coarse-to-Fine Q-Networks (CQN) : Hierarchical coarse-to-fine Q-learning, 3 levels × 5 bins (Seo et al., 2024), with the configuration of the official implementation, trained for 1M environment frames (500k agent steps with an action repeat of 2). CQN learns from pixels (84 ×84 images, 3 stacked frames), whereas all other methods observe the proprioceptive state, so its returns are not a like-for-like comparison with theirs. The CQN paper also reports results for state-based tasks, which we did not run, so this comparison contains no state-based CQN. • SGQN without pruning (earlier implementation) : an earlier version of our method without pruning, with different default hyperparameters and softmax selection. It is not the ablation of Section 5.4, which changes one component of the current method at a time. 

Hyperparameters. Each baseline uses the hyperparameters stated in its original publication. Where the publication does not state a value, or no official configuration exists, the baseline uses the default of our code, which Table 1 marks with C. SGQN uses layer normalisation, ELU activations, two hidden layers of 512 units, Adam optimiser with learning rate 10 −4, discount γ = 0 .99, replay buffer size 10 6, batch size 256, 3-step returns, target network hard update every 100 gradient updates, prioritised replay with α = 0 .6 and a constant β = 0 .4 (Appendix A). SGQN-specific hyperparameters are listed in Table 6. They were set by a small manual search on the tasks and are examined in Section 5.4. 

Differences between SGQN and the baselines. Table 1 lists the components of SGQN, GQN , DecQN 

and CQN as implemented in our code and, where the original papers state them, as specified there. Three points matter for reading the results. First, SGQN performs one gradient update per environment step, whereas our GQN and DecQN runs perform one per eight (a sample-to-insert ratio of 32 with batch size 256 in the code of both baselines; the DecQN and GQN papers do not state this ratio, and we could not check the official DecQN code). Pruning is weakly value-guided. Across the 56 runs there were 903 pruning events. None removed an anchor bin, as intended. ## 6 Discussion 

What the adaptive grid does. The original motivation was that a decoupled Q-function has a peaked landscape around the optimal action, so that selective growth should concentrate new bins near the peak. Our analysis does not show this. At the thresholds we use, the Q-values of the nine bins of a dimension differ by about one percent, which is too little for the growth rule to discriminate between them, and growth acts as uniform growth. Within SGQN, the ablation of Section 5.4 shows that starting coarse and ε-greedy selection matter, while value-guided growth and pruning do not change the return. This does not explain the advantage over the baselines, because GQN and DecQN also start coarse and use ε-greedy selection, and because SGQN differs from them in update frequency and other settings (Table 1). The warm start of new bins, the update frequency and the network were not ablated, and a fixed coarse grid without growth is missing, so we cannot say whether growth itself contributes beyond starting coarse. A possible reason for the flat Q-values is that the Q-value of a bin is evaluated for a state-action pair that is averaged over states, so that differences between bins are small compared to differences between states. 

How SGQN relates to the baselines. SGQN has the highest mean return on four of eight tasks and is within 1.1% of the best method on three more, but it is not uniformly best: Bang-Bang MPO is better on both Walker tasks, and GQN is nominally ahead on the two Reacher tasks by margins within the seed noise. Because the adaptive grid is not what drives the result, the comparison supports the claim that SGQN as configured is competitive with the strongest baselines here, and not the claim that adaptive discretisation is 15 better than uniform growth. Because SGQN also differs from the baselines in update frequency and other settings, it does not show which of its components is responsible. 

The role of lazy pruning. Pruning removes bins with lower Q-values than a random choice would, but the preference is moderate (Section 5.3). The eligibility conditions mean that a bin which the policy repeatedly declines becomes prunable after Lmax pruning events even without many selections, so that flagged bins are not held back for long. Removing pruning changes the return by at most 1.1% (Section 5.4), so pruning reduces the size of the grid without a measurable effect on the return. 

Why ε-greedy instead of softmax. An earlier version of SGQN used a temperature-annealed softmax over the Q-values of the active bins. With Q-values that differ by about one percent across bins, a softmax over them is close to uniform unless the temperature is very small, and the schedule of the temperature then determines the behaviour. ε-greedy has a single, interpretable exploration parameter, and in the ablation of Section 5.4 the softmax variant reaches 23% to 100% lower returns and does not learn on Hopper Hop. The earlier implementation, which used softmax with other defaults, did learn on Walker Run, so the softmax schedule and the other hyperparameters interact, and we did not tune softmax separately. 

Limitations. SGQN introduces hyperparameters compared to GQN (τQ, τp, ∆ g , ∆ p, vmin , Lmax and the regrow cooldown), and our defaults were chosen on the same tasks that we report. The final grid size depends on where in the growth and pruning cycle training stops, so we report the average over the last growth interval. The grid schedule is the same for all tasks and seeds in our runs, which means SGQN does not currently adapt the resolution to the task. The ablations use 4 tasks with 5 seeds and the sensitivity study 2 tasks with 3 seeds, so small differences between variants are not resolved. The independence assumption of decoupled Q-learning (Eq. 4) can also be limiting for tasks with strongly coupled action dimensions. We report training returns over the final 100 episodes rather than separate evaluation episodes. The baseline comparison has further limits. CQN learns from pixels and the other methods from the state, so it is not a like-for-like baseline. DecQN and CQN were run with the settings stated in their publications and the defaults of our code where these are not stated, were not tuned, and reach much lower returns than the other methods, which we did not investigate. Some baseline runs produced no data (Table 2), including all seven Bang-Bang PPO runs on Reacher Hard. We ran no significance tests. SGQN performs one gradient update per environment step and our GQN and DecQN runs one per eight, so part of the advantage of SGQN may come from more training per sample. We did not run GQN with the update frequency, exploration rule or network of SGQN, so the advantage over GQN cannot be attributed to a specific design choice. 

Future directions. Several extensions are promising: (1) a growth rule that uses a measure of Q-value differences that is not dominated by their mean, for example the advantage of a bin over the dimension mean in units of its standard deviation, so that growth can actually discriminate between bins, (2) learning per-dimension growth schedules, (3) combining selective discretisation with model-based planning, and (4) applying SGQN to real robotic systems where sample efficiency and discrete actuation align naturally. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced Selective Growing Q-Networks (SGQN), a value-based continuous control algorithm with an adaptive action grid and ε-greedy selection with a decaying exploration rate. SGQN reaches the highest mean final return on four of eight DeepMind Control Suite tasks, is within 1.1% of the best method on three more, and is 3.4% below Bang-Bang MPO on Walker Run, using on average 7.5 of 9 available bins per dimension. A direct analysis of the grid and a set of ablations show that this performance does not come from the adaptive grid: growth is effectively uniform, the Q-values of retained and removed bins differ by about one percent, and removing pruning changes the return by at most 1.1%. What matters within SGQN in our experiments is starting from a coarse grid, which prevents occasional collapsed seeds, and ε-greedy selection, which an annealed softmax does not match. The growth and pruning hyperparameters can be varied widely without changing the return. We therefore read SGQN as a competitive decoupled Q-learner whose advantage over the baselines we cannot attribute to a single component, since it also differs from them in update frequency 16 and other settings, and a growth rule using value differences between bins, which our rule does not exploit, remains an open design problem.
