Abstract 

Generalist robot policies offer strong behavioral priors, but achieving deployment-level reliability and throughput remains difficult. Reinforcement learning (RL) can improve these policies, yet its effectiveness is constrained by the cost of online interaction. Existing methods adapt frozen flow-matching policies by treating noise space as a continuous action space. We show that this behaviorally unstructured space makes exploration inefficient, often preventing these methods from extracting meaningful learning signals on challenging tasks. We introduce AnchorQ , which enables coarse-to-fine policy improvement through a structured, refinable latent action representation. At each decision step, AnchorQ uses flow reversal to map reference behaviors to state-conditioned latent anchors, samples local particles around those anchors, and applies value-guided sampling to explore promising refinements. Our analysis shows that anchors reduce identification complexity at the cost of support bias; particle refinement trades this bias against action-class complexity; and critic accuracy determines the reliability of KL-regularized, value-guided selection. Across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, AnchorQ outperforms state-of-the-art post-training baselines in sample efficiency, delivering a 37% improvement in real-world success rate and 

97% higher simulated task throughput under the same interaction budget. Additional visualizations are available on our website 1.

# 1 Introduction 

Generalist vision-language-action (VLA) models provide a powerful starting point for robotic manipulation. Through extensive pretraining, these policies can follow language instructions and transfer to objects, scenes, and tasks beyond the training set (Black et al., 2025a,b; Kim et al., 2025; TRI LBM Team et al., 2025). Yet deployment demands more than broad competence: robots must achieve high success rates and throughput in their target environments, while adaptation itself must remain inexpensive. Pretrained VLAs remain imperfect under changes in geometry, clutter, object placement, and task execution, while collecting demon-strations that densely cover these variations is costly. Reinforcement-learning (RL) post-training offers a natural route to deployment-specific improvement, but fine-tuning a large VLA is computationally expen-sive and can degrade useful pretrained behaviors. An appealing alternative is therefore to keep the generalist policy frozen and learn how to select and refine its actions. Prior methods adapts pretrained VLA policies by learning lightweight action corrections, steering latent inputs, or selecting among candidate actions (Nakamoto et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2026; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). Flow-matching policies provide an additional adaptation interface through the noise that initializes action generation. For a fixed state s and numerical sampler, an initial latent z is

The latent can therefore serve directly as the action for RL: changing how z is selected changes the executed behavior without modifying the VLA. For example, diffusion steering via reinforcement learning (DSRL) learns directly in the latent noise space of a frozen policy (Wagenmaker et al., 2025). This raises a more fundamental question: what action representation should be used for RL to adapt pretrained VLA policies? At one extreme, a handful of behaviorally meaningful actions, such as moving left or right, can make online learning much easier, but only by restricting the behaviors the policy can express. At the other extreme, existing approaches (Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025) search expressive but behaviorally unstructured continuous action or latent spaces. The learner must discover which action corrections or latent vectors produce useful behaviors in each state, without an explicit set of behavioral alternatives to guide this search. This makes efficient exploration extremely difficult: as we demonstrate in our experiments, DSRL and action-space residual RL baselines fail to learn effective policies on challenging tasks within the interaction budget. What is missing is an online action representation that is both structured and refinable : compact when coarse behaviors suffice, but able to expose richer behavior when needed. To address this gap, we construct a structured and refinable action representation around state-conditioned noise-space anchors, which we generate at every decision by applying flow reversal (Tang et al., 2026) to a small library of semantically meaningful reference actions. Building on this representation, we introduce 

AnchorQ , a coarse-to-fine RL method for adapting frozen flow-matching policies directly in their latent noise space. AnchorQ first samples local particles around each anchor and applies exponential value tilting to bias sampling toward high-value particles. It then selects an anchor and samples a refined noise latent from its tilted particle distribution, which the frozen flow policy decodes into an action. We train the critic 

Q using temporal-difference (TD) learning from online experience to guide both particle tilting and anchor selection. 

Contributions. The main contributions are summarized as follows: • A structured and refinable action representation for online RL. We propose a new online action representation and a coarse-to-fine RL method that turn continuous-space exploration into selection and refinement of behaviorally meaningful actions. • Guarantees for coarse-to-fine latent search. We show that the flow-reversed noise anchors reduces identification complexity (Theorem 1), while particle refinement trades support bias against the complexity of the local action class (Theorem 2). We further show that value tilting reliably amplifies high-value refinements under critic error (Theorem 3), while epistemic uncertainty adaptation calibrates exploitation to critic confidence (Theorem 9, Appendix I). 2• Consistent empirical gains in simulation and on real robots. Across three benchmarks in LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023a) and RoboTwin (Chen et al., 2025), AnchorQ outperforms the state-of-the-art steering baselines, which include continuous noise space RL and residual-based RL, with 97% higher throughput than the closest baseline. We also deploy AnchorQ to solve three challenging real robot manipulation tasks, where it outperforms both the frozen base policy and the RL baseline under the same interaction budget. 

# 2 Related Work 

The most relevant related work is presented here; Appendix A is a broader review. 

RL and steering in frozen generative policies. Several methods adapt frozen policies via their latent noise space. Value-guided methods rerank or steer sampled action chunks (Nakamoto et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025), while DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., 2025) treats the noise itself as a continuous action and learns a policy over the full latent space. FRS (Tang et al., 2026) instead inverts the frozen flow to recover latents corresponding to reference motions, using this primitive for zero-shot steering and to initialize continuous noise-space RL. UniSteer (Lu et al., 2026) also combines flow inversion with noise-space RL, yet uses online human corrections and learns a continuous latent actor. On the other hand, AnchorQ uses inversion to map a fixed library of coarse reference behaviors into latent anchors that are refined locally with continuous particles, and ranked by a critic. 

Reduced and coarse-to-fine action spaces. Reducing the effective action space can substantially sim-plify RL, through discretization (Tang and Agrawal, 2020), learned primitives (Lee et al., 2024; Shafiullah et al., 2022), or hierarchical refinement (Seo and Abbeel, 2024; Seo et al., 2024). These methods typically construct abstractions directly in the action coordinates. In contrast, AnchorQ maps a finite set of coarse reference actions to state-conditioned latent anchors through flow inversion, then refines these anchors locally using sampled continuous perturbations. 

# 3 AnchorQ : Value Learning over Online Flow-Reversed Latents 

AnchorQ adapts a frozen flow-matching VLA by learning to select actions in its latent noise space. At each decision, it flow-reverses a small library of coarse reference behaviors into state-dependent latent anchors, samples local refinements around those anchors, and uses a learned critic to select a latent for execution through the frozen VLA. We first introduce the flow-reversed anchors, then describe local value-tilted refinement, and finally present action selection and critic training. Figure 1 and Algorithm 1 summarize the method.

Sample Efficiency: Online noise anchors reduce identification complexity. To isolate the statisti-cal effect of restricting the online action class, we consider a one-decision MDP with N behaviorally distinct latent modes. Flow reversal exposes a subset of C < N modes, whereas an unrestricted learner may select any of the N .

The upper bound shows that when the C noise anchors contain a near-optimal action, searching only among them is sufficient to identify an ε-optimal action. For the lower bound construction, each non-flow-reversed action is suboptimal in the base instance but optimal in a corresponding alternative instance. ## 4.2 Simulation Performance and Ablation Studies 

We compare performance and sample efficiency on our simulation benchmarks and examine how particle tilting parameters, anchor composition, and the choice of Q function affect learning. 

AnchorQ Outperforms Steering Baselines in Simulation. As shown in Table 1 and Figure 2, An-chorQ outperforms other steering baselines by a wide margin. Especially on the LIBERO-90 15-task suite, it outperforms the closest baseline (DSRL) by at least 2× the success rate at any matched trajectory budget (over 250 − 2200 trajectories). We attribute this huge improvement to AnchorQ ’s efficient exploration thanks to its effective anchors and the particles that elicit meaningful exploratory behaviors. Similarly, on the hard-10 suite, AnchorQ also excels at exploring and learning the right task-solving behavior, even if the base policy performance is close to 0. Finally, on the RoboTwin bimanual suite, we demonstrate that AnchorQ can effectively learn dexterous behaviors that require the coordination of both arms, unaf-fected by the complex action space induced by the additional embodiment, whereas other baselines seem to struggle to beat the base policy’s performance on these tasks. AnchorQ achieves an average throughput of 294 completions/hr across all simulation tasks, compared to DSRL’s 149 , corresponding to 97% higher throughput.

σ versus fixed values; the composition of the anchor set; and learning Q in the continuous noise space versus over the discrete set of anchor options using RAINBOW (Hessel et al., 2018) (Rainbow-FRS). ## 4.3 Analysis on anchors and particle tilting. 

To illustrate how particle tilting helps with dexterous manipulation, we focus on task 81 , which requires precise placement of a book into the front compartment of a caddy and overcoming the base policy’s tendency to drop it into the wrong compartment. On this task, AnchorQ achieves an 84% success rate, the anchor-only ablation gets 22% , and DSRL gets 0% . Figure 4 (a) measures behavior in robot space: the realized end-effector motion already deviates from the commanded anchor axis for both anchor-based arms, and tilting deviates markedly more, giving it the freedom to express fine, off-axis motions that the six discrete anchors cannot. Figure 4 (b) shows why this steering is reachable at all. The noise anchors sit at an intermediate magnitude, between the near-zero noise DSRL collapses to and π0.5 prior, and AnchorQ spreads its executed noise across this anchor band, tilting to refine directions that the discrete anchors alone cannot reach. DSRL is a continuous noise-space actor regularized toward the π0.5 prior; however, it collapses to a near-zero noise vector and reproduces the base policy’s failure. AnchorQ refines within the anchor-supported region and explores beyond the discrete anchors, achieving the highest success rate. 

## 4.4 Real World Steering Experiments 

Setup and training. We evaluate AnchorQ on three household manipulation tasks: stacking cups, toasting bread, and hanging a towel. We use a Franka arm with a DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) setup, with observations from a third-person camera and a wrist camera. All methods steer the same frozen DROID pretrained π0.5 policy. We compare AnchorQ against the base policy without steering and DSRL. We give both RL methods the same interaction budget of 100 online episodes per task. A human operator observes each rollout, provides a reward at the end of the episode, and resets the scene to one of a predefined set of initial configurations. These training configurations are separate from the configurations used for evaluation. RL training runs asynchronously with data collection, updating the steering parameters while keeping the base policy frozen. 9Figure 5: Real world steering results. AnchorQ Outperforms Steering Baselines on a Real Robot. We evaluate each method on 20 held out configurations per task and report individual and average task success rates, with 95% Wilson score confidence intervals (Wilson, 1927) for the average. We show statistical significance using two sided t-tests comparing AnchorQ with each baseline, testing the null hypothesis of equal mean success. As shown in Figure 5, AnchorQ outperforms both π0.5 and DSRL on all three tasks under the same online interaction budget. On the towel hanging task, it is the only method that improves over the base policy. These results demonstrate that the our approach can significantly improve manipulation performance on a real robot under limited environment interaction. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduce AnchorQ , a coarse-to-fine RL method that adapts frozen flow-matching policies through a structured and refinable action representation. Flow reversal maps reference behaviors into state-conditioned latent anchors, while critic-guided selection and local particle tilting enable fine-grained refinement. Our analysis shows how anchoring reduces identification complexity, how refinement trades support bias against action-class complexity, and when value tilting remains reliable under critic error. Across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, AnchorQ improves sample efficiency over post-training baselines in chal-lenging manipulation tasks across simulation and real world benchmarks. These findings highlight action representation as a key design choice for efficient adaptation of frozen policies. 

Future work. AnchorQ requires additional memory from scoring particles at every decision. Distilling the adapted behavior into a policy could reduce the need for our sampling-based inference. Extending the current reference anchors to more complex embodiments and moving from task-specific to multitask post-training are also exciting future directions. 10 References

Generalist and generative robot policies. Policies trained on heterogeneous robot data generalize across scenes and instructions, from action-token transformers (Brohan et al., 2023; Zitkovich et al., 2023) to open checkpoints (Ghosh et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025). The current generation couples a vision-language backbone to a flow-matching action expert (Black et al., 2025a,b; Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025), inheriting a generative toolkit that includes action chunking (Zhao et al., 2023), diffusion policies (Chi et al., 2024), and flow matching with rectified flow (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b). We keep such a decoder frozen throughout and improve its behavior only through the latent that initializes action generation. 

RL post-training of generative policies. A complementary line of work updates the generative policy itself from reward. Q-score matching trains a diffusion policy from a learned value function (Psenka et al., 2024), while DPPO applies policy optimization to diffusion-policy parameters (Ren et al., 2024). Flow-based counterparts include Flow Q-Learning (Park et al., 2025) and ReinFlow, which fine-tunes a flow-matching policy with online RL (Zhang et al., 2025). Flow Policy Optimization brings flow matching into an on-policy, PPO-style policy-gradient framework without requiring exact action likelihoods (McAllister et al., 2026). These methods retain a rich continuous action distribution and can alter the behaviors represented by the pretrained generator, but require updating or distilling the generative policy. Our setting instead treats the pretrained flow model as a fixed behavioral prior: interaction data trains only the critic, and improvement occurs by choosing and refining the decoder’s input latent. This distinction is important for large generalist policies, where preserving pretrained behavior and avoiding backpropagation through the policy are primary design constraints. Between direct policy fine-tuning and frozen-policy steering, PLD (Xiao et al., 2025) separates RL-based adaptation from updating the generalist. It first trains lightweight residual actors with a frozen VLA, then collects recovery trajectories aligned with the base policy’s state distribution and distills them into the VLA through supervised fine-tuning. This shares our use of a frozen behavioral prior during online RL, but ultimately transfers the improvements into the generator’s parameters. We instead retain the frozen generator and express adaptation through its input noise, connecting our approach to the steering methods discussed next. 

Steering a frozen policy in its noise space. Closest are methods that fix the policy and search its latent input, reranking chunks by value (Nakamoto et al., 2024) or steering toward human intent (Wang et al., 2025). DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., 2025) makes the noise the RL action and trains a continuous-control agent (Haarnoja et al., 2018) in it; recent variants add trust-region perturbations (Simsir and Oguz, 2026), critic-guided denoising (Ruan et al., 2026), compositional steering (Tan et al., 2026), and retrieval priors (Zhao et al., 2026). FRS (Tang et al., 2026) contributes our inversion primitive, recovering the noise that elicits a reference motion and using reversed human demonstrations as noise-space training data. Its RL variant prefills DSRL’s replay buffer and adds a behavior-cloning term, after which the actor still searches the continuous noise space. UniSteer (Lu et al., 2026) independently combines a frozen flow decoder, noise-space RL, and action-to-noise inversion. Its reference actions are online human corrections, whereas ours are a fixed library requiring no human in the loop; moreover, it trains a continuous noise actor, while AnchorQ learns only a critic over state-conditioned anchors and their local neighborhoods. Thus our use of inversion is persistent and representational: it defines the structured action class used throughout online learning rather than only providing data or guidance for a continuous actor. More generally, latent-steering methods differ in whether the learned object is an actor, a denoising-time guidance signal, or a selector over proposals. LP-DS learns constrained perturbations in the latent space (Simsir and Oguz, 2026), while Q-Pilots injects critic guidance into the generative process (Ruan et al., 2026). Residual flow steering (RFS) jointly optimizes latent-noise modulation for global adaptation and residual actions for local corrections (Su et al., 2026). Compositional and retrieval-based approaches instead construct guidance from reusable policy components or prior experience (Tan et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). AnchorQ occupies a different point in this design space: inversion first supplies a small set of state-conditioned semantic coordinates, and online value learning operates over that structured set before 17 exposing continuous local refinements. Consequently, the critic need not discover useful modes and their latent locations simultaneously. 

Reduced and coarse-to-fine action spaces for RL. Shrinking the effective action space can aid ex-ploration through discretization (Tang and Agrawal, 2020), per-dimension branching (Tavakoli et al., 2018), clustered or vector-quantized primitives (Lee et al., 2024; Shafiullah et al., 2022), and temporal abstraction over options (Sutton et al., 1999) or chunks (Li et al., 2025). CQN and its action-sequence extension re-cursively refine intervals of normalized per-dimension action coordinates (Seo and Abbeel, 2024; Seo et al., 2024). Our hierarchy instead operates in a learned generative latent reached by an inversion operator, related to inversion methods used for generative editing (Rout et al., 2024; Song et al., 2021). RLMM-Flow (Wang et al., 2026) also uses the phrase “coarse-to-fine latent steering,” but its hierarchy varies latent dimensionality: the coarse level pools a continuous latent along the horizon, and the fine level relaxes it to a full-dimensional residual. It is offline and does not use inversion. In contrast, our coarse level is a finite set of inverted reference motions, and our fine level is an online, Q-weighted local particle refinement around the selected motion. 

Uncertainty-aware and regularized value selection. Entropy- and KL-regularized RL trade value improvement against deviation from a reference distribution. This principle underlies relative-entropy policy improvement methods such as MPO (Abdolmaleki et al., 2018), maximum-entropy control (Haarnoja et al., 2018), and the general theory of regularized MDPs (Geist et al., 2019). SAC can also tune a global entropy temperature through a constrained objective (Haarnoja et al., 2018). Our temperature has a different role: it is state-dependent and determined by epistemic uncertainty rather than by a target policy entropy. Randomized value functions and critic ensembles provide practical surrogates for epistemic uncertainty. Bootstrapped DQN uses randomized value functions for deep exploration (Osband et al., 2016); randomized prior functions preserve diversity away from observed data (Osband et al., 2018); and SUNRISE uses ensemble uncertainty in both Bellman backups and upper-confidence action selection (Lee et al., 2021). Our value-tilting rule combines this uncertainty machinery with KL regularization at the level of a realized local particle cloud: exponential weights are the exact optimizer of a KL-regularized value objective relative to the untilted cloud. Rather than adding ensemble uncertainty as an exploration bonus, we use it to set the state-dependent inverse temperature β(s) = 1 /u epi (s). The selector therefore remains close to the reference weighting where candidate values are poorly known and becomes sharper as epistemic uncertainty contracts. This provides an uncertainty-aware mechanism for deciding how strongly to exploit local refinements while preserving the support supplied by the anchors and particles.

The adaptive weights compare value gaps in units of the floored uncertainty scale: wj /w k = exp(( qj −qk)/u epi (s)) ,where qk = ¯Qψ (s, z k) and uepi (s) = max {buepi (s), u min }. Large uncertainty therefore softens the weighting, while small uncertainty permits stronger preference for high-scoring particles. The floor limits βloc to 1/u min ,preventing a near-zero disagreement estimate from producing an arbitrarily sharp tilt. Theorem 9 interprets these weights as an uncertainty-weighted KL-regularized improvement rule. Its reliability requires the calibration condition in Assumption 8: local critic error must be controlled by the 20 uncertainty scale. Neither a small ensemble standard deviation nor the use of random priors establishes that condition automatically. Corollary 13 additionally accounts for executing the weighted latent, with a separate interpolation penalty under local smoothness. Adaptive reach and adaptive tilt can be enabled independently. Reach uses disagreement over the probe cloud, while tilt uses disagreement recomputed on each anchor’s refinement cloud. Their realized values need not change monotonically during learning, since the critic and clouds also change. 

Critic updates and next-state refinement. For each nonterminal replay row, we retrieve the cached next-state bank and refine all candidates with fresh particles, then select the highest-valued refined latent using the online ensemble mean. Adaptive reach, tilt, and particle weights are recomputed using the current online ensemble at the next state. At each update, we sample a subset I of Ntarg target heads and form the detached target y = r + γ min i∈I Q ¯ψ,i (s′, ˜z′), where ˜z′ is the selected refined latent; true terminal rows use y = r. Prioritized replay supplies importance-sampling weights for the squared TD loss. Each optimizer step is followed by a replay-priority refresh and a Polyak target update; the VLA and random prior networks remain frozen. 

## B.2 Implementation Details and Hyperparameters 

Following FRS (Tang et al., 2026), we use six cardinal Cartesian reference motions and a defer candidate sampled from the VLA’s base noise prior. For bimanual RoboTwin tasks, the Cartesian product of the six directions for each arm yields 36 reference motions; adding defer gives 37 candidates. In simulation, 

AnchorQ and DSRL use REDQ’s ensemble update scheme (Chen et al., 2021), with multiple critic updates per decision and a minimum over randomly sampled target heads. Real-world training uses a three-step TD target (multi-step Q-learning) in place of the one-step target in Algorithm 2, and averages the sampled target heads. Table 3 lists the settings for each benchmark. 

Critic architecture. The critic evaluates Q(s, z ) from state features and the full latent noise trajectory. On LIBERO-90, two frozen 384 -dimensional DINOv2-S (Oquab et al., 2023) embeddings from the third-person and wrist cameras form a 768 -dimensional visual input, alongside an 8-dimensional proprioceptive vector. Separate linear layers with ReLU project the visual and proprioceptive inputs to 256 dimensions each. Their concatenation passes through a 512 → 256 → 256 state encoder. The 10 × 32 noise trajectory is flattened and encoded by a 320 → 256 → 256 MLP. Input dimensions follow each benchmark’s observation and latent spaces. With randomized prior functions enabled, each of the N = 10 heads has independent trainable featurizers and encoders. An output MLP maps the concatenated state and noise features through widths 512 →

256 → 256 → 1. Hidden layers use ReLU, without layer normalization or dropout. Each head adds an independently initialized, frozen prior network: Qψ,i (s, z ) = fψi (s, z ) + κp i(s, z ), with prior scale κ given in Table 3. Disagreement across heads supplies the uncertainty signal for local adaptation. For our DROID policy (Khazatsky et al., 2024), we stack four frames of proprioceptive and DINO features and use shared trainable encoders with independent scalar output heads, without random priors. The critic applies layer normalization to the flattened 15 × 32 noise input; execution uses the original latent.

It does not imply that every practical DSRL run must explicitly test N − C additional modes. Rather, it shows that FRS-only prefill cannot, without further structural assumptions, guarantee removal of the exploration cost associated with retaining the full latent action class. 

Remark 1 (What the separation establishes) . Theorem 4 is a nested-model or adaptation result. The flow-reversal-restricted learner commits to the hypothesis that the online flow-reversal action abstraction has bias at most ϵ/ 2. The full-class learner retains a correctness guarantee on instances in which a non-flow-reversed latent mode is better. The lower bound shows that retaining this broader guarantee has a statistical cost even on the base instance, where the flow-reversal abstraction is exactly sufficient. A full-class learner can avoid that cost only by declining to rule out the non-flow-reversed alternatives, in which case it no longer has the stated correctness guarantee on Mfull .

Remark 2 (Why buffer prefill with flow-reversed action anchors cannot remove the lower bound) . The lower bound grants the learner the exact Bernoulli reward law of every flow-reversed mode before any online interaction. This is more information than any finite flow-reversal prefill generated solely by those modes. Since the base and alternative instances have identical reward laws on all flow-reversed modes, no amount of such prefill changes the likelihood ratio between them. The lower bound therefore applies, a fortiori , to FRS-prefill followed by DSRL Tang et al. (2026). 

Remark 3 (Relation to continuous latent spaces) . The finite latent set {z1, . . . , z N } may be embedded in any continuous latent space Z ⊆ Rdz containing N distinct points. More generally, each index may represent a measurable latent region whose elements induce the same reward law. The theorem therefore models a latent space with N behaviorally distinguishable modes and makes no smoothness assumption linking the rewards of different modes. Since the construction is a one-state, one-decision episodic MDP, it is also a standard Bernoulli multi-armed bandit and can be inserted as a decision state in a longer-horizon MDP without changing the identification lower bound at that state.

## F.4 Sharpness and regime consequences 

Theorem 5 bounds the loss of the flow-reversal-restricted policy using a one-step support gap and a one-step selection error. A natural question is whether the resulting factors are merely artifacts of the Bellman argument. Persistent action abstraction versus continuous noise-space RL. The distinction concerns the online action class, rather than a particular initialization scheme. In the anchor-restricted setting analyzed here, AnchorQ receives the statistical benefit of a smaller action class and pays the persistent support price quantified above. Continuous noise-space RL instead learns an actor or perturbation that selects latents 43 beyond a fixed anchor bank. Examples include DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., 2025), FRS-prefill followed by DSRL (Tang et al., 2026), human-guided noise-actor learning in UniSteer (Lu et al., 2026), and the trust-region noise perturbations of LP-DS (Simsir and Oguz, 2026). These methods use different sources of guidance and constraints, but do not impose the persistent finite FRS action restriction studied here. When the online class retains all latent modes, it need not incur the fixed FRS-bank support gap. Theo-rem 4 shows that guaranteeing correctness over this broader class can nevertheless require additional online exploration, even when the FRS bank is sufficient on the instance encountered. FRS-only prefill is a concrete example: it provides information about bank modes without ruling out better modes outside the bank. This is a worst-case statement under the theorem’s information and model assumptions, not a method-specific lower bound for every noise-space RL algorithm; additional guidance, structural assumptions, or restrictions on reachable latents require separate analysis. Together, the two results identify the tradeoff between lower identification complexity with explicit support bias and broader expressiveness with a potentially higher cost of verifying that no better latent mode exists. 

Technical Novelty. The contraction, Bellman-residual, and resolvent arguments used in the proof are standard tools. The contribution is their organization around the state-dependent online flow-reversal ab-straction. In particular, the theorem introduces a full-Q⋆ one-step support gap for the latent bank generated via flow-reversal, proves a sharp bridge from that local geometric quantity to the infinite-horizon value price of persistent flow-reversal restriction, and separates it exactly from the finite-sample error of selecting among the current-state flow-reversed latents. This decomposition is the general-MDP counterpart to the adap-tation separation in Theorem 4 and provides the bias coordinate for the particle bias–complexity frontier developed next. 

# G AnchorQ traces a bias–complexity frontier 

Theorem 4 shows that a online flow-reversal action anchor abstraction can reduce online identification com-plexity when a small structured action class is sufficient. Theorem 5 shows that the same restriction incurs an explicit support price. Exact-anchor AnchorQ therefore represents one endpoint of a statistical tradeoff: it has the smallest persistent action class, but it can exclude useful latents that lie near an flow-reversed anchor rather than exactly on the anchor bank. Particle AnchorQ relaxes the abstraction locally. The purpose of this section is to formalize the resulting frontier. We first define a deterministic local envelope (whose size is parameterized by ρ) around the state-dependent flow-reversed anchors. We then show that increasing ρ monotonically reduces the one-step support gap while monotonically increasing, at every fixed behavioral resolution, the maximal number of pairwise behaviorally distinguishable local candidates. Finally, under a finite-state generative model, we give an explicit finite-sample bound in which both quantities appear. We use ρ ≥ 0 for the deterministic local-reach radius in the analysis. The symbol ϵB > 0 denotes resolution in the Bellman behavioral pseudometric, whereas ϵz > 0 denotes Euclidean resolution in latent space. The deterministic local reach ρ around flow-reversed action anchors is also not identical to the Gaussian particle scale σp used by the implementation. A Gaussian scale determines a distribution of finitely many random candidates and has unbounded support. A separate result at the end of this section gives a formal connection between a finite Gaussian cloud and a bounded local envelope of reach ρ.

## G.8 Interpretation, novelty, and connection to the preceding results 

Theorem 6 describes a reach-parameterized bias–complexity tradeoff connecting the two regimes identified by Theorems 4 and 5. At zero reach, ρ = 0 , the local action set is exactly the online flow-reversed anchor bank, so the structural bias is ¯∆F and the action class has at most C candidates before any further discretization. Increasing ρ

admits local residual corrections. The oracle support gap ¯∆ρ can only decrease, and under smooth local geometry it is bounded above by a quadratic function of the residual distance from the nearest anchor to an optimal latent. At the same time, the maximal number of pairwise behaviorally distinguishable actions at resolution ϵB can only increase. Under nondegenerate local geometry, every behavioral ϵB-net must contain at least order (ρ/ϵ B)deff candidates. The resulting finite-sample sub-optimality guarantee (68) separates three error contributions, each scaled by 1/(1 − γ): ¯∆ρ + ϵB + 2 ϵQ,

namely, the remaining local support bias, behavior discretization error, and finite-sample value-selection error, respectively. The same reach that reduces the first term enlarges the behavioral action class appearing in the sample count. This is the precise sense in which AnchorQ traces a bias–complexity frontier rather than simply providing more exploration. The connection to Theorem 4 is statistical. In a one-decision specialization, enlarging the local reach adds latent modes that must be ruled out if the learner remains correct over the larger class. Corollary 5 shows that the additional verification cost is proportional to the number of newly admitted modes, even on a zero-bias instance for the smaller reach. The connection to Theorem 5 is structural. That theorem is the zero-reach endpoint ρ = 0 . Proposition 3 applies the same one-step V ⋆ −Q⋆ support-gap logic to every local reach. Particle expansion therefore changes the support term itself; it does not merely improve selection among the original anchors. The contraction, packing, Höeffding, and approximate-value-iteration arguments are standard. The Q-FRS-specific contribution is their organization around the state-dependent local FRS action family. In particular, the theorem 

i. identifies a monotone path from exact-anchor Q-FRS toward broader latent control; 

ii. measures complexity in terms of behaviorally distinguishable candidates rather than raw particle count; 

iii. places support bias and finite-data selection error in the same full-MDP performance bound; 

iv. distinguishes deterministic local reach from the stochastic Gaussian scale used to instantiate a finite particle cloud. The finite-sample guarantee above applies to synchronous empirical Q-iteration on fixed behavioral nets under a generative model. The Gaussian coverage results connect finite particle clouds to the representation-error term, but do not by themselves provide a sample-complexity guarantee for the implemented hierarchical policy. Theorem 8 separately bounds the suboptimality of that policy, accounting for coarse-anchor selection, particle weighting, critic error, and barycentric refinement. 61 The reach parameter and the value-tilting parameter have different roles. Reach determines which local behaviors are made available; value tilting reallocates probability within the behaviors that are available. The next section studies the latter operation and its connection to entropic risk. 

# H Entropic weighting for local particle refinement 

Theorem 6 separates the coarse behavioral representation supplied by the flow-reversed anchors from the local degrees of freedom supplied by particles. In the implemented method, a coarse anchor is selected first. Exponential tilting is then applied only to the local particle cloud around that anchor, and the resulting weights are used to construct a weighted latent refinement. This section studies this local weighting operation and its effect on the executed barycentric refinement. Throughout this section, β ≥ 0 has one meaning only: it is the exponential-tilting intensity, equivalently an inverse temperature. It is distinct from the confidence parameter α, the MDP discount γ, the deterministic local reach ρ, the Gaussian particle scale σp, and the flow-integration step size h. We analyze only the positive, reward-seeking tilt β > 0; no negative or lower-tail tilt is considered.

Risk preference as a local refinement mechanism. Conditional on the selected anchor c, the law νs,c 

describes the local variation supplied by the particle construction. The random variable 

Y qs,c = q(s, Z s,c ), Zs,c ∼ νs,c ,

therefore describes variation in score across possible local deviations around that coarse behavior. Equa-tion (105) shows that positive β does more than optimize their mean score: to first order in β, it additionally rewards variance in local scores, and hence the presence of upper-tail value opportunities. The resulting exponential change of measure converts an undirected local particle cloud into a value-directed weighting of that cloud. In the implemented method, larger weight does not correspond to larger probability of executing an individual particle. Rather, it gives that particle greater influence on the barycen-tric refinement ˜zc,β (s). Thus the three stages of AnchorQ have distinct roles: • FRS anchor generation : reverse coarse behavior modes to latent anchors. • Local particles : expose local degrees of freedom. • Risk-sensitive weighting : direct the local refinement, and select coarse behavioral mode. As β ↓ 0, the weighting approaches the untilted local law. As β increases, the weighting concentrates progressively on the highest-scoring particles; in a finite cloud with a unique score maximizer, it converges to unit weight on that particle as β → ∞ .

Score risk and critic uncertainty are distinct. The upper-tail preference across local particle scores should be distinguished from uncertainty in the value estimate of a fixed particle. The random variable in the entropic objective is q(s, Z s,c ) with Zs,c ∼ νs,c , so its variability is across local candidate behaviors. ## H.9 Interpretation and novelty 

The Gibbs variational identity and the associated exponential-family derivatives are standard. The contri-bution here is their use within the specific coarse-to-fine structure of AnchorQ , together with results that connect the resulting risk-sensitive weights to the barycentric latent actually executed by the robot. First, the analysis identifies the precise random variable to which the entropic functional is applied. Conditional on state s and selected anchor c,

Y qs,c = q(s, Z s,c ), Zs,c ∼ νs,c ,

is the score of a random local refinement of that anchor. Positive β is therefore upper-tail seeking over local particle values, not over coarse FRS commands, critic-head uncertainty, or realized environment returns. It becomes an exact entropic functional of realized return only under the conditional transformation of Proposition 10. Second, the exponential weights have an exact optimization interpretation. The tilted local law νqs,c,β is the unique optimizing change of measure in the KL-regularized variational representation of the entropic func-tional. Thus the weights are not an ad hoc softmax heuristic: they are the optimal risk-sensitive reweighting of the local particle law under a KL penalty from its untilted reference distribution. This interpretation also separates the role of β from the particle parameters: (σp, M ) control which local refinements are represented, whereas β controls how aggressively value concentrates their influence. Third, the high-value amplification result provides a reliability guarantee for this local weighting.
