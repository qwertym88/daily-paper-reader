# Compositional Humanoid Manipulation through Isomorphic Teleoperation and Independent Skill Routing 

Yiming Wang 1, Peixian Yang 2, Lifeng Zhuo 1, Lai Wei 3,Xinling Du 4, Kun Song 2, Weiming Wang 1, and Daolin Ma 1

Abstract — Humanoid manipulation requires reusable arm skills, yet demonstrating every possible skill combination is impractical. We present Humanoid Semantic Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Policy (HS-MoDP), which composes sepa-rately demonstrated arm skills through independent semantic routing over a shared expert pool. This design preserves arm-specific skill control while sharing action-generation parameters across arms. A semantic scheduler integrates the learned policy with an existing locomotion controller through alternating manipulation and repositioning stages, extending the effective workspace and enabling skill reuse across location changes. To acquire demonstrations of whole-body contact manipulation, we develop G1Exo, an isomorphic exoskeleton that provides direct joint-level control of arm configurations. Experiments on a Unitree G1 show stronger skill composition than the baselines. G1Exo enables more reliable contact teleoperation than end-effector VR-IK, and its demonstrations improve contact-skill learning across four policy architectures. For HS-MoDP, switching from VR-IK to G1Exo demonstrations raises full cabinet workflow success from 35% to 65%. All project hardware designs and source code will be released as open source after the review process. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid manipulation uses dual-arm action, body con-tact, and mobility to interact with objects across an extended workspace [1]–[3]. Gathering objects with the forearms or holding them against the torso requires control over arm configurations. Reusing separately demonstrated single-arm skills in new dual-arm combinations requires preserving each skill as the other arm’s task changes. We study how to cap-ture the configurations needed for contact manipulation and compose separately demonstrated skills during execution. Vision-language-action (VLA) models learn robot behav-iors from large-scale visual, language, and action data [4]– [6]. Yet demonstrating every combination becomes increas-ingly costly: m left-arm and n right-arm skills admit mn 

candidate pairings in a single stage. As analyzed in Skil-lVLA [7], policies may retain demonstration-specific de-pendencies between arms in both predicted actions and internal policy representations. An arm’s learned behavior may therefore depend on what the other arm did during demonstration. Successful execution of individual skills does not establish that each skill will be preserved when paired with a different one. We address this composition problem through separate skill conditioning and routing for each arm.

Capturing the configurations needed for body-contact ma-nipulation is a complementary challenge. For a redundant seven-degree-of-freedom arm, an end-effector target does not uniquely determine elbow placement or forearm orienta-tion [8]. These configurations affect how the arms contact objects and support them against the torso. We develop G1Exo, a wearable exoskeleton kinematically isomorphic to the Unitree G1 arms. Direct joint-level mapping lets operators specify arm configurations, while a physical torso boundary provides a spatial reference for arm–torso interac-tions. For skill reuse, we introduce Humanoid Semantic Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Policy (HS-MoDP). Building on single-arm semantic interfaces and semantic expert routing [7], [9], HS-MoDP conditions two action streams on distinct skill rep-resentations and routes them independently through shared experts. Each stream receives its own wrist-camera and proprioceptive inputs alongside shared head-camera obser-vations. This design retains scene context and shares action-generation parameters while limiting direct dependence on the other arm’s local inputs. A frozen VLM schedules skills and coordinates the learned policy with an existing locomotion controller through alternating manipulation and repositioning stages. Experiments on the Unitree G1 evaluate skill composition, whole-body contact teleoperation, and staged mobile manip-ulation. With equal demonstration counts, G1Exo improves contact-skill learning across four policies and increases HS-MoDP’s full cabinet workflow success. Our contributions are threefold:

A. Teleoperation and Demonstration Interfaces for Hu-manoid Manipulation 

Teleoperation interfaces determine which aspects of hu-man motion are conveyed to a robot. Physical leader– follower systems provide demonstrations for bimanual and mobile manipulation [10], [11], while human motion track-ing and handheld grippers offer alternative collection inter-faces [1], [12]. For whole-arm contact, demonstrations must also specify configurations relevant to object contact. Exoskeleton interfaces provide joint-level motion inputs for teleoperation and demonstration collection across em-bodiments [13]–[16]. For humanoids, HOMIE integrates iso-morphic arm control with body and hand interfaces for loco-manipulation [2], while HumanoidExo uses wearable record-ings to support whole-body policy learning [17]. G1Exo focuses on directly specifying arm configurations and their spatial relationship to the torso during whole-body contact manipulation. Its low-cost wearable design combines joint-level arm control with a physical torso boundary, allowing operators to execute contact behaviors and provide corre-sponding demonstrations. 

B. Dual-arm Coordination and Compositional Skill Reuse 

Dual-arm policies model inter-arm dependence through joint action prediction [18], intermediate representation ex-change [19], [20], or explicit role assignment [21], [22]. Structured latent representations and coordinated versus arm-specific MoE pathways further distinguish shared behavior from individual arm adjustments [23], [24]. These mecha-nisms govern how the arms interact during execution. Com-posing separately learned skills additionally requires each arm’s intended behavior to remain independently selectable. Unimanual policy reuse has been explored through skill management, paired policy architectures, and energy-based coordination [25]–[27]. SkillVLA explicitly addresses skill entanglement and unseen left–right skill combinations [7]. It uses separate semantic instructions and low-level reasoning and action-generation streams for the two arms, with gated cross-arm communication to support cooperative behaviors. This establishes a formulation in which known skills can be invoked separately and recombined during execution. We study this formulation in humanoid joint-space manipulation, including concurrent skill execution in a changing shared workspace and manipulation involving arm–torso contact. 

C. Semantic Skill Conditioning with Mixture-of-Experts 

Sparse MoE policies use selective expert activation to share model capacity across tasks [28], [29]. The routing conditions determine how this capacity is organized: MoDE selects denoising experts according to the diffusion noise level [30], whereas MoE-ACT conditions routing on task context, visual observations, and proprioceptive states [31]. Skill-based approaches introduce additional structure, includ-ing primitive skill conditioning in diffusion policies and an orthogonal skill basis with sticky routing in SMP [32], [33]. SMoDP connects expert selection to skill semantics through semantic routing, action-chunk consistency, and dual semantic alignment of skill representations and routing be-havior [9]. It evaluates few-shot compositional adaptation with frozen experts on simulation benchmarks and bimanual multitask manipulation on ALOHA. We build on these routing and alignment mechanisms to support independently specified skills for concurrent arm execution. HS-MoDP gives each arm its own semantic condition and router within a shared diffusion transformer. In contrast to SkillVLA’s separate single-arm action modules, the two streams access a common expert pool. Arm identity conditions the shared experts, allowing the model to distinguish the G1’s mirrored arms while sharing action-generation parameters. III. G1E XO : H ARDWARE FOR HUMANOID -A LIGNED 

WHOLE -B ODY TELEOPERATION 

We developed G1Exo, a wearable bimanual teleoperation exoskeleton, to collect whole-body manipulation data for the Unitree G1 robot. Whole-body manipulation establishes contact with objects through body parts such as the arms and chest, using arm configurations and their positions relative to the torso to perform tasks such as holding and hugging [3]. G1Exo uses a bimanual mechanism that is kinematically isomorphic to the G1 upper limbs. It maps exoskeleton joint angles directly to the corresponding robot joints, allowing the operator to specify arm configurations, including elbow placement and forearm orientation. Handle triggers control gripper opening and closing. As shown in Fig. 1(a–b), G1Exo consists of a base with a chest plate, two exoskeleton arms, and trigger handles. The arms match the kinematic parameters of the G1 upper limbs, including the arrangement and rotational limits of all 14 joints and the mounting positions of the arms relative to the torso. Each joint incorporates an angular encoder. After zero-position alignment, the measured angle serves as the motion input for the corresponding robot joint. Arm supports and straps secure the operator’s arms to the exoskeleton, enabling direct control of the entire arm configuration through the operator’s movements. The chest plate provides a physical boundary correspond-ing to the robot’s torso. As the arms move inward, the plate restricts further motion into the torso region, allowing the operator to perceive the spatial constraints between the arms and torso. A vest and hook-and-loop straps secure G1Exo closely to the operator’s torso and reduce relative slipping during operation. The isomorphic arms and chest plate support chest hugging and underarm holding (Fig. 2), as well as bringing both arms inward around an object. G1Exo uses reusable modular rotary joints, as shown in Fig. 1(c). A cross-roller bearing supports relative rotation between the joint housing and shaft, a miniature rotary encoder measures the joint angle with 12-bit resolution, and a limit pin constrains rotation to the limits of the corresponding Fig. 1: G1Exo hardware and joint mapping: (a) wearable exoskeleton; (b) corresponding joints on the Unitree G1; (c) exploded view of a modular rotary joint. Each terminal handle incorporates a trigger and an encoder of the same type, mapping trigger rotation to the robot gripper’s opening width. The system contains 16 encoders: 14 measure arm joint angles, and two measure trigger inputs. All encoders communicate with the acquisition board via an RS-485 bus, with angle signals sampled at 100 Hz. A VIVE Tracker mounted on the base provides input for robot locomotion during mobile manipulation. The structural components of G1Exo are 3D-printed from glass-fiber-reinforced ABS to balance wearable weight and structural strength. Mechanical hardware and electronic com-ponents are commercially available, mass-produced parts, keeping the total bill of materials (BOM) below USD 600. HS-MoDP (Humanoid Semantic Mixture-of-Experts Dif-fusion Policy) realizes single-to-dual-arm skill composition by treating separately learned arm behaviors as reusable skill primitives. Each arm independently selects experts from a shared pool according to its skill representation and combines their outputs using normalized routing weights, enabling previously unseen left–right combinations without requiring paired demonstrations. Fig. 3 presents the policy and its integration with a seman-tic scheduler, which selects skills and connects the learned manipulation policy to an existing locomotion controller.

B. Single-Arm Skill-Semantic Conditioning 

Each arm receives a separate skill description. These descriptions specify the intended behavior of each action stream and come from demonstration annotations during training. The two streams share the task instruction and head-camera observations, while each stream uses its own wrist-camera view and joint and gripper states. This asymmetric allocation preserves shared scene context while suppressing demonstration-specific cross-arm correlations in local sens-ing, thereby improving transfer from single-arm demonstra-tions to dual-arm execution. A frozen CLIP text encoder maps each skill description to an embedding ua, and a frozen visual backbone encodes the camera observations. We combine action denoising with semantic supervision that connects skill meaning to learned representations and routing behavior. Adapting the diffusion and dual semantic alignment objectives of SMoDP [9] to single-arm represen-tations and routing gives 

L = Ldiff + λinter Linter + λintra Lintra + γLbal + δLz . (7) The denoising loss Ldiff is the mean squared error for preconditioned action denoising, averaged over supervised action elements. The inter-modal loss Linter aligns learned skill representations with frozen skill-text embeddings. The intra-modal loss Lintra encourages similar routing for seman-tically related skills. Both alignment losses use contrastive weights derived from skill-text similarity. For routing alignment, we use normalized, chunk-averaged sparse routing weights at each layer, whereas SMoDP for-mulates this term using flattened routing logits. The load-balancing loss Lbal balances expert utilization, and the router z-loss Lz regularizes logit magnitudes. Alignment losses are computed separately for each arm and averaged, using skill descriptions as the alignment labels. For demonstrations with only one annotated arm, the other arm contributes neither action loss nor alignment supervision. We train the skill encoder and diffusion transformer while keeping the visual and text backbones frozen. The VLM-based semantic scheduler and the locomotion controller remain fixed during training. 

E. Skill Scheduling and Closed-Loop Execution 

At deployment, a frozen VLM selects skills from pre-defined vocabularies based on the task instruction, scene observations, and task progress. Each skill has a semantic label, an execution interface, and a completion condition, together with the parameters required by its controller. During manipulation, HS-MoDP uses the latest obser-vation history and current arm-specific skills to predict an action chunk. The robot executes a configured prefix before generating the next chunk from new observations. This receding-horizon process updates action generation and expert selection as the scene and selected skills change. In the mobile cabinet task, manipulation-stage transitions are driven by VLM assessments of task completion, aggre-gated over a recent observation window. At the locomotion stage, the scheduler issues a predefined base repositioning command while the arms hold their current pose. Manipula-tion resumes after the locomotion executor reports comple-tion, producing a sequence of manipulation and repositioning stages. V. E XPERIMENTS

Q2: Does G1Exo improve whole-body contact teleoperation and downstream policy learning over end-effector VR-IK? Q3: Can learned skills support manipulation with staged repositioning? Platform and configuration. We use a Unitree G1 with two Dex1 grippers, a head-mounted RealSense D435i, and a RealSense D405 on each wrist. HS-MoDP predicts 16-dimensional actions (14 arm joints and two grippers) from two observation frames, using a 48-step horizon. Training and inference use a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. 

Baselines, datasets, and evaluation. We compare HS-MoDP with the generalist policies π0.5 [5] and GR00T N1.7 [6], and the MoE diffusion policy MoDE [30]. E. Ablation Study 

We examine independent routing, semantic alignment, and observation isolation using the box-pushing and toy-placement task and demonstration protocol in Section V-B. The box is both the left arm’s manipulated object and the right arm’s placement target, testing skill composition in a changing shared workspace. Each configuration is evaluated separately on both unimanual skill primitives and their com-bination, with 20 trials per setting and matched initial-scene distributions. Standalone success requires the box to reach the table center or the toy to be grasped and placed inside the box, respectively. Combined success requires both goals within one episode. The variants below otherwise follow the full model’s configuration. 

Coupled routing. We retain both arm-specific routers and average the dense routing probabilities of arms assigned active manipulation skills at each layer. One shared subset of k experts is sampled during training or selected by top-k

at inference, with identical renormalized weights applied to both streams. Routing remains consistent across each action chunk and denoising rollout. 

Without semantic alignment. We set λinter = λintra = 0 

while retaining skill-text conditioning, action denoising, load balancing, and the router z-loss. 

Unified observations. Both skill encoding and diffusion action generation receive the head and both wrist-camera views, and each action stream receives the complete dual-arm joint and gripper states. Single-arm skill conditions, independent routing, and shared experts are retained. All ablations retained comparable standalone performance but reduced combination success (Fig. 7). Coupled routing caused the largest decline, with failures to release the toy or premature release during placement, while box pushing was largely preserved. Without semantic alignment, the toy arm often failed to adjust placement when the box stopped short. Unified observations produced repeated corrections and oscillations, consistent with a mismatch between sep-arately collected demonstrations and the cross-arm inputs encountered during concurrent execution. These patterns support independent expert allocation, semantic alignment, and local observation isolation for skill composition. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented HS-MoDP for composing separately demon-strated arm skills through independent semantic routing over shared experts, and G1Exo, a low-cost wearable isomorphic exoskeleton for joint-level control and demonstration collec-tion. A VLM-based scheduler integrates the learned policy with an existing locomotion controller across manipulation and repositioning stages. Experiments on the Unitree G1 showed stronger skill composition than the baselines, with ablations supporting independent routing, semantic align-ment, and observation isolation. G1Exo improved contact teleoperation over VR-IK, and its demonstrations improved contact-skill learning across four policy architectures. HS-MoDP composed skills using either demonstration source, with G1Exo data further improving concurrent manipulation and full cabinet workflow success.
