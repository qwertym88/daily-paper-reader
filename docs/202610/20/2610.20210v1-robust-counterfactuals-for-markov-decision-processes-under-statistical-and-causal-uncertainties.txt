## Abstract 

Counterfactual reasoning (asking what would have happened, had a decision been different ) is a powerful tool for identifying causes of failure and explaining the behaviour of AI systems in sequen-tial decision-making tasks. In Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), two sources of uncertainty complicate counterfactual inference: statistical uncertainty in the MDP transitions when the MDP is es-timated from trajectory data, and uncertainty about the unknown causal model, as there are multiple structural causal models (SCMs) compatible with each MDP. Existing work addresses these two chal-lenges in isolation. We address both simultaneously by introducing a framework for partial counterfactual inference in MDPs that is robust to both statistical and causal uncertainty. Our approach pro-vides: 1) closed-form analytical bounds for the counterfactual prob-abilities that are valid across all SCMs entailed by a given interval MDP (IMDP); 2) a method to compute minimal robust interventions to achieve a desired counterfactual outcome via pessimistic value iteration on the induced counterfactual IMDP. Since the latter is constructed using our analytical bounds, our approach remains efficient and scalable to realistic MDP environments. Experiments across multiple case studies demonstrate our method’s robustness and practical utility for identifying minimal sets of actions that, if changed, would have led to improved outcomes.

## 1 Introduction 

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are a fundamental model for describing sequential decision-making processes under uncertainty, including reinforcement learning (RL) problems. Counterfactual reasoning for MDPs is central to policy evaluation, explanation, and accountability. Given an observed MDP path, i.e., a state-action se-quence, one may ask: Would a different sequence of actions have led to a different outcome? Such questions arise in numerous applications, such as generating counterfactual explanations [23, 29, 30], facili-tating policy transfer [15], data augmentation with counterfactual paths [18, 28], as well as LLM counterfactuals [4, 25]. However, two distinct sources of uncertainty complicate counter-factual reasoning for MDPs. First, counterfactuals in MDPs are gen-erally not point-identified: many structural causal models (SCMs) can be compatible with the MDP, each yielding different coun-terfactual outcomes. We call this causal uncertainty . Yet, many approaches assume a fixed SCM, typically a Gumbel-max SCM [2, 14, 15, 22, 29, 33], potentially leading to invalid counterfactual conclusions. Second, the transition probabilities of an MDP are often estimated from finite trajectory data. Yet, most counterfac-tual analyses treat these point estimates as precise probabilities, ignoring statistical uncertainty in the learned MDP. Recent work by Lally et al. [17] addresses the causal uncertainty problem by bounding counterfactual probabilities across all SCMs consistent with the MDP. Also, there is a well-established line of research (e.g., [21, 26, 27]) on capturing statistical uncertainty by using uncertain MDPs, where each transition probability lies within a data-derived confidence set, most often an interval (yielding in-terval MDPs). However, there is no work to date that addresses both kinds of uncertainty, even though both matter for identifying robust interventions and explanations. Consider a learned model for sep-sis treatment. Suppose that, under a nominal transition estimate, changing the dosage at step 𝑡 prevents an adverse outcome. So we would conclude that action 𝑎 𝑡 is a sufficient intervention to prevent that outcome, but only if the transition probabilities were precise. Since these are learned from data and subject to sampling variabil-ity, there could be alternative models supported by the data where the adverse outcome would have occurred regardless of the dosage at 𝑡 , i.e., where intervening on 𝑎 𝑡 alone is not enough. Thus, we seek counterfactual interventions that are robust to statistical uncertain-ties, i.e., valid across all MDP models entailed by some data-derived confidence set. For a similar reason, since there are many SCM models compatible with an MDP, we want such interventions to be robust to causal uncertainty, because each SCM can yield differ-ent counterfactual probabilities and thus different counterfactual policies that would have led to the patient’s recovery. In this paper, we develop a framework for robust counterfactual reasoning in uncertain MDPs. Our contributions span two main areas: 

1) Counterfactual bounds for uncertain MDPs. We derive analyti-cal bounds for the counterfactual transition probabilities of a given interval MDP (IMDP). Our bounds are robust to both sources of uncertainty in this setting: they are guaranteed to include the true (unknown) counterfactual probabilities for every MDP M entailed by the IMDP, and every SCM compatible with M. To achieve this, we build on the work of Lally et al. [17], who derived bounds on counterfactual probabilities for standard (certain) MDPs, and ex-tend it – for the first time – to the uncertain MDP setting. Crucially, without our analytical characterisation, computing robust counter-factual probabilities becomes prohibitive, as it entails solving an optimisation problem with a number of constraints exponential in the size of the MDP [7, 31, 32]. Finally, we prove that our analytical bounds are exact (i.e., tight) in all but one case. For this remain-ing case, we provide conservative (i.e., sound) bounds that remain empirically efficient. As a contribution of independent interest, we

2) Minimal robust counterfactual interventions. Building on these bounds, we define the notion of a minimal robust counterfactual intervention as an MDP policy that performs the smallest set of action changes to induce a desired counterfactual outcome across all the causal models induced by statistical and causal uncertainty. In our MDP setting, w.l.o.g., we define outcomes as constraints over the (counter)factual path’s expected return. Given an IMDP estimated from data and an observed path, to derive such robust interventions, our method first constructs a counterfactual IMDP by computing bounds for all its transitions; then, interventions are found as solutions to a robust value iteration procedure [20] modi-fied so that its value function keeps track of the allowed number of action changes. Finally, we evaluate our approach on a variety of MDP environments to investigate sources of failure in suboptimal RL policies, demonstrating that our method remains accurate and robust to uncertainty in both the MDP transitions and the causal model.

## 3.2 Closed-form Bounds for MDPs 

Lally et al. [17] prove that, in MDP settings, this optimisation prob-lem reduces to exact, closed-form expressions. These bounds also incorporate two key assumptions, counterfactual stability and coun-terfactual monotonicity, to avoid unintuitive counterfactual prob-abilities. Without these assumptions, the optimisation in (3)-(5) tends to produce loose or trivial [0, 1] bounds, which compound over multiple transitions in sequential decision-making and reduce the usefulness of counterfactual inference. Counterfactual stability is a standard assumption in the literature [23, 29] and states that the outcome under an intervention changes only if the relative probability of some other outcome increases relative to the observed outcome. Counterfactual monotonicity ensures that observing an outcome cannot make it less likely in the counterfactual setting, and that unobserved (but possible) outcomes cannot become more likely. ## 4 Methodology 

We first describe how to derive an IMDP from paths sampled from the unknown MDP, and how to construct a counterfactual IMDP (CFIMDP) for a given observed path. We then formalise the notion of minimal robust counterfactual interventions and show how to compute them via robust value iteration over the CFIMDP. 

## 4.1 Learning IMDPs from Data 

We begin by collecting observed trajectories of length |𝜏 | (an environment-specific hyperparameter) from either the initial state, if defined, or any non-sink state. Following the standard approach [21, 26, 27], we estimate the IMDP by computing a confidence interval for each transition probability from these observations, assuming that the MDP’s topology is known (the so-called grey-box 

setting) 2. We use the Clopper-Pearson method [5], with an overall confidence level 1 −𝛼 distributed evenly across all nondeterministic transitions (see Appendix D for more details). We assign a minimum probability 𝜖 to all possible transitions ( 𝜖 = 0.01, or 10 −4 for Sepsis, whose smallest transition probability is approximately 10 −4) to en-sure that observed transitions have strictly positive lower bounds, as required for valid counterfactual inference. We note our frame-work is agnostic to the estimation method: more sample-efficient methods than Clopper-Pearson may yield tighter IMDP bounds from the same data, and hence tighter CFIMDP bounds.

Relation to actual causation. Minimal robust counterfactual in-terventions are closely related to the notion of actual causes [11]. Informally, a set of observed actions is an actual cause of an outcome if changing those actions, while keeping all other actions at their observed values, would have changed the counterfactual outcome, and no strict subset of these actions has this property. Identifying an actual cause requires knowing the SCM and the context (the exogenous noise values), which together determine a single coun-terfactual path. In our setting, neither is known: we only know that the true CFMDP lies within the CFIMDP, and each CFMDP induces a distribution over counterfactual paths. We therefore require the change in outcome to hold robustly and in expectation, making our interventions a robust, policy-based analogue of actual causes.

## 5 Evaluation 

We evaluate our approach along five axes: (1) its accuracy in off-policy evaluation tasks; (2) how its policies compare against those obtained from the exact CFIMDP (i.e., the CFIMDP derived from the true MDP); (3) its robustness relative to the Gumbel-max approach; (4) how well it identifies minimal action changes to achieve a desired counterfactual value; and (5) the trade-off of using conservative vs. tight lower bounds. We evaluate our approach on a range of MDP tasks, ranging from simple models (GridWorld and Frozen Lake) to more complex clinical and navigation tasks (Sepsis and Aircraft). Full descriptions of the MDP environments and system details can be found in the Appendix F. The code is available at: https://github.com/jessicalally/robust-cf -inference-beyond-mdps.

## 5.2 Evaluating Performance 

Next, we evaluate the impact of learning the model from data by comparing the performance of the counterfactual policies de-rived from our data-driven IMDP (with conservative lower bounds) against those derived from the exact CFIMDP. We use a subopti-mal observational policy 𝜋 that selects a random action over the optimal action with probability 0 .5, construct an IMDP with hyper-parameters (𝑁 = 100 , 000 , 𝛼 = 0.1), and derive CFIMDPs for 100 paths sampled under 𝜋 using both approaches. We then compare the average values 𝑉 ∗ (𝑠 0, 0, 𝑘 ) of the resulting pessimistic optimal policies, where 𝑠 0 is the MDP’s initial state, for each maximum number of action changes 𝑘 .For GridWorld and Frozen Lake (Figures 2a-2c), our approach yields estimates of 𝑉 ∗ (𝑠 0, 0, 𝑘 ) close to those obtained from the exact CFIMDP, showing that the statistical uncertainty in the MDP has little impact on the robust counterfactual policies. For Sepsis and Aircraft (Figures 2d and 2e), 𝑉 ∗ (𝑠 0, 0, 𝑘 ) is slightly lower, as the larger state spaces require wider bounds in the IMDP to maintain an overall significance level of 𝛼 = 0.1; with more data, these estimates would tighten. The resulting policies may therefore identify more conservative interventions, i.e., change more actions than strictly necessary. However, since the statistical guarantees on the IMDP carry over to the CFIMDP, these interventions remain valid at the stated confidence level. Finally, as shown in Section 5.3, policies derived from data-driven IMDPs tend to exceed their estimated performance when evaluated on the true CFIMDP, suggesting that these policies are generally accurate and close to the optimal policy obtained without statistical uncertainty. 

## 5.3 Evaluating Robustness 

To evaluate robustness, we compare the counterfactual policies generated by our approach (with conservative lower bounds) and from the data-driven Gumbel-max approach on the exact CFIMDP, i.e., by their worst-case counterfactual value across all causal mod-els compatible with the true MDP (Table 3) 3. Our policies perform close to the optimal policies learned from the true CFIMDP, show-ing little impact from statistical uncertainty in these examples. In contrast, the Gumbel-max CFIMDP policies perform suboptimally, suggesting that committing to a specific causal model (even when accounting for uncertainty in the transition dynamics) can be unre-liable if the assumed model is misspecified.

## 6 Conclusion 

We have presented a counterfactual inference approach for MDPs that is robust to both uncertainty in the underlying transition dy-namics and the unknown causal model. We derived closed-form bounds on counterfactual transition probabilities, enabling scalable inference in larger MDPs. Building on these bounds, we introduced minimal robust counterfactual interventions: non-stationary poli-cies that guarantee a desired expected return under all admissible counterfactual dynamics, while minimising the maximum number of action changes. Experiments across diverse MDP environments demonstrate the robustness and scalability of our approach, with counterfactual interventions closely tracking those of the exact counterfactual MDP. Future work will address unobserved con-founding in offline trajectory datasets and extend our approach to continuous and partially-observable environments.

## F Environment Descriptions and System Details 

Below, we provide brief descriptions of the MDP environments and system specifications used in our experiments. The same environments as Lally et al. [17] are used to allow for comparison. 

## F.1 GridWorld 

In the GridWorld MDP, an agent navigates a 4 × 4 grid starting from the top-left corner with the objective of reaching the goal state in the bottom-right corner. At each time step, the agent can choose to move up, down, left, or right; however, it moves only in the chosen direction with probability 𝑝 , otherwise moving in a random direction. Reaching the goal state yields a reward of 100, and intermediate transitions provide shaped rewards proportional to the agent’s proximity to the goal. The grid also contains a hazardous, terminal state in the centre, and entering it incurs a penalty of −100. In our experiments, we use two variants of the environment with different values of 𝑝 , 𝑝 = 0.9 (i.e., 90% chance of moving in the chosen direction) and 𝑝 = 0.4. 

## F.2 Frozen Lake 

The Frozen Lake MDP is adapted from PRISM [10, 16]. In this environment, the agent navigates a 4 × 4 grid to retrieve a frisbee, while avoiding a set of holes scattered across the grid. Since the original PRISM environment is formulated as a reachability problem, the environment has been converted to a reward-based setting, which yields a reward of 100 for successfully reaching the frisbee, and a penalty of −100 for falling into a hole. Both the goal state and states with holes are terminal. 

## F.3 Sepsis 

The Sepsis MDP [23] simulates patient trajectories in a sepsis treatment setting. At each time step, the patient’s state is defined by four vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen concentration, and glucose levels), each categorised as low, in normal range, or high. The RL agent controls three treatments (which can be switched on or off), with the goal of discharging the patient (which occurs when at least three of the vital signs are in normal range, and all three treatments are switched off). The patient dies if at least three vital signs are out of normal range at any time step. Rewards are shaped by the number of vital signs that are out of normal range, ranging from −1000 for patient death to 

+1000 for patient discharge. 

> 28

## F.4 Aircraft 

The Aircraft MDP is a navigation task, also adapted from PRISM [10, 16]. In this environment, the RL agent controls an aircraft that advances forward by one unit at each time step, and must choose to increase, decrease, or maintain its altitude, with a fixed probability of success. The goal is to successfully reach the right-hand side of the grid without colliding with another aircraft, which independently increases, decreases, or maintains its current altitude at each time step. Since the original PRISM environment is formulated as a reachability problem, it has been converted to a reward-based setting, which yields a reward of +100 for reaching the goal or a penalty of −100 for colliding with the other aircraft. Intermediate transitions receive a shaped reward inversely proportional to the proximity of the two aircraft. 

## F.5 System Specification 

Our experiments were run in parallel using 32 threads on a 128-core machine with an Intel Xeon CPU and 512 GB RAM. Simulation of IMDPs and construction of the corresponding CFIMDPs were implemented in Python 3.10, while our extended value iteration algorithm was implemented by extending the Julia Interval MDP package (v0.6.0) with the algorithm described in Appendix E.
