# Abstract 

We investigate multi-constraint policy optimization in constrained Markov deci-sion processes (CMDPs), where interactions among constraint gradients often yield ill-conditioned update directions, causing numerically fragile steps and un-intended cancellation or redundant overlap in constraint corrections. To address this, we propose LOCU, which mitigates numerical instability arising from con-straint interactions by applying symmetric Löwdin orthogonalization to the natural gradients of the reward and constraints under the Fisher–Rao metric. Combined with Fisher-geometric screening and near-collinear compression, LOCU yields a compact reduced space in which the trust region becomes a Euclidean ball and constraints are handled symmetrically through a low-dimensional active-set solve. For infeasible iterates, LOCU constructs a Pareto-descent direction which preserves the local feasibility of satisfied constraints within the same reduced formulation. Experiments show that LOCU remains stable under high constraint coupling, nar-row feasible regions, and infeasible starts, and generalizes across different cost critic architectures. Code is available at: GitHub Repository .

# 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning has achieved notable success in sequential decision making, but deployment in safety-critical domains requires policies to additionally satisfy explicit operational constraints rather than maximize reward alone [20, 21, 1, 2]. Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) formalize this requirement by optimizing return subject to cost limits. In practical scenario, agents could face several constraints simultaneously, such as velocity, collision, or resource limit in au-tonomous driving or robotic control. Existing safe policy-optimization methods commonly proposed and evaluated in single-constraint regimes, and their behavior can degrade when multiple constraints interact. The core difficulty with multi-constraint is not merely computational overhead, but the interaction geometry among constraints [18]. Existing approaches expose one or more of following failures (i) unstable updates under near-dependent constraint gradients or severe scale disparity, (ii) weakened effective updates under constraint interaction, caused by cancellation, dominance by a single direction, or projection-order dependence, and (iii) inefficient feasibility recovery after constraint violations,

as noted in prior work [14].The problem worsens under many or near-parallel constraints, making stability depend on hyperparameter tuning rather than geometry-consistent structure. A geometric perspective clarifies why these issues arise and how to address them. Policy opti-mization can be analyzed on a Riemannian manifold whose metric arises as the second-order local approximation of the KL divergence, known as the Fisher–Rao metric [4, 3]. Reward and constraint objectives define tangent directions in natural-gradient coordinates [12], and each linearized constraint induces a halfspace normal to its constraint gradient. With multiple constraints, feasible updates lie in the intersection of these halfspaces and the local Fisher-metric ellipsoid [1, 28]. Redundant or ill-conditioned constraint directions under this metric make both first- and second-order updates fragile and conservative, an effect amplified by finite-sample noise or critic error. Building on this geometric insight, we introduce the Löwdin Orthogonalized Constraint Update (LOCU), a second-order framework for multi-constraint RL that mitigates constraint coupling in Fisher-consistent coordinates. LOCU constructs a Fisher-orthonormal basis from the natural gradients of the reward and constraints via symmetric Löwdin orthogonalization [17]. This transformation maps the Fisher-metric ellipsoid into a spherical trust region in a reduced Euclidean space, making constraint interactions explicit and numerically stable. For large constraint sets, LOCU employs Fisher-geometric screening to prune non-binding or redundant constraints. Furthermore, it handles infeasibility with a geometry-aligned recovery step that seeks to reduce violations while preserving satisfied constraints in linearized local model. The update operates purely at the policy-optimization layer, ensuring compatibility with various critic architectures. We conduct experiments with multi-constrained tasks of [8, 15] applying the constraint configurations introduced by [29]. Evaluations under narrow feasible regions and strong constraint coupling, LOCU satisfies all constraints while limiting return drops to around 21% in both settings, compared to 26–49% and 13–32% drops with frequent violations in baselines, performing consistently from infeasible starts and generalizing across different cost critic architectures. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in maintaining stable policy optimization by symmetrically handling constraint interactions under the Fisher–Rao geometry. 

# 2 Related Work 

Existing constrained RL methods differ in how they incorporate multiple constraints into policy optimization and maintain feasibility during actor updates. These design choices become tightly coupled with the representation of constraint interactions. When constraint interactions are not handled in a geometry-consistent manner under the Fisher–Rao metric, stable learning often relies heavily on environment-specific tuning of numerous algorithmic choices and hyperparameters. 

Primal-dual Methods. Lagrangian-based methods adopt a primal-dual formulation where con-straints are managed via Lagrange multipliers co-optimized with policy parameters [23]. However, with multi-constraint, these methods aggregate costs from multi constraint into a scalar, obscuring the directional structure of individual constraint gradients. Depending on alignment, constraint gradients may cancel and suppress effective update, or reinforce and destabilize multiplier dynamics, causing oscillations and training instability. As the number of constraints increases, performance becomes increasingly sensitive to multiplier initialization and tuning can be “as hard as solving the RL problem itself” [19]. Recent work has attempted to address gradient-level interference in multi-constraint settings by analyzing directional interactions and applying shaping rules to improve update consistency [29]. While this mitigates some interference issues within the Lagrangian framework, its reliance on Euclidean angle-based similarity may not faithfully reflect the true correlations under the Fisher– Rao geometry [12]. Furthermore, it does not alleviate the burden of tuning an expanding set of hyperparameters as constraints grow. A separate line of works handle dual variables by solving a quadratic program (QP) or projection defined by linearized constraints within a KL-based trust region [1]. In multi-constraint regimes, feasibility recovery is either not explicitly addressed once iterates leave the local feasible region [1], or handled via repeated constraint-wise projections that shrink the effective reward step and introduce imbalances as the number of constraints grows [28]. Moreover, the active set is unknown a priori, these updates rely on generic QP solvers. Under linear dependence or near-collinearity 2among constraint gradients, the resulting QP can be ill-conditioned, leading to solver failures or high-variance solutions where it is difficult to distinguish intrinsic constraint degeneracy from solver-induced numerical errors. Recent works in risk-constraint RL proposed gradient integration mechanisms to manage infeasibility start and recovery in multi-constraint settings and further extend these ideas toward global convergence guarantees [14, 15]. Despite these advances, general applicability across constrained RL is largely limited to gradient integration, while the absence of a geometry-consistent formulation and continued dependence on generic solvers hinder scalability and stability as the number of constraints and interaction grows. 

Primal Methods. Primal methods can be naturally extended to multi-constraint settings by switch-ing between reward optimization and constraint minimization [26]. However, as the number of constraints increases, handling one constraint at a time leads to slower convergence and inconsistent updates. Furthermore, substantial correlations between adaptive weights and stochastic gradients are known to bias the update step and cause convergence failure [31]. To address such instabilities, recent work proposes a soft-switching strategy [9], which reduces oscillations and accelerates convergence by removing conflicting components between reward and constraint gradients. While it improves stability in single-constraint scenarios, effectiveness in multi-constraint settings remains unverified. For a geometric discussion of multi-constraint update pathologies in representative primal–dual and projection-based methods, see Appendix C.1.

3.5 Symmetric Löwdin orthogonalization 

When working with multiple update directions, it is often useful to replace a correlated basis by an orthonormal one in a way that is symmetric and insensitive to the ordering of directions. A classical construction is the symmetric orthogonalization of Löwdin [17]. Let S = [ s1, . . . , s p] ∈ Rn×p be a matrix whose columns are linearly independent directions. Define the Gram matrix G := S⊤S ∈ Rp×p. Since rank( S) = p, we have G ≻ 0 and the principal inverse square root G−1/2 is well-defined. The symmetric Löwdin orthogonalization constructs 

E := S G −1/2 ∈ Rn×p, E⊤E = G−1/2S⊤S G −1/2 = I. (10) so the columns of E are orthonormal and span( E) = span( S).Unlike Gram–Schmidt, the Löwdin construction is symmetric: it depends only on G = S⊤S and does not privilege any particular column order. This order-invariant structure is the property used by LOCU when constructing reduced coordinates from reward and constraint directions.

Computing a natural gradient with conjugate gradient (CG) further requires Tcg iterations per gradient, each consisting of one or two backpropagations depending on the implementation. # 5 Evaluation and Results 

5.1 Tasks and Baselines Tasks We evaluated on two settings. First, we use Circle tasks from Bullet-Safety-Gym [8] with Ball, Car, and Drone agents, denoted as BC, CC, and DC, under the multi-constraint setting of [29]. The task is to follow a circular trajectory, where costs are incurred for leaving the boundary region, violating upper and lower velocity limits. We vary constraint thresholds and extend with redundancy variants [29] to control feasible-set geometry and induce near-collinear constraint directions. Second, we evaluate legged locomotion tasks introduced by [15] on the bipedal and quadrupedal robots Cassie (Agility Robotics) and Laikago (Unitree), respectively. The objective is to track a commanded velocity while enforcing three constraints on body balance, height, and foot contact timing. To further test heterogeneous constraint handling, we add a goal-navigation variant that retains the three locomotion-stability constraints and introduces three qualitatively different constraints on hazard contact, speed, and energy which was originally on the reward side. This variant requires the agent to reach a goal while avoiding hazards and satisfying operational limits. Both versions are initialized from infeasible states with simultaneous constraint violations under high-dimensional observations, making these tasks suitable for assessing feasibility recovery and compatibility with alternative critic types. For locomotion tasks, all methods use the same quantile-based cost critic. 

Baselines The baselines include the actor update of SDAC [14], denoted as SDAC(A), which uses a second-order trust-region QP update with gradient integration for recovery, while all other components are aligned with our setup. We also evaluate two primal methods, PCRPO [9] and CRPO [26], alongside PPO and TRPO Lagrangian with PID-style multiplier updates [23], each with and without gradient shaping (GS) [29], denoted as PPOL-GS, PPOL-V, TRPOL-GS, and TRPOL-V, respectively. For the final task, we further apply Clarabel, a modern interior-point conic solver [7], to both LOCU’s reduced QP and the full-dimensional QP. Detailed descriptions for both task and baselines are provided in Appendix B.

LOCU avoids this by screening out inactive or degenerate constraints, and constructing an orthogonal basis of the retained gradients. Despite some constraint violations, the Reduced QP variants which use LOCU’s orthogonal basis perform closest to LOCU in both return and constraint satisfaction. This suggests that LOCU’s orthogonal basis construction can be integrated into existing algorithms to enhance numerical stability, while its proposed active-set solver can be replaced with alternative solvers depending on computational and real-time requirements. We provide the additional robustness test on goal-object task with redundancy in Appendix C.4.3. 

# 6 Limitations and future works 

LOCU provides local feasibility control but no global convergence guarantee as other second order trust region methods [1]. A possible approach is to reinterpret KL-induced Fisher–Rao geometry through a Bregman-proximal mirror-descent perspective over the convex occupancy-measure CMDP, which may provide a route to primal-dual convergence analysis. Also, computing natural-gradient directions and the Fisher Gram matrix for all constraints may raise computational-efficiency concerns. However, as discussed in Appendix C.3.1, parallelized constraint processing can mitigate this issue by better utilizing GPU throughput. It may also be useful to extend natural gradient calculation with curvature approximations to further reduce computation of CG iters and Fisher-related functions. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced LOCU, a second-order framework for multi-constraint policy optimization that mitigates constraint coupling in a geometry consistent manner. By applying symmetric Löwdin orthogonalization to natural gradients under the Fisher–Rao metric, LOCU yields an orthonormal reduced coordinate system in which the KL trust region becomes a Euclidean ball and constraint interactions are handled in a numerically stable way. B.2 Implementation details B.2.1 Baseline Implementation SDAC, PID Lagrangian, and Gradient Shaping (GS). For SDAC, we use the official imple-mentation released by the original authors. For PID Lagrangian and Gradient Shaping (GS), we adopt the implementation built on top of the FSRL framework [16], as released by the authors of GS. Across these baselines, we align common experimental settings and remaining algorithm-specific components, including multiplier dynamics, and shaping rules, are kept identical to the respective authors’ implementations without modification. 

CRPO. We build CRPO on top of the CRPO implementation provided by OmniSafe [11] and extend it to the multi-constraint setting. When multiple constraints are violated simultaneously, we sample a single constraint to update the policy at each iteration. The sampling probability is proportional to the magnitude of constraint violation, so that more severely violated constraints are prioritized during training. 

PCRPO. To the best of our knowledge, no official public implementation of PCRPO is available, although the original paper reports that PCRPO is implemented and evaluated using the OmniSafe framework. We therefore implement PCRPO directly following the algorithmic description and the configuration settings reported in the paper. In particular, the appendix provides a configuration table that lists shared hyperparameter settings for PCRPO, including the use of CG iterations. Based on this specification, we implement PCRPO as a second-order primal method that computes natural-gradient style updates using a CG with its soft-switching method. 24 PCRPO uses two slack thresholds per constraint, denoted as an upper slack and a lower slack, to determine which objective is optimized at each update. We set these thresholds by scaling the corresponding constraint limit bk. In Bullet-Safety-Gym, we use an upper slack of 1.25 bk and a lower slack of 0.75 bk. In locomotion tasks, we use an upper slack of 1.0 bk and a lower slack of 

0.75 bk, applied per constraint. At each iteration, constraints are categorized into three groups based on their current costs relative to the slack thresholds: (i) constraints with cost above the upper slack, (ii) constraints with cost between the upper and lower slacks, and (iii) constraints with cost below the lower slack. If any constraint falls into group (i), one violated constraint is sampled from this group and a constraint-reduction update is performed. Otherwise, if group (ii) is non-empty, one constraint is sampled and a reward update is applied together with the gradient-manipulation rule specified by PCRPO to mitigate interference between reward and constraint gradients. If all constraints belong to group (iii), a feasible update is performed. 

Clarabel. We use Clarabel to solve the local constrained policy-update problem in two forms: full QP and reduced QP. The full QP optimizes the coefficients of the natural reward and cost gradients, while the reduced QP optimizes the update in LOCU’s Löwdin coordinates, including the orthogonal reward direction. For constraint recovery, the full QP minimizes total constraint slack when the reward-maximization problem is infeasible. The reduced QP minimizes slack for currently violated constraints while enforcing the other constraints.

C.2.2 Empirical comparison 

We evaluated LOCU on the BC-v3 Redun : 3 task by replacing Löwdin orthogonalization with Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization under three gradient orderings (original, random, and reverse), while injecting observation noise into each training batch following [16]. As shown in Figure 8, Löwdin orthogonalization generally performs below Gram–Schmidt index but above random and reverse, attributed to its symmetric distribution of perturbation-induced errors across gradient directions. In this task, the last three constraints in the indexed order are the loosest low-velocity constraints, and the differences among the Gram–Schmidt variants reflect their inherent ordering dependence. Under the indexed order, accumulated errors primarily propagate to these loose constraints, whereas under the reverse order, they affect the reward gradient and the tighter high-velocity constraints. The noise implementation of [16] adds perturbations of a fixed magnitude without accounting the relative scale of each observation. At the highest noise level of 0.25, all methods degrade substantially, while the pronounced failure of Gram–Schmidt-random suggests that Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization can be more vulnerable than Löwdin when large stochastic perturbations distort the gradient geometry. 30 Figure 8: Performance comparison of Löwdin and Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization under observa-tion noise on BC-v3 Redun 3.

C.3.2 Parallel processing 

To examine whether this bottleneck can be reduced, we construct a MuJoCo-JAX (MJX) [24] version of the CircleRun-v3 Redun task and implement LOCU, SDAC(A), CRPO, PPOLag, and TRPOLag with vectorized constraint processing and end-to-end XLA compilation. This targets a common limitation of Python-based safe RL implementations, where most computations are executed sequentially and therefore use only part of the available hardware throughput. Table 5 reports the 31 wall-clock time of the MJX implementation on PointCircle with redundancy levels 2, 3, and 5. The results show that full-batch second-order actor updates can be faster than minibatch-based first-order updates with parallel processing. LOCU, SDAC(A), CRPO, and TRPOL complete each run in roughly 10–11 minutes, whereas PPOLag takes 15–21 minutes depending on the minibatch size, denoted as bs in the table. As expected, LOCU and SDAC(A), which compute natural-gradient directions for all constraints, show a larger redundancy-dependent increase than CRPO and TRPOL, which only compute single natural-gradient. Under the MJX implementation, however, this increase is small: from redundancy level 2 to 5, LOCU increases from 10:19 to 10:44 and SDAC(A) from 10:55 to 11:19, i.e., less than 30 seconds while the number of constraints grows from 6 to 15. By contrast, in the Python-based implementation in Table 4, increasing from BC-v2 with two constraints to BC-v3 Redun 3 with nine constraints increases LOCU and SDAC(A) by roughly 7–8 minutes. Compared with the substantially larger cost of PPOLag, the remaining difference between LOCU and the single-natural-gradient methods can be considered minor. Overall, the results indicate that parallelized constraint processing substantially reduces wall-clock sensitivity to the number of constraints compared with sequential Python-based implementations. The same implementation strategy may therefore be useful for other multi-constraint safe RL methods or multi-objective settings. More broadly, the results weaken the view that second-order safe RL methods are impractical solely due to computational burden. When multiple constraints must be handled and constraint satisfaction is central to the task, LOCU or other trust-region-based second-order methods can remain practical options under parallel processing. 

C.3.3 Inverse fisher preconditioner 

Natural gradients are typically computed using conjugate gradient (CG), which needs to be applied separately to each gradient, the computational cost increases linearly with the number of constraints. An alternative option can be using a shared approximate Fisher inverse or preconditioner across all gradients. Since the FIM is defined solely by the outer product of policy score functions, independent of advantage estimates, layer-wise curvature approximations such as K-FAC and its variants can provide a single approximate inverse applicable to all reward and constraint gradients. Although these approaches can be more computationally efficient than using CG iterations depending on the network architecture and the number of constraints, further investigation is required to assess its approximation accuracy, numerical stability, and overall effectiveness in multi-constraint policy optimization. 

C.4 Additional Experiments 

We conduct additional experiments to evaluate LOCU from three perspectives. First, we examine the contribution of its algorithmic components through ablation studies. Second, we investigate hyperparameter sensitivity by varying the clustering threshold and KL limit. Finally, we evaluate robustness to redundant constraints by increasing the number of constraints while preserving the feasible region. 

C.4.1 Component Analysis 

We compare LOCU against two ablated variants and a CPO baseline. The two ablated variants in Figure 9 are labeled as w/o Lowdin and w/o Lowdin, Screening & compression . w/o Lowdin removes Löwdin orthogonalization and only normalizes each direction by its Fisher norm before applying the same reduced update. w/o Lowdin, Screening & compression further removes screening and clustering and uses all normalized constraint directions in the same update procedure. For the CPO baseline, we follow the original implementation where infeasibility and degenerate cases use a recovery direction formed by summing the gradients of currently violated constraints, and the resulting QP is solved with OSQP [22]. Removing Löwdin slows convergence relative to CPO and only recovers return late in training. When both Löwdin and screening with compression are removed, the early stage becomes highly seed dependent under simultaneous violations, and some runs finish with less than half of LOCU’s final return. C.4.2 Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

We test the cosine similarity threshold εdup that controls near-collinear clustering in Section 4.2. Next, we evaluate sensitivity to the trust region radius against SDAC(A) on the BC-v3 task. As the limit size decreases, a smaller KL limit generally reduces approximation error but yields less policy improvement per update. C.4.3 Robustness to Redundant Constraints 

To evaluate robustness to a larger number of constraints without changing the feasible region, we introduce redundant constraint variants to the goal-object Cassie task. For each base constraint, we keep the original tight threshold and add two looser variants, increasing the total number of constraints from 6 to 18. Figure 13 compares the final evaluation results between the original 6-constraint Cassie task and the redundant 18-constraint version. LOCU preserves reward under 3× constraint scaling while satisfying all constraints at convergence. Figure 20 further shows that the learning dynamics remain stable after adding redundant constraints, indicating that the additional constraints do not substantially degrade optimization. Table 6 reports the screening statistics during training in the 18-constraint setting. Even with 18 nominal constraints, only a small subset is active at each update, and redundancy compression further reduces the basis dimension. By the end of training, the average active set size is 4.9 and the average basis dimension is 2.8, so the practical optimization remains compact.
