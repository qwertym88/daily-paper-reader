# SOMA: Stack-of-Objects Manipulation using a Quadruped Robot 

Stergios E. Bachoumas, IEEE Student Member and Panagiotis Artemiadis ∗, IEEE Senior Member 

Abstract — Executing heavy-duty physical manipulation in unpredictable environments remains a major challenge for quadrupedal robots lacking dedicated manipulators. This paper presents SOMA, a hierarchical reinforcement learning frame-work for whole-body non-prehensile manipulation of multi-object stacks using a quadruped robot. Unlike traditional methods restricted to two-dimensional planar pushing, SOMA integrates explicit robot base-height control, unlocking full three-dimensional pushing strategies. This capability enables the quadruped to manipulate complex payload stacks as a unified system while preventing relative sliding or tipping of upper layers. The architecture factorizes control into ahigh-level manipulation policy using a Simplified Structured State Space Sequence (S5) encoder to reason over long-horizon temporal history and predict bounded target tracking com-mands via Beta distributions, and a low-level locomotion policy that dynamically adapts leg stance configurations. In extensive simulation benchmarks, SOMA achieves a 90 .3% success rate for single-object reposing and up to 90 .1% for dynamic two-box stacks under stringent error margins ( 0.05 m position error, 

1° orientation error). Finally, we validate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot across diverse payload masses and geometries, demonstrating active base-height adjustments to maintain traction and balance during heavy pushing tasks. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent work on quadrupedal locomotion has enabled sig-nificant maneuverability across diverse and complex terrains [1, 2], and has also enabled gymnastic-style maneuvers such as a running backflip [3]. However, these platforms still exhibit limitations when executing tasks that require close physical interaction with the environment. Truly autonomous robotic systems must physically interact with their environ-ment and thus act as mobile manipulators [4]. Recently, the robotics community has increased efforts in this direction, with promising work combining locomotion and manipula-tion for quadruped robots, commonly referred to as loco-manipulation. Unlike bipeds, quadrupedal platforms inherently lack ded-icated end-effectors for physical interaction. Nevertheless, in [5–8], the authors used pedipulation, i.e., manipulation using the legs of a quadruped as an on-demand manipulator, to accomplish tasks such as opening spring-loaded doors, pushing lightweight objects out of the way, doing laundry, pushing switches, and even collecting rock samples in the wild. While these strategies prove effective for versatile manipulation, most prior work [6, 8] strictly constrains the robot’s mobility by prioritizing base static stability, with the notable exceptions of [5, 7]. To overcome this limitation, a prominent body of work has successfully integrated robotic manipulators onto quadruped bases, achieving significant progress in mobile prehensile

SOMA reasons about contact height and physical parameters to push stacked payloads without destabilizing them during movement. loco-manipulation [5, 9–15]. Despite these advancements, dedicated manipulator-base systems face severe physical constraints when deployed in complex environments. The increased spatial footprint and payload capacity limits of mounted arms hinder operation in tightly confined spaces and restrict the maximum load the system can manipulate. This gap becomes particularly critical in search-and-rescue operations, where robots must navigate narrow passages and interact with heavy, unstable debris under shifting dy-namic loads. Consequently, achieving heavy-duty physical manipulation within highly constrained, unpredictable, and hazardous environments remains an open challenge. Quadrupedal robots can manipulate objects without dedi-cated arms either by deploying individual limbs or by lever-aging their entire body for pushing interactions. While limb-based manipulation enables fine control over lightweight objects, whole-body pushing provides a compelling mech-anism for transporting heavy or bulky payloads. Although non-prehensile pushing was historically underexplored [16], foundational work by Mason established the underlying mechanics and motion planning challenges of contact-driven manipulation [17]. Modern optimization-based techniques have since made this problem more tractable by formu-lating pushing as a multi-contact planning problem [18]. Recently, these optimization frameworks have been extended to quadrupedal platforms to enable body-pushing behaviors [19, 20]. However, planning through contact remains inher-ently difficult [17], and current optimization methods rely heavily on pre-specified environmental models and fixed robot parameters. As a result, existing controllers struggle arXiv:2610.20968v1 [cs.RO] 14 Oct 2026 to generalize across diverse or unpredictable environments, leaving generalizable, whole-body pushing locomanipulation an open challenge. To address these limitations, we leverage learning-based paradigms [21, 22] and propose SOMA, a novel frame-work designed for whole-body object-stack manipulation on quadrupedal platforms (see Fig. 1). Built upon a hierar-chical reinforcement learning architecture [22, 23], SOMA decouples control into a high-level manipulation planner that commands a low-level locomotion policy. We extend prior work by incorporating explicit control over the robot’s base height, unlocking full three-dimensional pushing strate-gies. This vertical degrees-of-freedom integration enables the quadruped to intelligently manipulate and reconfigure complex stacks of objects from arbitrary initial states to target poses. The contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:  

> •

A learning-based framework for quadruped whole-body manipulation of a stack of objects. Our approach incor-porates direct robot base height commands, enabling full 3D pushing strategies—a crucial component miss-ing from previous methods that restrict the robot to a predefined height and, consequently, to 2D planar pushing strategies.  

> •

The proposed policy successfully reasons about la-tent physical parameters of the stack—such as ob-ject mass, geometry, inter-object friction, and ground-surface friction—thereby enabling robust generalization across diverse problem instances.  

> •

We evaluate the robustness of our method through ex-tensive simulations. The proposed framework achieves a success rate of 90 .3% when reposing a single box-shaped object and 90 .1% when manipulating a dou-ble box stack, consistently meeting stringent accuracy thresholds of 1 ° in orientation error and 0.05 m in position error.  

> •

Finally, we validate the efficacy of our proposed method in the real world by deploying it on a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot. We run an extensive suite of exper-iments by varying the geometry, mass, and number of objects in the stack the robot must rest on in the real world. II. M ETHODS

B. Hierarchical Policy Architecture 

Each phase of the target task demands distinct plan-ning strategies, sensing modalities, and temporal scales. For instance, the Approach Phase is a pure locomotion task governed primarily by proprioceptive feedback xt and the stack’s location. Conversely, the Pushing and Final Stages require coordinating robot ego-motion with the dynamics of the object stack, presenting a challenging whole-body ma-nipulation problem for the combined underactuated system. While monolithic policies often struggle to balance such competing locomotion and manipulation objectives [22], this multi-stage breakdown naturally motivates a modular design. We propose a hierarchical reinforcement learning scheme that factorizes the policy space into high- and low-level components ( Π ≡ Πlow × Πhigh ) mapping the previously defined observation space to execution actions.

During deterministic inference, we use the mode of the distribution directly. Crucially, modeling πhigh using a Beta distribution pro-vides a distinct mathematical advantage over standard Gaus-sian (Normal) formulations for bounded continuous con-trol. While Gaussian policies during training require post-sampling artificial clipping (e.g., using a bounding function) to enforce physical limits, this clipping alters the true distri-bution, causing a disproportionate accumulation of probabil-ity mass at the boundaries. This boundary bias reduces policy gradients near the operational edges, causing optimization to stall. By contrast, the Beta distribution natively restricts its support to the compact interval [0 , 1] , eliminating the need for external clipping, removing boundary bias, and ensuring stable policy gradient updates.

> •

Action Rate (rrate ): Penalizes large variations in joint commands between consecutive discrete timesteps to bound joint acceleration and ensure control smoothness.  

> •

Nominal Joint Deviation (rdefault ): Penalizes the Eu-clidean distance between current joint positions and the default stance configuration to minimize energetic expenditures during steady-state locomotion.  

> •

> •

> •

Object Tilt (rupright ): Enforces rigid stack stability by penalizing absolute deviations of the manipulated pay-load’s roll ϕbox and pitch θbox angles relative to the gravity vector.  

> •

Stack Alignment Deviation (rstack align ): Evaluates the horizontal geometric slip between nested layers of the multi-body object stack by tracking the maximum Chebyshev ( L∞) norm of the planar position offset between any two adjacent stacked elements.  

> •

Stack Layer Slip Acceleration (rstack acc ): Suppresses structural shear forces and non-rigid sliding by penal-izing the maximum magnitude of the relative linear acceleration vector between the base links of adjacent stacked bodies.

For control-system training, we use Proximal Policy Op-timization (PPO) [27] in three stages. Because we train the policies in simulation, we use domain randomization at each stage to ensure the system generalizes well to different objects and physical properties in the real world. 

1) Stage 1: Locomotion Command Tracking: In the first stage, only the low-level locomotion policy is trained to track random locomotion commands. At the start of each episode, a random target locomotion command is generated and then resampled every 4 s. To make the policy robust against payloads, we sample a robot base link mass variation in the range [−1, 3] kg from its nominal mass. To improve the pol-icy’s disturbance-rejection capabilities, we apply simulated velocity disturbances. Specifically, we sample linear velocity disturbances in the range [−0.5, 0.5] m/s for the forward and lateral directions and [−0.4, 0.4] m/s for the vertical direction of the robot. For the angular velocity disturbances, we sample for all main axes in the range [−45 , 45] °/s. The disturbances are applied to the base link every 1 − 3 s [28]. Finally, to facilitate sim-to-real transfer, we inject synthetic additive Gaussian noise into the locomotion policy’s observation xt.

2) Stage 2: Single-Object Manipulation: In the second stage, we freeze the low-level policy parameters and train the high-level manipulation policy using a single object (box). The robot’s task is to push the box to a goal pose with position error less than 0.05 m and orientation error less than 

1°. To encourage policy generalization, we randomly sample each dimension of the box independently from the range 

[0 .35 , 0.45] m at the start of each episode. We also sample the box mass uniformly from [6 , 10] kg, which remains constant for the duration of the episode. Crucially, we also randomize the sliding friction coefficient between the box and the ground, sampling it uniformly from [0 .3, 1.5] at the start of each episode. 

3) Stage 3: Stacked-Object Manipulation: The task is then extended to a stack of two box-shaped objects. The top object is unconstrained and free to move or slide relative to the bottom object. The robot must complete the same re-posing task as in Stage 2, but with the added requirement of keeping the stack stable, i.e., the top object must always remain on top of the bottom object.

We evaluate our framework’s capabilities in both sim-ulation and the real world. We measure the success rate on the following task: re-posing objects with a quadruped robot under various conditions, including different object shapes and inertial parameters. We also evaluate our system’s effectiveness by comparing its performance against several baseline methods in simulation. The training process was conducted in Mjlab [29] on a computer equipped with a 12th Gen Intel ® Core ™ i9-12900K processor featuring 24 cores, as well as an NVIDIA ® RTX A5500 GPU. The overall training process takes approximately 1 hour and 20 minutes. This is a notable reduction from the previous state of the art, which reported 15 hours of total training time [22].

B. Efficacy of the proposed Training Strategy 

To evaluate both architectural choices and the proposed multi-size training strategy, we compare our model (S5 SOMA) against three baselines: a memoryless policy using only a Multi-Layer-Perceptron (MLP), a policy using astandard Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) [30] as its memory module, and a policy using a Simplified Structured State Space Sequence (S5) memory module [26, 31, 32]. The three baseline models were trained exclusively on fixed 0.4×0.4×

0.35 m boxes with masses sampled from [6 , 10] kg. As shown in Fig. 3, memory-augmented policies consis-tently outperform the memoryless MLP baseline across all conditions, demonstrating that temporal history is essential for reliable non-prehensile manipulation. Furthermore, the S5 state-space encoder systematically outperforms the GRU, highlighting its superior capacity to capture long-horizon temporal dependencies in contact-rich tasks. The policy with the S5 memory module outperformed the remaining baselines. To this end, we implement Stage 2 of SOMA using only the S5 model as its core component. We train a model, called S5 SOMA, on environments with boxes shaped from 0.35 × 0.35 × 0.35 m to 0.45 × 0.45 × 0.45 macross the same mass range [ 6, 10 kg]. Comparing training strategies, our method (S5 SOMA) yields substantial performance gains on out-of-distribution box geometries, particularly under light payloads (4 kg) where single-box baselines degrade significantly. C. Stack-of-Objects Evaluation 

To evaluate the framework’s capacity for multi-object stack manipulation ( Problem 1 ), we established a three-stage experimental pipeline: environment redesign, policy fine-tuning, and downstream evaluation. First, we modified the simulation environment by adding a secondary object atop the base object to form a dynamic two-tier stack. Second, we resumed policy optimization using the top-performing single-object weights for warm-start initialization, transferring foun-dational pushing primitives. To promote structural stability during physical interaction, we augmented the reward for-mulation with penalty terms for stack alignment deviation (rstack align ) and inter-layer slip acceleration ( rslip acc ). We evaluated this fine-tuned policy across nine scenarios featuring two-cube stacks of identical dimensions. The evalu-ation matrix spans three cube side lengths ( 0.3 m, 0.4 m, and 

0.5 m) and three base-object masses ( 4 kg, 6 kg, and 8 kg), while the mass of the top object was held constant at 4 kg. We benchmarked each configuration across 500 parallelized simulation environments with an episode horizon of 100 s. As detailed in Table II, the framework achieved a peak task completion rate of 90 .1% with a 0.4 m object side length and an 8 kg base mass ( Mbottom = 8 kg , M top = 4 kg). Under this optimal configuration, structural stack failures dropped to 1.0% , with the remaining 8.9% of uncompleted trials (%Other) predominantly caused by episode timeouts. Because this work focuses on learning the loco-manipulation policy rather than state estimation, we used motion capture to provide ground-truth pose measure-ments used throughout our framework. In the absence of external tracking infrastructure, onboard perception sensors could be integrated to localize the robot and target objects. We evaluated five experimental configurations, varying stack geometry, total stack weight, and goal poses (detailed in Table III). We considered an experiment successful if the stack did not topple and the final pose of the bottom-most object landed within a tolerance of 0.05 m and 5 ° of the goal. The controller autonomously terminated the experiment when it identified that no further progress could be made. This emergent behavior significantly enhances autonomy, preventing the policy from getting trapped in a cycle of endless micro-adjustments that risk stack destabilization. In this work, we presented SOMA, a learning-based frame-work for quadrupedal loco-manipulation of heavy, multi-body object stacks. We validated its effectiveness through comprehensive simulation evaluations and zero-shot hard-ware deployment on a Unitree Go1 quadruped. Future work will extend this framework along three main vectors: in-tegrating spatial workspace constraints for narrow-passage motion planning, replacing external tracking with onboard perception for field deployment in unstructured environ-ments, and incorporating online parameter estimation (e.g., mass, friction, and stiffness) to dynamically tailor interaction strategies.
