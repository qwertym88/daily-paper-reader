# Validation of Human Motor Development Scales with Humanoid Robots 

Fanxing Meng 1 and Jing Xiao 1

Abstract — We investigate how humanoid robots can learn a series of motor skills defined by human infant motor develop-ment scales, and we evaluate which scales are comprehensive enough to serve as self-contained guides for developing gross motor skills up to standing and walking. We represent each motor development scale as a developmental graph that guides the training of a humanoid robot. We then apply this graph within a reinforcement learning framework so that the robot learns skills following the natural phases of human motor de-velopment described by the scale. Our work demonstrates that graphs derived from three out of four popular pediatric motor scales can successfully guide humanoid robots to develop these skills. The fourth scale misses some intermediate milestones that the other scales have, which causes some failures. After adding a missing milestone in its graph, it also enables the robot to develop those skills successfully. Video available at 

https://youtu.be/SR3LUVhohV4 .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Human infants acquire motor skills in a predictable order: rolling precedes sitting, kneeling precedes crawling, and standing precedes walking. Developmental scientists have captured this progression in standardized motor scales that list milestones and the typical order in which they appear. These scales are used clinically to detect delays in infant development, but they also reflect something deeper: a struc-tured curriculum in which simpler skills provide the physical and coordinative prerequisites for more complex ones. For humanoid robots, standing up and walking from an arbitrary fallen state remains difficult. Most learning-based approaches either imitate reference trajectories, which must be captured, cleaned, and retargeted for each skill, or require carefully designed reward functions and regularization terms with hand-tuned coefficients. An alternative is to utilize the curriculum that developmental science has already discov-ered. Our hypothesis is that if a developmental scale correctly orders human motor learning, it should also guide humanoid robot motor learning, provided the robot’s body is sufficiently human-like. This paper tests that hypothesis. We take four widely used pediatric scales: the Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS) [1], the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (BSID) [2], the Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM) [3], and the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales (PDMS) [4]. We translate each into a developmental graph that directly encodes the milestone sequence and prerequisite structure described in the scale. We evaluate the four graphs on a simulated 21-DoF humanoid robot in Isaac Lab [5]. Three of the four scales

II. R ELATED WORK 

This section introduces the developmental scales used in this research, as well as notable ideas from developmental robotics and curriculum learning. 

A. Developmental Scales 

AIMS is a norm-referenced observational assessment of infant gross motor development from birth to independent walking [1]. It evaluates four positions: supine, prone, sitting, and standing, as well as the transitions between them. AIMS is designed to detect motor delay by comparing an infant’s observed skills to age norms. BSID is a broad assessment of cognitive, language, and motor development in young children [2]. The motor scale includes items from prone control and sitting through crawl-ing, standing, and walking. BSID’s progression is similar to that of AIMS but with fewer intermediate milestones, making it a test of whether a sparser developmental graph can still guide a robot to walking. GMFM was developed to evaluate changes in gross motor function in children with cerebral palsy [3]. It organizes skills into five dimensions: lying and rolling, sitting, crawling and kneeling, standing, and walking/running/jumping. Because GMFM was built to track functional improvement, its mile-stones are explicitly ordered by difficulty and prerequisite. This makes it particularly natural to translate into a robot curriculum. PDMS assesses gross and fine motor skills in children from birth to five years [4]. Its gross-motor subtest includes reflexes, stationary skills, locomotion, and object manipula-tion. Unlike AIMS, BSID, and GMFM, the PDMS gross-motor pathway contains only one upright transition (sitting to standing) without the intermediate half-kneeling or high-kneeling milestones that appear in the other scales. 

B. Developmental Robotics 

Developmental robotics uses robots to model cognitive and motor development [6]. The field focuses on autonomous, arXiv:2610.25701v1 [cs.RO] 19 Oct 2026 open-ended learning [7]. It draws on principles such as embodiment (how the body shapes learning) [8], intrinsic motivation [9], and the shift from egocentric (self-centered) to object-centric views [10]. A key concept is Piaget’s theory of developmental stages, in which new abilities are built on mastered ones [11]. This implies that the sequence of learning is just as important as the skills themselves. While studies have applied these principles to simpler robots, it remains difficult to create a framework that lets a complex humanoid robot progress through a full trajectory from helplessness to functional behavior. 

C. Curriculum Learning 

Curriculum learning structures training from easy to hard to improve efficiency [12]. In robotics, this takes various forms. Some methods hide difficult goals initially [13] or generate intermediate goals [14]. Others break the learning process into stages [15] or sequence discrete subtasks [16], [17]. A third category changes the environment, such as starting the agent in states progressively farther from the goal [18], [19] or slowly increasing terrain difficulty [20]. The main goal of these techniques is usually to speed up convergence for a specific, single task. III. M ETHOD 

Our method converts each scale into a developmental graph that guides a reinforcement learning (RL) strategy for a humanoid robot to acquire related motor skills. 

A. Developmental Graphs 

Each pediatric scale is translated into a developmental graph, as shown in Fig. 1. The master graph contains one node per gross milestone (e.g., prone, sitting, kneeling, standing, walking). 1 Directed edges represent prerequisite conditions or pose transitions defined by the scale. 2

We implement the developmental graph as an achievement-gating reward structure: a reward node can contribute to the total reward only after at least one of its parent nodes has been achieved once in the current episode. In RL terms, a node is activated when at least one parent’s reward value exceeds a passing score, even briefly. Because upright milestones are mechanically composite, we further decompose them into mini-graphs, as shown in Fig. 2. For example, standing is represented as a sequence of force-propagation conditions: both feet must be flat on the ground, both lower legs must be upright, both upper legs must be upright, the torso must be upright, and the arms should be brought in. Each condition is a node in the standing mini-graph, and the conditions must be satisfied in order. A node in a mini-graph is activated only when all of its internal predecessors are simultaneously above their passing scores; this forces the robot to maintain, not merely visit, the supporting posture.

B. Reward Functions 

The reward definition for each node is based on the poses graphed in AIMS; a few drawings are reproduced in Fig. 3 for illustration. For static poses, each reward term is the cosine similarity between the depicted orientation of a body part and its actual orientation, so perfect alignment yields 

+1 and the opposite orientation yields −1.For dynamic motions, the reward term is the robot’s velocity along the relevant torso axis described in the scale: for example, crawling and creeping are rewarded along the torso’s superior axis, while walking and scooting are rewarded along the torso’s anterior axis. The same canonical pose definitions are reused when a milestone appears in more than one scale. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We implemented the four developmental graphs in Isaac Lab. The robot is a 21-DoF humanoid model based on the Noetix Bumi robot, with torque-controlled joints. Training uses 4,096 parallel environments on a single consumer-grade GPU (less than 8 GB of VRAM), with each run limited to 1,250 episodes and taking less than 12 GPU-hours. Each episode lasts 10 seconds at a 50 Hz control frequency. The policy is a feed-forward multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with layers [800 , 400 , 200 , 100] trained with proximal policy op-timization (PPO) [21]. Domain randomization includes mass scaling, friction scaling, observation noise, action noise, and periodic gravity perturbations. Table III lists the simulation and randomization hyper-parameters. The action vector is 21-dimensional and represents normalized joint torque commands which are multiplied by each joint’s maximum torque and the current torque scale before being applied. Besides the developmental graph, our training setup uses three implementation choices borrowed from prior work in developmental and reinforcement learning [22], [23], [24]. First, the positions, orientations, and velocities of the robot links are expressed relative to the robot’s own root frame affixed to the pelvis. Second, the observation includes sixteen time-varying sinusoids to mimic the effect of central pattern generator signals. Third, the available joint torque is scaled linearly from zero at the start of each episode to the full motor limit over the first half of the episode. These are training aids taken from earlier studies; the developmental graph is the only component studied in this paper. We trained each developmental graph at four passing scores (50%, 60%, 70%, and 80%). A rollout was considered successful for standing if the robot maintained a torso-upright posture with head height above a threshold for at least 100 consecutive steps, and successful for walking if its forward speed exceeded 0.5 m/s. V. R ESULTS 

Robots trained under the AIMS, BSID, and GMFM graphs learned the full progression from supine lying to standing and walking under all four tested passing-score settings. Fig. 5 shows representative snapshots of the learned standing-up motion for each graph at the 50% setting. The PDMS graph did not learn sustained standing at the 50% setting or at the 80% setting. This is consistent with the structure of the PDMS gross-motor pathway: it defines a direct sitting-to-standing transition but omits the half-kneeling and high-kneeling intermediates that appear in AIMS, BSID, and GMFM. Without those intermediate support postures, the reward landscape contains a large mechanical gap that the policy could not bridge, and the robot falls back to a scooting pose. To test whether this structural difference explains the failure, we inserted the half-kneeling milestone, together with its mini-graph, between the kneeling and standing nodes of the PDMS graph and retrained at all four passing scores, leaving every other setting unchanged. The augmented graph learned to stand and walk at the 50% passing score, where the original PDMS graph failed. It still did not succeed at the 60%, 70%, or 80% settings. This test supports the explanation that the kneeling bridge is an important structural feature for getting up from the ground. At the same time, it shows that adding a single milestone does not by itself make a sparse curriculum robust across all passing-score settings. Table IV reports the success rate of standing without falling and the final walking speed. Table V compares the time-to-skill statistics of the four graphs under different passing-score settings. Success rates do not simply increase with passing score. Under domain randomization, some robots in the population have limb masses or action noise that make it hard to reach the near-perfect orientations required by a high passing score. When a prerequisite milestone does not beat the threshold, downstream milestones are never unlocked, so those robots cannot progress. This is why the highest passing score sometimes gives a lower success rate than a moderate one, and why some cells in Table V are empty at higher passing scores even though the same milestone was learned at a lower threshold. This also shows that some parts of skill development are overlapping or concurrent in practice: setting a passing score too high can prevent the robot from developing related skills together. Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 illustrate the progression of rewards and achievement scores for each milestone during typical training episodes. The results suggest that the presence of a kneeling-to-standing bridge is an important structural feature for learning upright locomotion from the ground up. The supplemental video further shows that the kneel-forward milestone in the GMFM graph allows the robot to learn an efficient alternating gait even though no explicit reward or regularization term was provided for gait quality. Table VI shows the 5th–95th percentiles of time to mile-stone achievement when executing a trained model on a large population of robots with randomized physical and mechanical properties. The small variance shows that the developmental-graph approach allows the robot to achieve milestones at roughly the same time across all robots, sug-gesting that the learned policy can systematically perform a chain of actions rather than relying on random exploration. VI. C ONCLUSIONS 

We showed that developmental graphs derived from hu-man pediatric motor scales can guide a simulated humanoid robot to stand up and walk without reference trajectories (e.g., from learning by demonstration) and with no need for hand-tuning reward weights. Three of the four scales we tested succeeded under all four tested training settings. The fourth scale failed at the lowest and highest tested passing scores, and a targeted test in which we inserted the missing half-kneeling milestone shows that this structural gap is a likely cause of the failure. This validates both the feasibility of using clinical developmental scales for humans as robot curricula and the specific milestone structure they prescribe. (a) AIMS

This framework is novel and practical in that it leverages the human motor developmental scales to enable systematic robot motor skill learning rather than the prevailing litera-ture of robot RL through large and random exploration of motion data guided by human demonstration. As such, this framework does not require ad hoc tuning of parameters, enables automatic determination of reward weight assign-ment from graph levels, and uses decomposition of complex milestones into force-propagation mini-graphs. Future work can examine other multiplier growth-rate schemes, combine the training setup with robot morphology changes, extend this approach to finer motor skills, and transfer it to the real world.
