## ABSTRACT 

Understanding loss landscapes is central to explaining neural-network training, yet their structure remains only partially understood even in simple models. We study the Gaussian population loss of shallow, bias-free ReLU networks in the teacher– student setting. This provides a simple model for studying essential aspects such as feature learning and overparameterization. For teacher networks with positive output weights and planar features, we show that including a learned linear skip removes all spurious local minima with non-negative student output weights once the student network is at least as wide as the teacher network. In contrast, without the skip, we construct a fixed teacher network with positive output weights and only three hidden neurons in input dimension two whose spurious local minima persist at every student width at least three. Thus, a learned linear skip can remove spurious minima that persist under arbitrary overparameterization. Furthermore, we show that a positive output weight student network always learns the subspace spanned by the teacher features: student features at local minima with non-negative student output weights lie in the span of the teacher features. For ReLU networks in two dimensions, even heavily overparameterized student networks have effective width controlled by the teacher width: every critical point with positive student output weights has at most twice as many distinct student feature directions as teacher neurons. Finally, we transfer the benignity result to empirical minima over parameter balls of any prescribed radius, with the required sampling accuracy depending on that radius. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Training a neural network typically requires solving a highly non-convex optimization problem, and understanding the corresponding loss landscape is a central problem in machine learning. Overpa-rameterization and adding residual connections are both associated with easier training. Sufficient overparameterization enables convergence guarantees for gradient descent from random initialization under suitable assumptions (Du et al., 2019). Empirical studies show improved training with residual connections (He et al., 2016a;b) and two-dimensional loss visualization suggest less rugged loss landscapes with residual connections (Li et al., 2018). We ask when increasing width or adding a linear skip connection actually excludes spurious local minima. Teacher–student models provide a standard setting for studying loss landscapes and training dynamics (Tian, 2017; Safran & Shamir, 2018; Arjevani & Field, 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). When the student network architecture contains the teacher network’s, the problem is realizable: zero population loss is attainable. Moreover, it is a simple model to study essential aspects such as feature learning and overparameterization. The latter can be modeled by allowing the student network to have more hidden neurons than the teacher network. For a shallow teacher network without a skip, labels depend only on the input’s projection onto the span of its feature directions , namely the normalized non-zero input-weight vectors of its hidden neurons. It is therefore a particular multi-index model and provides a controlled setting for asking whether training identifies the target-relevant subspace. Prior work studies such subspace learning through gradient-based training (Damian et al., 2022; Mousavi-Hosseini et al., 2023); this leaves open to what extent subspace learning is a property of the optimization dynamics or of the loss landscape itself. 1arXiv:2610.01728v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 Under review Overparameterization also raises the question whether additional neurons learn distinct features or become redundant. We measure effective width by the number of distinct feature directions among neurons with non-zero contribution, and ask whether criticality alone bounds it, even when the student network does not fit the teacher network. Realizability alone does not exclude spurious local minima: for shallow ReLU networks with Gaussian inputs they exist even for teacher networks with orthogonal feature directions (Safran & Shamir, 2018), in families whose Hessian spectra are known (Arjevani & Field, 2021), and increasing width turns only certain of them into saddles (Safran et al., 2021; Arjevani & Field, 2022). It remains natural to ask whether an architectural change can remove minima that survive arbitrary overparameterization. We study Gaussian population squared loss for bias-free, scalar-output, one-hidden-layer ReLU networks. The population setting retains the non-convex geometry of feature learning while separating it from finite-sample effects, making it a useful model for isolating the roles of width and architecture. At the same time, population-level landscape guarantees can inform the finite-data setting. We compare the respective loss landscapes of one-hidden-layer ReLU networks with and without a linear input-to-output skip connection. We refer to these models as plain network and skip network .1.1 OUR CONTRIBUTIONS 

We establish five results relating the loss landscape to the architecture, the teacher geometry, and the student width. • Main result: A linear skip eliminates spurious local minima under the following assumptions: Suppose that the teacher network and the student network are both skip networks and the teacher network has positive output weights and feature directions in a two-dimensional linear subspace. Whenever the student network is at least as wide as the teacher network, every local minimum of the Gaussian population loss with non-negative student output weights has zero loss (Theorem 10). • Limits of overparameterization: Increasing width alone need not eliminate spurious local minima. More precisely, we construct as teacher network a plain network in input dimension two with three hidden neurons and positive output weights. At every student width at least three, its population loss admits a spurious local minimum with positive student output weights (Theorem 11). Our main result (Theorem 10) excludes such minima once a linear skip is added. • Feature learning: Local minimality confines student features to the teacher subspace. More precisely, with or without a linear skip, at every local minimum with non-negative student output weights, each student neuron with non-zero output weight has its feature direction in the span of the teacher network’s feature directions (Theorem 8). • Effective width: At critical points, the teacher width limits the number of student features. More precisely, for a plain network in input dimension two, every critical point of the popu-lation loss with positive student output weights has at most twice as many distinct feature directions as the teacher network has hidden neurons. This bound is sharp (Theorem 12). • Finite sample consequence: We extend the main result from population loss to empirical loss given some additional local structure. More precisely, assume that the same conditions are fulfilled as in the ‘main result’ and that the teacher-generated samples are sufficiently accurate. Then, a locally optimal student network has zero empirical loss if the locality depends on the accuracy (Theorem 13). The key to our analysis of skip networks is the identity [z]+ = 12 |z| + 12 z: a plain network splits into a centered network with activation |z|/2 and a linear map that a learned skip can absorb (Zhou et al., 2021). Centering removes exactly the first angular Fourier harmonic and leaves the even ones unchanged, so the centered network is π-periodic in the direction angle; for non-negative masses the split is a translation of zonotopes (Figure 3 in Section A.1).

Teacher–student models. For shallow ReLU networks with Gaussian inputs and fixed output weights, Tian (2017) derives explicit population gradients and uses them to analyze critical points and convergence. Safran & Shamir (2018) give a computer-assisted construction of spurious local minima even for teacher networks with orthogonal teacher feature directions. Li & Yuan (2017) prove SGD convergence with a pre-activation identity connection under Gaussian inputs and a prescribed random-initialization regime; unlike our learned input-to-output skip, it acts before the activation. More recently, Tinaz et al. (2026) study the learning of a linear target by a ReLU network, showing that gradient descent from a moderately small random initialization converges globally at a linear rate despite the presence of non-strict saddle points. 

Population loss with a learned linear term. Zhou et al. (2021) study Gaussian population regres-sion for one-hidden-layer networks with absolute-value activation and non-negative output weights, and derive its equivalence to ReLU regression with an additional learned linear term. They prove gradient-descent convergence from a sufficiently small-loss initialization for teacher networks of any feature rank with sufficiently separated feature directions and any student width at least the teacher network’s. In contrast, for rank-at-most-two teacher networks we characterize the full land-scape: every local minimum with non-negative output weights is an exact fit, without a small-loss or teacher-separation assumption, thereby ruling out positive-loss local minima left open by their local convergence analysis. Their proof is perturbative around the teacher configuration, using low loss to aggregate student neurons around teacher neurons and construct a descent direction, whereas our residual formulation globally encodes the loss and its first derivatives and enables structural argu-ments from local minimality. For other residual architectures, local-minimum guarantees compare risk with that of the best linear predictor under architectural and regularity assumptions (Shamir, 2018; Kawaguchi & Bengio, 2019; Yun et al., 2019). Such bounds need not imply exact fitting of a non-linear teacher network, unlike our zero-loss conclusion under the stated assumptions. Liang et al. (2018) remove bad local minima for suitable empirical classification losses using a regularized exponential neuron; we instead use an unregularized linear skip. 

Learning the relevant subspace. Damian et al. (2022) show that gradient descent can learn representations depending only on the relevant subspace of low-dimensional polynomial targets, yielding a sample-complexity advantage over kernel methods. For Gaussian multi-index models, Mousavi-Hosseini et al. (2023) prove that online SGD with weight decay drives the first-layer weights toward the target-relevant subspace and use this to obtain width-independent generalization bounds. 

Duplicated neurons. S¸ ims ¸ek et al. (2021) analyze how neuron replication and permutation symme-tries generate connected sets of global minima and families of critical points in overparameterized 3Under review networks. When a network is widened by duplicating neurons without changing the function it represents, a local minimum of the original network may remain a local minimum or become a saddle point in the wider network (Fukumizu & Amari, 2000; Fukumizu et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2025). 

Empirical loss landscapes. Sample-dependent width bounds can exclude spurious valleys without excluding non-strict spurious minima (Venturi et al., 2019). Convex reformulations characterize optima and stationary points of regularized two-layer ReLU networks (Pilanci & Ergen, 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2025). Our transfer uses fixed-radius minimality and uniform convergence of loss values, rather than of gradients (Foster et al., 2018).

## 5 SPURIOUS LOCAL MINIMA FOR RELU 

In this section, we construct a spurious local minimum for plain networks with positive second-layer weights that persists under arbitrary overparameterization.

## 7 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We expect the planarity assumption to be removable: it enters only through the reduction to the circle in Section 4, while confinement (Theorem 8) and the zero-mass argument (Theorem 9) hold in every dimension. 

Conjecture 14. Let d ≥ 1, let the teacher network satisfy (t, v ) ∈ P ++ (d, m ) with any linear skip 

bt ∈ Rd and let n ≥ m. Then every local minimum (bs, s, w ) of Lskip on Rd × Rn × (Sd−1)n with 

(s, w ) ∈ P +(d, n ) is an exact fit. 

What is missing is a replacement for the angular residual analysis: the interlacing and descent arguments rely heavily on the one-dimensional order of lines on the circle, and no way to extend these techniques to higher dimensions is known to us. Like most of the loss landscape theory it builds on, our analysis is confined to one hidden layer: the residual representation of Theorem 7 rests on the closed form of the pair moments, which has no counterpart for deeper networks, so the effect of a learned skip on the landscape of deep networks remains open. Moreover, our result on the empirical loss landscape concerns minima that persist on a prescribed parameter ball. Whether one can rule out ordinary positive-loss empirical local minima, without a fixed-radius stability assumption, remains open. Understanding when population-landscape benignity transfers quantitatively to finite samples is an important direction for future work. Our results are landscape statements and do not imply convergence of a particular optimization algorithm. A natural next question is whether our insights into the geometry of the loss landscape can be used to analyze gradient-based dynamics. 9Under review

The argument analyzes the change of loss when altering the mass si and direction wi of the zero-mass unit. In case the residual does not vanish on an open set around the direction wi, we would be able to find student parameters arbitrarily close to (s, w ) with smaller loss, a contradiction. The argument works using the representation of the loss as a quadratic polynomial in the student mass coordinates with the values of the residual at the respective student and teacher directions as the coefficients (see Theorem 7, Lean ). This representation also comes in handy to show the final statement of the theorem, namely that the loss is zero: by Theorem 7 it suffices to show that the residual vanishes at the student and teacher directions, see Lean . Hence, in our second step we show that the residual vanishes on the whole sphere, see Lean . For d ≥ 3 this follows from an elegant argument using the fact that an analytic function vanishing on an open set vanishes everywhere. The centered kernel (9) is analytic on 

(−1, 0) ∪ (0 , 1) and the plain kernel is analytic on (−1, 1) ; in either case it is easy to see that the residual is analytic away from the finitely many points {± wi, ±vk | i, k }, which we call its atoms .Hence, it is also analytic restricted to great circles , the intersection of the sphere with a hyperplane. We observe that for any point x on the sphere Sd−1, there is a great circle through it that intersects the zero set U in an open set and avoids the finitely many residual atoms. As the residual restricted to this great circle has to vanish, it vanishes at x. As the residual vanishes at all points on the sphere except the atoms, its continuity implies it also vanishes at the atoms. This concludes the proof for the case d ≥ 3.For d = 1 , the residual is centrally symmetric, so the zero set from the first step extends to the whole sphere. We also used AI assistance for manuscript formulations and for locating and interpreting relevant declarations in the Lean development. In particular, the proof ideas in the main paper were reconstructed and written largely manually by the authors, with AI assistance for formulations and navigation of the formal development. This does not imply that their underlying ideas were exclusively human-generated: AI was heavily utilized for idea generation and contributed substantially to the discovery and development of the mathematics. The authors take responsibility for the final statements, arguments, citations, code, and accompanying artifacts. As a rough measure of computational usage, the project consumed approximately 40 full weekly Codex Pro20 usage allowances and 20 full weekly Claude Max20 usage allowances in the time span from the beginning of July to the end of September 2026. We report usage in these terms because complete token counts are not available and, moreover, token counts alone do not capture the heterogeneous computational resources involved in long-horizon agentic workflows. C.1 SCOPE OF THE CONTRIBUTION AND REPRODUCIBILITY 

This paper contributes mathematical results, not an empirical evaluation of an AI research system. We do not introduce the agent orchestration as a separately evaluated method, nor do we claim fully autonomous discovery, comparative model performance, proof-search success rates, or improvements in research efficiency. The workflow account below documents the provenance of the results, the extent of AI assistance, and the limitations encountered during the project. C.2 PROGRESSION OF THE RESEARCH WORKFLOW 

The workflow evolved through the following phases. The phases describe the progression of the project rather than a single fixed pipeline that would have been planned or applied perfectly in serial. • Phase 1: Text/TeX-only exploration. We developed an agentic proof-search setup with specialized roles for literature exploration, conjecturing, proving, and verification/utility judging, and experimented with multiple language models and orchestration pipelines. Mathematical knowledge was organized as a directed hypergraph: statements formed the vertices, while proofs formed hyperedges connecting their required ingredients to their conclusions. Statements and proofs were stored in individual Markdown files. Python code orchestrated the workflow by invoking OpenCode with different models as subprocesses. Verification remained informal, relying on judge agents to examine arguments step by step or attempt to reproduce results. The physical analogy behind Section 4, that the angular residual is the radial displacement of a thin elastic ring under point forces and hence obeys the classical ring equation (Theorem 19), was found in this phase, when an agent searching the literature for the differential equation of the kernel matched it with the elasticity of curved rods. Hallucinations and failures of this verification process motivated the transition to formal proof checking. • Phase 2: Auto-formalization and consolidation. We used language-model-assisted auto-formalization to translate selected exploratory results and supporting mathematics from the literature into a shared Lean 4 library. Compilation was supplemented by semantic review: we examined whether the formal statements expressed the intended mathematical claims, including their definitions, implicit assumptions, and quantifiers. We also checked the existence of objects satisfying relevant assumptions to guard against vacuous formulations. Prompts and reusable agent instructions were revised in response to formalization errors and discrepancies between formal statements and their intended meaning. • Phase 3: Agentic proof search with a shared Lean library. Prompt- and skill-based agents explored focused subproblems, developed candidate proofs, reused verified lemmas across related settings, and were incentivized to combine intermediate results into stronger statements. Human guidance included selecting and decomposing research questions, reviewing proposed claims, and transferring useful ideas between subproblems. Any time the search would stall, the authors would ask the agents to read relevant literature or find new related work, brainstorm ideas and directions, or evaluate ideas given by the authors. 41 Under review When the proof search was at a branching point, the authors would discuss significance, difficulties and generalizability of proof techniques with the agents in order to decide where to focus next. Numerical experiments helped test conjectures and identify counterexamples, while failed approaches were preserved to inform subsequent searches. Candidate Lean proofs were compiled, and incorporation into the verified library required both a completed formal proof and review of the statement’s mathematical meaning. Integration into the Lean library would also include a rebuild of its changed parts. When a major new result would have been integrated into the Lean library, the authors would check the respective Lean proposition and let the agents generalize from unnecessary theorem requirements. • Phase 4: Proof simplification and fresh searches. We conducted additional searches to simplify arguments and reduce the complexity and build times of the Lean development. For selected statements, fresh searches received the statement and necessary definitions either in Lean or in natural language, but not the existing proof. These searches sought alternative or simpler arguments; proposed replacements were subject to the same formal and semantic checks. • Phase 5: Attempted proof translation and author-led exposition. We used Codex and Claude Code to translate the earlier Lean proofs into an appendix of conventional mathematical proofs. Despite substantial prompting effort and repeated instructions and revisions concerning mathematical presentation, the generated drafts remained difficult to read and did not provide satisfactory exposition. We therefore extracted the proof ideas, formulas, and logical dependencies and substantially reorganized the arguments. The authors wrote the resulting proof ideas largely manually, while continuing to use AI for formulations and assistance in navigating and interpreting the Lean theorems. These reconstructed proof ideas, rather than the earlier automatically generated proof appendix, are the exposition presented in the main paper. The assessment of the generated drafts describes our experience in this project; it is not a systematic evaluation of the models’ mathematical writing capabilities. • Phase 6: Re-formalization of the simplified proof structure. The author-led reconstruc-tion substantially simplified the organization of the arguments. We then instructed Codex with Astra Ultra to produce Lean proofs following the structure of the revised proof ideas. The resulting development forms the supplementary Lean certificate accompanying the current manuscript. C.3 VERIFICATION AND AUTHOR RESPONSIBILITY
