# Constrained Federated Reinforcement Learning for Secure Offloading in Vehicular Edge Computing 

Zhiyu Wang, Muhammed F. Esgin, and Mohammad Goudarzi *

Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, Australia 

Abstract —Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) reduces latency for computation-intensive vehicular applications by offloading tasks to roadside edge servers. Real vehicular workloads, how-ever, consist of dependent subtasks with stringent reliability needs, while the cross-region collaborative learning used to adapt offloading policies must stay robust to heterogeneous data and secure over open wireless channels. We propose CRAFT, a Constrained Reinforcement-based Adaptive Federated Task Offloading framework that casts Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)-structured offloading and resource allocation as a Constrained Markov Decision Process. CRAFT couples centralized-critic pol-icy learning with Lagrangian relaxation to explicitly regulate the long-term task-completion constraint, and uses two-tier federated aggregation with proximal regularization to jointly mitigate non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) drift and stabilize constraint adaptation. Confidentiality and integrity for federated parameter exchange are provided by post-quantum ho-momorphic encryption and lattice-based signatures with modest amortized overhead. On a Virtual Machine-based testbed over a realistic Melbourne Central Business District network with real computation kernels, CRAFT cuts weighted cost by up to 42% over state-of-the-art baselines, maintains a completion rate of around 96%, and adds 6.4% security overhead. 

Index Terms —Vehicular edge computing, task offloading, multi-agent reinforcement learning, federated learning, post-quantum cryptography. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The rapid advancement of connected and autonomous vehicles has given rise to computation-intensive applications such as autonomous driving perception, augmented reality navigation, and in-vehicle voice assistants [1], [2], all imposing stringent real-time computing requirements. Constrained by the limited computing resources and battery capacity of onboard plat-forms, relying solely on local execution can hardly satisfy these demands. Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC), which offloads tasks to Edge Servers (ESs) deployed on Roadside Units (RSUs), offers a promising solution. However, VEC task offloading remains extremely challenging due to rapid channel variations caused by vehicle mobility, an exponentially growing joint decision space [3], and the need to balance conflicting metrics including latency, energy consumption, and task completion rate. Beyond these general challenges, four deeper technical difficulties persist. First, real vehicular applications such as autonomous driving perception pipelines inherently comprise multiple Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)-structured process-ing stages with data-flow precedence constraints, requiring

subtask-level parallel scheduling along the critical path [4]. Second, task completion rate is a safety-critical metric that demands formal long-term guarantees [5], [6]. Third, vehicles independently trained within isolated edge regions inevitably converge to region-specific strategies, causing severe policy mismatch when vehicles cross coverage boundaries. Federated Learning (FL) offers a natural solution for cross-region knowl-edge sharing [7], [8], but the significant regional differences in traffic density, task composition, and vehicle populations give rise to strongly heterogeneous data distributions that severely compromise aggregation quality and learning stability [9]. Fourth, the cross-region federated collaboration introduced for the third challenge is itself the source of a new security problem: parameters and training signals must be repeatedly exchanged over open Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) channels that are susceptible to eavesdropping and tampering [10], risking confidentiality leakage of vehicles’ behavioral patterns (not only to external eavesdroppers but also to the honest-but-curious edge servers that process the parameters), as well as integrity violations that propagate through aggregation to all participants. Moreover, as long-lifecycle assets, vehicles face harvest-now-decrypt-later threats [11], and with major providers including Google 1 and Cloudflare 2 migrating to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) by 2029, post-quantum resilience is imperative from the outset. Addressing these challenges calls for an offloading policy that adapts online to dynamic mobility and channel conditions without relying on accurate system models. Deep Reinforce-ment Learning (DRL), which learns such policies directly through environment interaction, is naturally suited to this setting [12], while a multi-agent formulation is needed to avoid the exponentially growing joint action space that single-agent DRL incurs in multi-vehicle multi-server scenarios [3]. To this end, we propose CRAFT (Constrained Reinforcement-based Adaptive Federated Task offloading), a secure federated Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework that models DAG-structured offloading in VEC as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) and addresses the four challenges through four cooperating components: (i) a Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)-based multi-agent module with Actors on vehicles for offloading decisions and Critics on ESs for value estimation; (ii) a Lagrangian relaxation module that folds the long-term completion-rate constraint into the reward via online dual updates; (iii) a two-tier federated Actor aggregation

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Task Offloading in Vehicular Edge Computing 

Model-based optimization methods derive theoretically opti-mal offloading solutions through rigorous mathematical for-mulations, predominantly via game theory. Sun et al. [16] combined a Bargaining Game for intra-server allocation with Matching-based inter-server offloading to maximize utility, Fan et al. [17] adopted a Potential Game to minimize pro-cessing delay, while Yin et al. [18] modeled the system as a multi-stage Stackelberg Game to maximize tripartite benefits. Several works further couple optimization with se-curity: Xiao et al. [19] integrated block-cipher encryption into a Generalized Benders Decomposition (GBD) scheme, Yang et al. [20] secured a pricing-based Stackelberg Game for platoon offloading via Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), and Liang et al. [21] adopted a blockchain framework with game-theoretic pricing. However, these methods rely on accurate model assumptions and adapt poorly to rapid mobility and time-varying channels [22], [23]. Reinforcement Learning (RL) enables online adap-tive decision-making without accurate system models. Qiu et al. [24], Fan et al. [25], and Ju et al. [26] respectively applied Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG), Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3), and Asyn-chronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) to joint offloading and resource/power allocation, with the last leveraging Physical Layer Security (PLS) against eavesdroppers. However, such Single-Agent RL (SARL) methods suffer from exponentially growing joint action spaces in multi-vehicle multi-server set-tings, severely limiting scalability [3]. Multi-Agent RL (MARL) mitigates this by assigning each vehicle a learning agent. Abbas et al. [27] proposed DPTORA based on the Generalist Pursuit Learning Algorithm (GPLA) for flexible partial offloading across RSUs. Several Multi-Agent DDPG (MADDPG)-based schemes target different ob-jectives: Xu et al. [28] maximized task completion rate, Fofana et al. [29] balanced time, cost, and success probability, and Xiao et al. [30] embedded PLS into the reward for secure offloading. Wei et al. [31] adopted Multi-Agent Soft Actor-Critic (MASAC) to optimise latency, while Ju et al. [32] used a PLS-aware Multi-Agent Double Deep Q-Network (MAD-DQN) to minimize processing delay against eavesdroppers. More recently, FL has been integrated for collaborative knowl-edge sharing: Wu et al. [33] studied auxiliary vehicle resource exploitation, Lei et al. [34] proposed Fed-MADDPG for joint scheduling without state sharing, and Zeng et al. [35] proposed a Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)-based method minimizing average delay.

Gap 2: No long-term constraint enforcement. Task com-pletion rate is safety-critical, yet no existing work guarantees a minimum long-term rate through a formal constraint-handling mechanism; where considered, it appears only as a soft reward term without any threshold guarantee. 

Gap 3: Federated learning without non-IID handling. 

Only three works [33], [34], [35] adopt FL, and none addresses non-IID data: as traffic density, speed, and task composition vary across regions, standard aggregation lets local models drift from the global model, degrading aggregation quality and destabilizing training. 

Gap 4: Incomplete security without PQC-based param-eter protection. Although a few works secure the offload-ing process, each addresses only one of confidentiality and integrity [19], [20], [26], [32], [30], [21]; even when confi-dentiality is provided, it guards only against external eaves-droppers, leaving the parameters exposed in plaintext at the edge servers that process them. The FL-based schemes [33], [34], [35], by contrast, leave the exchanged model parameters unprotected altogether. Across all of them, none employs PQC to protect the parameters. 

Gap 5: Simulation-only evaluation. All existing works evaluate purely in simulation, estimating delay and energy from analytical formulas rather than measuring real compu-tation kernels on actual hardware. CRAFT is designed to simultaneously address all five gaps by integrating CMDP-based Lagrangian constraint handling with DAG-aware scheduling, proximal-regularized federated aggregation with post-quantum security protection combining CKKS and ML-DSA signatures, and VM-based evaluation with real application workloads. A. System Overview 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we consider a Vehicular Edge Comput-ing (VEC) system consisting of multiple vehicles and multiple roadside Edge Servers (ESs). The system operates over a discretized time horizon divided into T equal-length time slots, denoted by T = {1, 2, . . . , T }, each of duration τ . We adopt a quasi-static model, where the channel conditions remain approximately constant within each time slot. Each DAG task is either completed or declared failed within the time slot in which it is generated, and the system begins a new decision epoch at the start of the next time slot. The system comprises N vehicles, denoted by N =

{1, 2, . . . , N }, and M ESs deployed on Roadside Units (RSUs), denoted by M = {1, 2, . . . , M }. Each ES m has a coverage radius Rm and a total computing capacity Fm

(cycles/s). Each vehicle generates computation tasks with internal dependencies (modeled as DAGs) while driving. A vehicle can either execute subtasks locally or offload them to an ES within its communication range via V2I links. The offloading destination, including the choice of which ES to use, is dynamically determined by the system rather than simply selecting the nearest ES. 4

C1 is a long-term statistical constraint requiring the overall task completion rate to be no less than θ, where the success condition encompasses both the deadline and the sojourn coverage requirements. Unlike per-slot hard constraints, C1 permits occasional task failures in individual time slots as long as the long-term average success rate meets the threshold. C2 is the ES computing resource capacity constraint. C3 is the DAG dependency constraint, ensuring that a subtask begins execution only after all its predecessors have completed and their output data have been received. C4 restricts the offloading target to currently reachable ESs. C5 ensures each offloaded subtask receives a positive amount of computing resources. 

P1 is a Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) problem, where the offloading decisions a are discrete vari-ables and the resource allocations f are continuous variables. Solving this problem poses three key challenges: (i) coupling of discrete and continuous variables causes the search space to grow exponentially with the number of vehicles and subtasks, rendering the problem NP-hard in general; (ii) the time-varying nature of vehicle mobility and channel conditions necessitates online real-time decision-making; and (iii) C1 is a long-term statistical constraint spanning multiple time slots, which cannot be verified within a single slot. Given these challenges, 

P1 is naturally modeled as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP), where C1 is handled via Lagrangian relax-ation and transformed into a dual optimization problem. In Section IV, we propose CRAFT, a secure federated multi-agent Reinforcement Learning framework with Lagrangian-based constraint handling, to solve the problem online. IV. A. Framework Overview 

To solve the CMDP problem formulated in Section III-F, we propose CRAFT. As shown in Fig. 2, CRAFT consists of four cooperating components: (i) a multi-agent decision module following the CTDE paradigm, where Actors are deployed on vehicles for offloading decisions and Critics are deployed on ESs for value estimation; (ii) a Lagrangian relaxation-based constraint handling module incorporating the long-term completion rate constraint into the reward function via online 6

CRAFT operates two parallel loops. The decision loop 

executes once per time slot: vehicles make offloading decisions based on local observations, ESs compute advantage estimates using global states and return them to vehicles, and vehicles update their local Actors accordingly. The aggregation loop 

executes every K time slots: vehicles upload encrypted Actor parameters to ESs, which are aggregated through two tiers and redistributed to all vehicles, with the Lagrangian multiplier synchronized simultaneously.

C. Lagrangian-based Constrained Optimization 

The choice of CMDP with Lagrangian relaxation is motivated by three structural properties of C1. First, C1 is a trajectory-level statistical constraint that cannot be evaluated at individual states, ruling out per-step safe action filtering methods [37]. Second, the binary indicator sn(t) is non-differentiable, pre-cluding direct application of trust-region constrained policy methods such as Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) [38] that rely on smooth constraint surrogates. Third, fixed-penalty reward shaping lacks an adaptive mechanism for correct-ing long-term constraint violations [39]. The CMDP frame-work [40] naturally accommodates such long-term constraints, and Lagrangian relaxation provides a principled way to adap-tively penalize task failures while remaining compatible with actor-critic architectures and federated aggregation. D. Multi-Agent Policy Optimization 

This subsection details the multi-agent decision-making and training mechanisms. 

Architecture Design. CRAFT adopts the CTDE paradigm, whose suitability arises from the inherent architecture of VEC systems: ESs naturally acquire state information of all vehicles within their coverage through the task offloading management process, and can therefore serve as centralized Critics with zero additional communication overhead. Specifically, the Ac-tor network πwn is deployed on vehicle n and outputs actions based on local observation on(t); the Critic network Vwcm is deployed on ES m and outputs state value estimates based on global state sm(t).

E. Secure Federated Actor Aggregation 

Although the CTDE-based multi-agent training enables ef-fective local policy learning within each ES region, inde-pendent training across different regions may lead to policy fragmentation, where vehicles in high-density areas, low-density areas, urban roads, and highways learn significantly different strategies. To address this, CRAFT introduces FL for cross-region knowledge transfer. FL aggregates only the Actor parameters and not the Critic, since the Critic operates on global states whose semantics and dimensions vary across ES regions, making aggregation meaningless.

Constraint-Aware Non-IID Federated Aggregation. In VEC scenarios, experience data collected across different regions exhibit significant non-IID characteristics, with het-erogeneity arising from three dimensions. First, spatial het-erogeneity : traffic density variations across regions lead to substantially different state space distributions, e.g., urban centers feature dense vehicle populations with intense resource contention, whereas peripheral road segments have sparse vehicles with abundant ES resources. Second, task distribution heterogeneity : the dominant task types differ across regions, e.g., commercial districts are dominated by augmented reality navigation tasks while highway segments primarily generate autonomous driving perception tasks, causing systematic bias in the task characteristic dimensions of each vehicle’s collected experience. Third, temporal non-stationarity : vehicles contin-uously enter and exit the coverage of different ESs, causing participants in each aggregation round to change dynamically, further exacerbating the instability of data distributions. Under these strong non-IID conditions, standard Federated Averaging (FedAvg) aggregation allows local models to devi-ate substantially from the global model between aggregation rounds, destabilizing the quality of the aggregated global model. We adopt the proximal regularization mechanism from Federated Proximal (FedProx)[43] to address this challenge, rather than alternative approaches such as Stochastic Con-trolled Averaging for Federated Learning (SCAFFOLD) or Federated Normalized Averaging (FedNova), based on the following considerations. SCAFFOLD requires transmitting additional control variates in each aggregation round, dou-bling the V2I uplink communication volume, which incurs prohibitive overhead in bandwidth-constrained vehicular net-works. FedNova requires explicit normalization over the num-ber of local update steps across participants, but in VEC scenarios, vehicles’ local update counts naturally vary due to mobility and are difficult to predict, resulting in high implementation complexity. FedProx requires only adding a proximal term to local loss function with no additional com-munication overhead, and inherently tolerates heterogeneous local update steps. Incorporating the proximal term into CRAFT’s local train-ing objective yields the complete loss function: 

Llocal (wn) = Lclip (wn) + μ

2 ∥wn − wglobal ∥2, (38) where Lclip is the clipped surrogate objective based on the augmented reward ˜rn(t) (which already incorporates the La-grangian penalty described in Section IV-C), and μ > 0 is the proximal coefficient. Notably, within CRAFT’s constrained optimization frame-work, the proximal term serves a dual role. Its first role is the classical non-IID drift control: by anchoring local models near the global model, it prevents policies across different regions from diverging into mutually incompatible local optima due to heterogeneous experience data. Its second role is acting as a stabilizer for the Lagrangian dynamic adjustment. In CRAFT, the Lagrangian multiplier λ is updated dynamically based on the task completion rate: when the completion rate falls below the threshold, λ increases to impose heavier penalties on task failures. However, a sudden increase in λ alters the optimization objective of all vehicles through the augmented reward ˜rn(t), driving all local models to simultaneously shift toward conservative strategies with large parameter updates. Without the proximal term, such a global policy shift would cause local models to deviate substantially from the current global model before aggregation, resulting in severe oscil-lations in the aggregated global model. The proximal term constrains the magnitude of parameter changes during each round of local updates, imposing a damping effect on the policy adjustments triggered by λ variations and ensuring a smooth constraint adaptation process. The effectiveness of this dual role is validated through the ablation study in Section V. 

Post-Quantum Security Protection. Throughout CRAFT’s training pipeline, data is continuously exchanged among ve-hicles, ESs, and the global aggregator. These communication links face two categories of security threats: (i) privacy leak-age , where honest-but-curious ESs or external eavesdroppers 9

may infer vehicle driving behavioral patterns and preferences from plaintext model parameters; and (ii) integrity violation ,where man-in-the-middle attackers can intercept and tamper with data in transit, e.g., injecting noise into model parame-ters or systematically corrupting advantage values to induce cumulative policy drift that later propagates through federated aggregation. CRAFT addresses these threats through a layered post-quantum security design: CKKS protects the confidential-ity of model parameters during aggregation and enables com-putation over encrypted data, while ML-DSA digital signatures protect the integrity of all exchanged data. Both primitives are built on lattice-based hardness assumptions, yielding a fully post-quantum secure training pipeline. 

Key setup and enrollment. We assume a standard vehicu-lar Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), as adopted in Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) security credential management sys-tems [10]: a trusted, offline Certificate Authority (CA) enrolls each legitimate vehicle and issues a certificate binding the vehicle’s ML-DSA public key pk i to its authorized identity. Each entity signs with its own private key sk i, and a recipient accepts a message only if (i) the accompanying certificate is valid and unrevoked under the CA, and (ii) the ML-DSA signature verifies under the certified pk i. Consequently, a self-generated key pair without a valid CA certificate is rejected, and impersonating an enrolled vehicle requires its private key, which never leaves the vehicle. The trusted global aggregator additionally holds a single CKKS key pair (pk HE , sk HE ),distributing pk HE to all enrolled vehicles and retaining sk HE 

as the sole decryption key. 

Confidentiality: CKKS Homomorphic Encryption. Conven-tional encryption schemes (e.g., AES) protect parameters only against network eavesdroppers: since one cannot compute over their ciphertexts, the edge server must decrypt before aggre-gating, exposing every vehicle’s plaintext parameters to an honest-but-curious server. CKKS instead supports computation over ciphertexts, so the two-tier weighted averaging in (36)– (37) runs entirely in the encrypted domain and no edge server ever observes individual plaintext parameters, providing confi-dentiality against both eavesdroppers and edge servers. Beyond this homomorphic property, the selection of CKKS [13] is motivated by three further considerations. First, neural network parameters are floating-point values, and CKKS natively sup-ports approximate arithmetic over real numbers, whereas exact HE schemes such as Brakerski/Fan-Vercauteren (BFV) and Brakerski-Gentry-Vaikuntanathan (BGV) [44], [45] require additional fixed-point quantization that introduces unnecessary precision loss and implementation complexity. Second, com-pared with Differential Privacy, CKKS does not inject artificial noise into model parameters, thereby avoiding the privacy-accuracy trade-off. Third, compared with Secure Aggregation protocols, CKKS does not require all participants to be simul-taneously online, making it better suited for VEC scenarios where vehicles dynamically enter and exit ES coverage. Since CKKS supports addition and scalar multiplication on ciphertexts, the two-tier weighted averaging defined in (36) and (37) can be performed entirely in the ciphertext domain. In each FL aggregation round, every participating vehicle encrypts its Actor parameters under the aggregator’s public key pk HE : en = CKKS Enc( pk HE , wn). Integrity: ML-DSA Post-Quantum Digital Signatures. 

CRAFT deploys ML-DSA digital signatures on all commu-nication links to protect data integrity. ML-DSA is a lattice-based post-quantum signature scheme built on the Module Learning with Errors (MLWE) problem, sharing the same cryptographic foundation as CKKS. ML-DSA is chosen over classical ones (e.g., Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)), preventing overall security from being downgraded by the quantum vulnerability of a classical signature scheme. The signing protocol applies uniformly to all transmissions. For any payload z transmitted from entity i to another entity, the sender holding its private key sk i computes the signature

During each FL aggregation round, the global aggregator updates λ based on the average task success rate ¯s over the past K time slots reported by all ESs, and broadcasts the updated λ together with the global Actor parameters, incurring no additional communication rounds.

B. Convergence Performance 

Fig. 4 shows the convergence of all methods over 100 episodes in normalized latency, energy, task completion rate, and weighted cost. CRAFT converges fastest, with all metrics stabilizing around episode 50 at the lowest values of 0.21 (latency), 0.52 (energy), and 0.37 (cost). Its completion rate settles at about 0.96, closely tracking the threshold θ = 0 .95 

and validating the online Lagrangian adjustment. The baselines each reveal a structural limitation. LF-MAPPO is unstable early on (latency 0.40, completion rate 0.86) under unconstrained exploration; although its latency drops to 0.27, its energy never improves, as it favors local execution to avoid communication delay. Fed-MADDPG at-tains competitive energy via federated strategy sharing but converges to a high latency of 0.32, limited by the poor exploration of deterministic policy gradients in the hybrid action space. DPTORA, driven by learning-automata updates, shows small fluctuations but weak optimization across all metrics, reflecting insufficient policy expressiveness. Overall, CRAFT reduces weighted cost by about 16%, 20%, and 42% over Fed-MADDPG, DPTORA, and LF-MAPPO, respectively.

F. Security Overhead Analysis 

Fig. 8 quantifies the security overhead. In the per-round breakdown (Fig. 8a), local training time is identical with and without security (3264 ms). CKKS encryption and decryption add 72 ms, ciphertext expansion raises communication time from 6 ms to 117 ms, and ML-DSA signing and verification, accumulated over the K = 20 per-slot exchanges and the aggregation round, contribute about 25 ms. The total overhead 14 

of 208 ms accounts for 6.4% of the training time, indicating a modest amortized security cost relative to local training. Fig. 8b shows the scaling of the dominant component, CKKS. At h = 256 (71.7K parameters), encryption and decryption take 58 ms and 14 ms; even at h = 512 (274.4K parameters) they remain practical at about 220 ms and 55 ms. ML-DSA overhead is independent of model size, operating on a fixed-size hash digest, and since FL aggregation runs only periodically, the CKKS and communication costs are further amortized and never become a bottleneck. VI. C ONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

This paper proposes CRAFT for joint offloading and resource allocation of DAG-structured tasks in VEC. CRAFT models the problem as a CMDP solved through four cooperating components: CTDE-based multi-agent decision-making, La-grangian relaxation for constraint handling, two-tier federated aggregation with dual-role proximal regularization, and post-quantum security combining CKKS encryption for confiden-tiality with ML-DSA signatures for integrity. Experiments on a VM-based platform with a realistic Melbourne CBD network show that CRAFT lowers weighted cost by about 16%, 20%, and 42% over three state-of-the-art baselines, holds the completion rate (96%) near the constraint threshold, and incurs only 6.4% security overhead. Future work proceeds along three directions: (i) incorporat-ing Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) cooperative offloading to exploit idle resources on nearby vehicles via joint V2I-V2V schedul-ing; (ii) adaptive multi-objective optimization that adjusts the latency-energy weights to real-time traffic and application demands; and (iii) attaching zero-knowledge proofs of well-formedness to CKKS-encrypted updates, so that maliciously tampered parameters from compromised-but-certified vehicles can be filtered in the ciphertext domain.
