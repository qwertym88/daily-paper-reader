Abstract — Humanoid dexterous manipulation needs refer-ence motions that are both semantically rich and kinematically feasible for the target robot. Text-conditioned hand–object interaction (HOI) generators supply diverse, language-specified demonstrations, but they synthesize motion in human (MANO) space. The embodiment gap is therefore deferred to post-hoc retargeting, where trajectories deployed on the robot can fail to maintain contact with objects and also degrade policy learning. Our key idea is that robot reachability should constrain the interaction trajectories while they are being generated . We present EMBODY HOI, which runs a differentiable inverse-

We feed back gradients from the embodiment-aware IK solver into the HOI sampler and jointly optimize the position of the humanoid with respect to the object. Because guidance acts only at inference, one HOI trajectory generator trained on human hand poses transfers across humanoid embodiments without retraining. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands remains difficult for humanoids that can reliably walk and loco-manipulate [1]–[4]. A robot must coordinate reaching, finger contact, and whole-arm motion under embodiment-specific kinematic constraints to execute trajectories that actually manipulate objects. A human trajectory when kinematically retargeted to a humanoid is often not executable, due to differences in finger morphology, joint limits, and wrist reachability. We study how to generate human interactions which can easily close the above gap. Given a language prompt and an articulated object, we produce bimanual hand–object trajec-tories specific to a humanoid, which are easily executable by the robot. A natural route to this goal of end-to-end policies is generate-then-track, but today’s Hand-Object Interaction (HOI) generators have no notion of the robot that will execute their output. In this route, a generator proposes diverse task-conditioned reference motions and a physics-based RL policy learns to track them under contact and actuation limits [5]. Hand-centric HOI generators are the natural source of dexterous references as they model finger-object contact, but they generate in MANO [6] space with no term that couples the wrist to a robot’s kinematic chain. This mis-match only surfaces during retargeting, after the finger and object motions are committed, and the tracking policy inherits wrist targets that the arm cannot reach. Previous works resolve this on either side of generation. Training the motion prior in robot space [7] yields one generator per robot, which rules out cross-embodiment reuse. Whereas repairing a generated human motion [8], [9] only makes delta modifications, and cannot always rectify retargeted wrist trajectories unreachable for the robot. Tracking systems such as DexMachina [10] and ManipTrans [11] sidestep the question by commanding a floating wrist with no arm. We take a different approach: keep a frozen MANO HOI generator as a reusable motion prior and make its samples embodiment-aware during generation (Fig. 1). We build on MAD-HOI [12], a text-conditioned HOI generator that fills object and hand tokens over a sequence of unmasking steps, integrating a flow-matching ODE for the unmasked tokens at each step. Inside every ODE step, we decode the current wrist trajectories, solve a differentiable IK problem on the target humanoid, and add a gradient of the IK residual ( IK energy ) to the learned velocity field, in a manner of classifier guidance. A wrist correction made in one unmasking step conditions the finger and object tokens generated in later ones, so the interaction re-forms around the corrected wrist instead of being patched afterward. A full humanoid raises another problem that floating hands and fixed-base arms do not, where the robot stands. The base placement decides whether both arms stay reachable over the whole interaction, and a fixed-base IK cannot differentiate between a feasible interaction performed from the wrong standing position from an infeasible one. We therefore solve for the base translation together with the arm joints, and re-solve it at every unmasking step so that the base follows the partially generated motion. Finally, we retarget the guided motions and train a PPO tracking policy with separate imitation rewards for arm and hand, as they differ in error scale and contact requirements. 

IK energy is a diagnostic, not a quantity to minimize on its own. Guidance costs 32 × a native sample (5 s to 160 s on an A100), paid once per reference, so we compare it with alternatives that lower the energy after generation at the same or lower cost. Post-hoc IK refinement of a finished sample reaches the lowest energy of any method, and compute-matched best-of-N selection also beats guidance on energy, yet both train worse policies (Table I). Refinement moves the wrists after the fingers and object are fixed, and selection returns a different interaction instead of correcting the requested one. Reachability has to be imposed while the interaction is still being generated. Our contributions are as follows.

A differentiable IK solve on the target humanoid runs inside every ODE step of the sampler, and its gradient steers the hand tokens. One objective with one set of weights serves the Unitree G1 and H1-2, and it also low-ers the IK energy of a second generator, Text2HOI [13], without modification.

EMBODY HOI draws on three lines of work, and none of them adapts the reference motion to a humanoid’s arm chain while the interaction is being generated. 

Human-space HOI generation and test-time guidance. 

Text-to-motion models generate full-body SMPL [14] mo-tion with pose-space diffusion [15], [16] or masked-token 2generation [17]. Full-body HOI methods [18], [19] model contact only coarsely at the body surface. Hand-centric methods parameterized by MANO [6] model fine-grained finger–object contact on datasets such as ARCTIC [20], GRAB [21], and OakInk [22], from grasp synthesis and inter-action denoising [21], [23] to language-conditioned sequence generation [12], [13], [24]–[26]. Test-time guidance steers such generators toward keyframes, trajectories, and contacts, but always toward targets specified in human space [27], [28]. Our guidance has the same form but no human-space target: it scores the decoded wrist against a robot’s kinematic chain, so its optimum moves when the robot or its base changes. 

Retargeting and RL from human demonstrations. 

Human-to-robot retargeting typically optimizes joint angles against keypoint and end-effector errors [29], [30], and it un-derpins teleoperation [30], [31] and robot-space datasets [7]. Dexterous-hand retargeting [32]–[34] is a standard prepro-cessing step for RL from human demonstrations. Manip-Trans [11] pretrains a hand-trajectory imitator and then fine-tunes a residual policy. DexMachina [10] uses retargeted joints and contacts as auxiliary rewards under a decaying virtual-object curriculum. DexMan [35] extends this recipe to RGB video, and HERMES [36] unifies heterogeneous motion sources for mobile bimanual manipulation. In all of them the reference is fixed before the robot is considered. ManipTrans and DexMachina command a floating 6-DoF wrist with no arm chain, and DexMan drives a G1 arm through IK but keeps the robot at the origin, so neither arm reachability nor base placement is ever optimized. We reuse their object-side recipe (Sec. III-C) and make the reference itself the variable. 

Generative priors for policy learning. Example-guided RL [37], [38] extends to HOI imitation [39], [40] and to tracking-as-reward execution [5], [41]. The two works closest to ours resolve the embodiment mismatch after the motion exists. Chen et al. [9] guide an RL finger controller with a generative prior that emits only wrists, fixed before the robot is considered, and DemoDiffusion [8] denoises a retargeted human trajectory with a robot diffusion policy that stays close to that trajectory and so inherits its reachability. DreamControl-v2 [7] avoids retargeting by training in robot space, and fine-tuning a human-space generator on reachable data would likewise remove our inference cost; both need robot-feasible data for every embodiment, which a method like ours would have to produce, and yield one generator per robot. However, none of them keeps one frozen human-space generator: we resolve reachability inside its sampling loop and handle base placement for a full humanoid arm chain. III. M ETHOD

Unless stated otherwise, the target is a Unitree G1 with two XHands: 15 controllable upper-body DoFs (torso and both arms), fixed lower body, and 12 DoFs per hand. Actions are 39-D: increments on the previous joint target for the 15 upper-body DoFs, which keep large arm motions smooth, and absolute targets for the 24 finger DoFs, whose joint ranges are small. We never actuate the object, so unlike DexMachina we use no virtual-object-controller curriculum. 

Arm and hand get separate imitation rewards and terminations. Coarse free-space reaching and contact-rich finger control differ in error scale, so a single imitation term over all 39 DoFs biases optimization toward whichever subsystem dominates the error. We therefore split imitation into an upper-body reward rimi ,upper over 15 DoFs and a dexterous reward rimi ,dex over 24 DoFs, each a joint-tracking error against the retargeted reference with its own weight and temperature. Terminations are decoupled in the same way, on the half-squared joint error 12 ∆q2: the upper-body error terminates on its maximum over joints, because a large arm failure ends the rollout, and the finger error on its mean , because an isolated finger error is not catastrophic. A. Is Guidance During Generation Necessary? (Q1) 

Guidance costs 32 × a native sample, so each alternative in Table I receives the same budget, the same 51 prompts, and the same IK solver and base optimizer. Base-placement 

optimization alone keeps the sample and re-solves only the base. This cost is paid once per reference and amortized over 

≈4 h of policy training it supports, so the wall-clock com-parison is a worst case rather than a deployment constraint. 

Post-hoc IK refinement optimizes the wrist channels of a finished unguided sample against the same energy (Adam, 100 steps). Best-of-N draws N unguided samples with fixed seeds and keeps the one with the lowest terminal E under the same optimized base. 

Base placement is the cheapest large gain. Solving for the base with a single unguided sample reduces the median energy from 2.30 to 1.89 and raises the success rate from 68.1 to 73.3 at no sampling cost (Fig. 3). Guidance adds a smaller, consistent correction on top: it lowers the energy of the seed-matched sample on 40 of the 51 prompts (78%; median 1.81) and raises the success rate to 79.7. 

IK energy is not the quantity to optimize. Energy is di-agnostic only at fixed interaction content: it is meaningful for a seed-matched guided-vs-unguided pair, and meaningless across independently drawn samples. Post-hoc refinement reaches the lowest energy of any method ( E=0 .35 ) but moves the wrists by 33 cm after finger and object motion are fixed, raising penetration from 0.52 to 0.59 and collapsing the success rate to 32.8. Selection lowers the energy further than guidance at every budget: 1.36 at N =8 and 1.21 at the compute-matched N =32 , and wins on 88–94% of prompts — 88% on the easiest third by unguided E, 94% on the hardest — so its advantage does not shrink where reachability is worst. (see Section II-B). The reason is variance. Energy varies across samples of one prompt (s.d. 0.36) far more than guidance shifts it (median per-prompt shift 0.04), so drawing more samples finds a low-energy trajectory more easily than correcting one sample does. Yet the policies trained from selected references succeed less often than from guided ones (73.1 at N =8 , 74.0 at N =32 , vs. 79.7), a gap of 5.7 ± 0.7 points at matched compute. The energy measures wrist reachability only; it is blind to whether fingers and object still form a coherent interaction around the wrists it prefers. 

Guidance corrects, selection re-draws. The selected best-of-N sample lies as far from the seed-matched one as any other sample (13 cm), whereas guidance moves the wrists of the seed-matched sample by 0.3 cm and leaves penetration and contact unchanged. Only guidance edits the sample already in hand, which is what matters when that sample is one a user chose. Base-placement optimization is therefore the baseline to report alongside guidance: it is free, selection is a no-op on top of it at either budget ( −0.2 ± 0.8

at N =8 , 0.7 ± 0.8 at N =32 ), and raising N from 8 to 32 does not help either ( 0.9 ± 0.8). 

B. Kinematic Conditioning and Generation Quality (Q2) 

Conditioning. Base placement and guidance remove dif-ferent failure modes. D. Transfer Across Embodiments and Generators (Q4) 

As guidance acts at inference on a frozen generator, shifting to a new robot only needs change in the IK module. On the Unitree H1-2, whose arms are 68.5 cm long compared to G1’s 45 cm, guidance lowers the IK energy of the seed-matched sample on 46 of 50 prompts (median 2.06 → 1.95 ,mean −6% ) while moving wrists by 0.3 cm. Both conditions 8stay far from singularity ( σmin ≈ 0.13 ) because H1-2’s longer arms are closer to human proportions. Notably, the base moves in the opposite direction to G1’s (Fig. 7): the longer arms over-extend and must stand back, where the G1’s must close in. No term in the objective encodes either behaviour; both fall out of the same residual. The gain carries through to policy learning: on the same four-task suite, guidance raises H1-2 success rate from 62.3 ± 0.4% to 75.7 ± 0.5% (5 seeds, 10 episodes per task), a 13.4-point gain comparable to the G1’s 11.6 points (68.1 to 79.7, Table I) despite the different arm geometry. The module is also generator-agnostic. Text2HOI [13] represents the wrist pose identically to MAD-HOI, so the IK objective and the base prior transfer without modification. Applied to its DDPM sampler as a gradient step on prediction 

x0, the guidance lowers the IK energy on 48 of 50 ARCTIC prompts (median 2.36 → 2.34 , mean −1.3% ) with at most 

0.4 cm of wrist adjustment. The same objective on the H1-2 lowers Text2HOI’s energy on all 50 ARCTIC prompts (median 1.77 → 1.75 , mean −1.2% ), again with the wrists within 0.5 cm. The effect is smaller than on MAD-HOI because Text2HOI’s contact refiner post-processes the hands, but its direction is consistent across generators and robots. We therefore do not depend on MAD-HOI: its checkpoint is public [12], we will release the guidance code, all generated references, and the trained policies. V. C ONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

EMBODY HOI helps a frozen HOI generator in producing motions a target humanoid can reach. The key component is running a differentiable IK solve inside its sampling loop and optimizing the robot’s base. Policies trained on the guided references succeed more often than on unguided ones (79% vs. 68% on 50 random tasks, 76% vs. 62% on the H1-2). Base placement being the larger of the two contributors (4.0 vs. 2.9 points) and also the cheaper, since it adds no sampling cost. More broadly, our comparison against post-hoc refinement and best-of-N selection is a caution for reference-generation pipelines in general: a kinematic feasibility metric is a valid diagnostic only when the rest of the interaction is held fixed, and driving it down directly buys feasibility at the cost of the interaction it was meant to preserve. Guidance is kinematic: better reachability does not imply dynamic or contact feasibility, and the RL stage absorbs what guidance does not model. The cost is a 32 × slower sampler, paid once per reference; restricting guidance to a subset of the refinement steps would reduce it. Our scope is limited in two ways. The lower body is fixed: we optimize where the robot stands, and not how it gets there. Integrating a locomotion controller [4], [44] is left for future work. All evaluation is performed in simulation on ARCTIC’s articulated objects; real-world rollouts are the immediate next step. Beyond scope, fine-tuning the generator on its own guided samples would distill the guidance into the prior and remove the inference cost, at the price of one generator per embodiment. A. Flow Matching and Energy Guidance 

This section expands the sampling formulation summa-rized in Sec. III-B, so that the guidance derivation is self-contained for readers unfamiliar with flow matching. 

Flow-matching sampler. MAD-HOI [12] generates con-tinuous motion latents with a flow-matching objective. Rather than learning a sequence of discrete denoising transitions, it learns a time-dependent velocity field vθ (x, t, z) that transports samples from a simple noise distribution toward the data distribution, conditioned on context z. Under a linear interpolation (coupling) path between clean latents and Gaussian noise, the learned field defines the probability-flow ODE 

˙x = vθ (x, t, z), (4) whose integration from t=0 to t=1 transforms noise into a motion sample. The context z is produced by the transformer from the currently unmasked tokens and the text prompt. 

Energy guidance. Following classifier-style guidance for diffusion and flow models [42], we bias sampling with an auxiliary energy E(x),

˙x = vθ (x, t, z) − w ∇xE(x), (5) where w ≥ 0 controls guidance strength. The gradient term steers trajectories toward lower-energy regions while preserving the generative prior encoded by the learned flow. In our method E(x) is the inverse-kinematics residual of Eq. (3), evaluated on the target humanoid, and guidance is applied only at inference: the generator’s weights are never updated. We use a constant guidance weight w=0 .5 rather than a schedule w(t). We integrate Eq. (5) with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta solver using 20 steps per unmasking round. Unguided baselines are drawn with MAD-HOI’s released sampler, so the comparison in Table II evaluates the guided sampler as a whole (RK4 integrator plus energy term) against the released one rather than isolating the energy term. 

Masking. The gradient is applied only to hand tokens, through the elementwise mask mhand of Alg. 1. Object tokens receive no guidance gradient, so the generated object trajectory and articulation are bit-identical between a guided and its seed-matched unguided sample. This is what makes the object-side reference, and therefore the task reward, directly comparable across the two conditions.

Generated sequences are expressed in MANO hand pa-rameters and object trajectories; policy learning requires robot joint trajectories. Wrist trajectories are converted to upper-body joint configurations with PyRoki [29], which optimizes joint states to match target wrist positions and orientations subject to the embodiment’s kinematic limits, solved from the base placement produced by guidance rather than from a fixed origin. MANO finger articulations are mapped to the XHand embodiment with dex-retargeting [32]. We additionally apply contact retargeting following Dex-Machina [10] to obtain reference contact locations ˆct on the hand links, used as auxiliary supervision during policy learning. The generated object trajectory and articulation are used unchanged as the object-side reference in observations, rewards, and terminations. Because guidance never edits object tokens (Appendix I-A), the only difference between a guided and a seed-matched unguided reference after retargeting is the arm and finger trajectory, and the base placement they are solved from.

Reachability-free reference point. Sec. IV-C notes that DexMachina’s released floating-wrist system reaches 86.1% on the four-task suite. That system commands a free-floating 6-DoF wrist with no arm chain, so it never encounters the reachability problem we study; its number is an upper bound obtained by assuming away the constraint, not a baseline.
