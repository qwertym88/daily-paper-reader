# State-Dependent Objective Coordination for End-to-End Balance and Navigation 

Xuhao Huang, Xitao Xu, Yuxin Zheng, Yunfan Ren, Zheng Gong ∗

Abstract — Traditional robotic navigation systems separate mapping, planning, and low-level control through cascaded modules, introducing computational overhead and accumulated latency. End-to-end reinforcement learning offers a compact unified policy that maps onboard observations directly to control actions. However, on inherently unstable platforms such as two-wheeled inverted-pendulum robots, navigation progress and obstacle avoidance can conflict with attitude stabilization in a strongly state-dependent manner. We propose a lightweight state-dependent objective coordination method for integrated navigation and control. A pitch-conditioned scalarization coef-ficient β(s) ∈ [0 , 1] adjusts the relative contributions of balance stabilization and navigation objectives during training, with-out extra control hierarchies or auxiliary attitude-stabilization controllers, such as proportional–integral–derivative (PID) or linear–quadratic regulator (LQR) controllers. To support zero-shot deployment, simulation training models execution delay, action hold, asynchronous and noisy observations, and dynam-ics uncertainty. Gradient analysis reveals persistent directional interference and state-dependent variation in the objectives’ relative scales. Comparisons with alternative reward-weighting, constraint, training, and control-structure strategies show that PPO+ β(s), where PPO denotes proximal policy optimization, achieves 98% and 95% success rates with small attitude excur-sions on the fixed obstacle map and the randomly generated high-obstacle-density map, respectively. Zero-shot hardware ex-periments further demonstrate bounded attitude behavior and consistent obstacle avoidance using only onboard observations. Training and evaluation code will be made publicly available upon acceptance. 

I. INTRODUCTION Real-world robot deployment increasingly requires com-pact control policies that satisfy multiple objectives within a single dynamical system under limited onboard computation and strict latency constraints. In strongly coupled robotic sys-tems, these objectives may induce state-dependent conflicts, where an action that improves one objective can degrade another. Modular or hierarchical frameworks usually han-dle such interactions through interfaces between perception, planning, navigation, and low-level control [1]–[3]. However, when multiple objectives share the same control inputs and are jointly constrained by system recoverability, interface-level coordination may lead to inconsistent or overly conser-vative behavior. We study this problem on a two-wheeled inverted-pendulum robot that must maintain attitude stability while performing goal-directed obstacle-avoiding navigation. Navi-gation progress, obstacle avoidance, and attitude stabilization all act through the same wheel-torque commands. Structural alternatives, such as stage-wise training, hierarchical control, and policy composition, do not necessarily adjust this trade-off according to the current attitude state. We further incorporate deployment-relevant discrepancies into simulation training, including execution delay, action hold, asynchronous and noisy observations, and dynamics uncertainty. The resulting actor is trained in NVIDIA Isaac Sim and directly transferred to the physical robot without policy adaptation, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

RELATED WORK Strongly coupled robotic control tasks cannot always be decomposed into independent subproblems. On underactu-ated or inherently unstable platforms, navigation, obstacle avoidance, attitude recovery, and action smoothness share the same control inputs, such that improving one objective may compromise another. Existing robotic systems commonly or-ganize these behaviors through modular, hierarchical, or hy-brid architectures that separate perception, planning, naviga-tion, and low-level control [4]–[10]. However, under strongly coupled dynamics, objective conflicts may still emerge at the action level and vary with the robot state. In reinforcement learning, multiple objectives are typically coordinated through reward design or scalarization. Weighted sums provide a simple formulation [11], but fixed weights do not explicitly adapt the relative objective preference to state-dependent risk. Dynamic weighting, preference adaptation, and reward scheduling have therefore been explored [12]– [14]. However, these approaches typically rely on predefined weighting strategies or external preference variables. In con-trast, the coefficient in this work is analytically prescribed from the robot attitude state and is used only for training-time objective scalarization without introducing additional policy inputs or online coordination. Safe reinforcement learning further shows that objective conflicts may appear in policy-gradient directions. ESPO exploits reward–cost gradient conflicts for adaptive optimization [15], motivating objective coordination beyond reward weights alone. State-wise safe reinforcement learning considers instan-taneous constraints associated with the current state, using safety evaluators, recovery policies, and reachability-based mechanisms [16], [17]. These studies indicate that safety objectives should adapt to current risk rather than rely only on global penalties. Model-based safety frameworks, such as CLF–CBF–QP and CBF-constrained MPC, enforce safety constraints through optimization [18], [19]. Although providing stronger theoretical structure, they typically require explicit dynamics, online optimization, or dedicated safety controllers. This challenge is particularly evident in obstacle-avoiding navigation of two-wheeled inverted-pendulum robots, where navigation progress must be balanced with attitude recover-ability. Prior studies have coordinated posture, mobility, and navigation through hierarchical optimization, modular plan-ning, MPC, or learning-based control [20]–[23]. However, existing approaches typically rely on separated stabilization and navigation components. In contrast, this work addresses the state-dependent conflict between balance stabilization and obstacle-avoiding navigation through training-time ob-jective coordination, yielding a compact unified policy suit-able for direct deployment. For an inherently unstable platform, inappropriate domi-nance has different consequences across attitude states. Near the upright state, excessive balance dominance may suppress goal-directed motion; at larger attitude deviations, exces-sive navigation dominance may reduce recoverability and increase the risk of falling. This motivates state-dependent objective coordination that adapts the relative objective con-tributions to the current attitude state. IV. METHODOLOGY To address the state-varying directional conflict and rel-ative gradient-scale imbalance identified above, we intro-duce state-dependent objective coordination through reward-level state-conditioned scalarization. Specifically, a state-conditioned scalarization coefficient β(st) adjusts the relative contributions of the balance-stabilization and navigation-progress objectives according to the current attitude state. Fig. 2 summarizes the complete pipeline, and the following subsections specify the scalarization coefficient and reward components.

EXPERIMENTS The experiments test the preceding formulation and pro-posed state-dependent objective coordination in three stages. We first measure directional conflict and relative gradient scale across pitch-related regimes, then compare alternative coordination strategies, and finally supplement the quantita-tive results with trajectory and zero-shot hardware evidence. The deployed actor has a model size below 8 MB and runs onboard on a Raspberry Pi 5, supporting lightweight deployment. 

A. Experimental Setup and Metrics 

All policies are trained in NVIDIA Isaac Sim using parallel environments of varying difficulty. Evaluation fol-lows the task and termination conditions in Section III-A, with randomized initial poses and a fixed maximum episode length. A fixed obstacle map is used for standard comparison, while the highest-difficulty randomized setting, termed the hardest map, is used for robustness evaluation. All methods use matched maps and initial-state distribu-tions. Unless otherwise specified, end-to-end policies share the same state and action spaces and deployment-oriented rollout model. The primary policy combines proximal policy optimization (PPO) [24] with a long short-term memory (LSTM) network [25]. Across all valid updates, the mean cosine similarity is 0.211, and 30.4% of the updates exhibit negative cosine similarity. Together with the high success rate and consistent ob-stacle avoidance under matched layouts, these results demon-strate zero-shot transfer of the learned balance–navigation policy without policy adaptation. VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION This work investigates end-to-end integrated attitude stabi-lization and obstacle-avoiding navigation for a two-wheeled inverted-pendulum robot. Since both objectives share the same wheel-torque commands, their relative importance varies with the attitude state. The proposed β(s) coordinates balance and navigation objectives during training by adapting their scalarization according to the robot state. This coordi-nation only affects reward construction, while the deployed system remains a single actor without additional control hierarchies, online scalarization, or auxiliary stabilizing con-trollers. Gradient analysis reveals persistent directional interference and state-dependent scale variation between the balance and navigation objectives. These results support state-dependent coordination over the evaluated state-independent alterna-tives. PPO+ β(s) achieves more consistent navigation success and attitude stability than alternative weighting, training, and control-structure strategies, while the DDPG+ β(s) results further provide evidence that the coordination mechanism can also benefit another RL algorithm. Zero-shot hardware experiments further demonstrate direct sim-to-real transfer using onboard observations only, with bounded attitude be-havior and consistent obstacle avoidance. Despite these results, the current formulation has several limitations. The attitude risk representation relies only on pitch magnitude and does not explicitly consider angular velocity, obstacle proximity, actuation limits, or recover-ability boundaries. Moreover, the method does not provide formal guarantees on safety, stability, or recoverability, and hardware validation is limited to the current platform and environments.
