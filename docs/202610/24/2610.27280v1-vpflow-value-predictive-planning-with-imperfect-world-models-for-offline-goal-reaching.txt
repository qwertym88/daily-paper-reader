# VPFlow: Value-Predictive Planning with Imperfect World Models for Offline Goal Reaching 

Yunzhe Hu, Dong Xu 

School of Computing and Data Science The University of Hong Kong Offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning enables skill acquisition by reaching any goal from any state without dense rewards. Model-based methods allow for planning by scaling test-time compute to solve complex tasks. Combining both holds appealing promise for pre-training on suboptimal demonstrations and zero-shot adaptation via goal specification and planning. Yet existing methods assume effective planning requires accurate next-state prediction in pixel or latent space. In this work, we show that value prediction is more useful for planning, whereas accurate state prediction is often unnecessary. Our key insight is that offline goal reaching naturally admits a closed-form Monte Carlo return, enabling value regression to replace temporal-difference bootstrapping. We use this return as the sole supervision for latent world models: imagined latents are trained to predict goal-reaching value rather than match future states, implicitly preventing representation collapse. These planning-oriented representations let imperfect world models imagine on the level sets of value field beyond dataset transitions. We then present VPFlow , a value-predictive planner that pairs the world models with a one-step flow policy and plans in its noise space via evolutionary search. It enables policy improvement with imagined generalized advantage and reduces out-of-distribution queries common in action-space planning. On 30 state and pixel OGBench datasets, VPFlow 

surpasses model-free baselines by at least 30.3 and 10.1 percentage points in success rate, respectively, and outperforms latent planners LeWorldModel, PLDM, and TD-MPC2. It achieves this despite a latent prediction error orders of magnitude larger than with state prediction, while minimizing this error lowers success rates. This demonstrates that value-predictive representations, not exact state prediction, are what offline planning requires.

# 1 Introduction 

Offline reinforcement learning (RL) seeks to learn reusable behaviors purely from previously collected trajectories with no additional environment interaction (Levine et al., 2020). In its goal-conditioned setting, an agent is tasked with reaching a desired goal at test time without optimizing a hand-designed reward (Kaelbling, 1993; Schaul et al., 2015; Park et al., 2025a). This makes offline goal reaching especially attractive for reward-free skill acquisition: relabeling future observations as goals turns one unlabeled dataset into supervision for many tasks (Andrychowicz et al., 2017). Pairing it with model-based planning is a natural next step: pre-train on offline data once, then adapt to new goals in zero shot by searching over imagined futures at test time. This combination is promising, yet comparatively unexplored. Strong offline goal-conditioned methods, spanning implicit Q-learning (Kostrikov et al., 2022), contrastive learning (Eysenbach et al., 2022), and quasi-metric representations (Wang et al., 2023; Myers et al., 2025), learn a reactive policy or value function from relabeled trajectories and act greedily at deployment. While robust and efficient, they are static at test time: once training ends, additional inference compute cannot improve decisions on challenging tasks. Model-based planning offers a complementary advantage. From Dyna to TD-MPC (Sutton, 1991; Hafner et al., 2019, 2020; Hansen et al., 2022), learned world models convert extra test-time compute into better decisions by searching over imagined futures. Schwarzer et al., 2021; Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b). We argue that this state-predictive assumption is both overly restrictive and misdirected. What a planner needs is not a model that reproduces the next observation, but one that preserves the relative ordering of candidate futures according to their goal-reaching value. This view builds on a rich lineage: value prediction networks learn dynamics only as much as they reproduce Bellman backups (Oh et al., 2017); universal value functions and successor representations factor value into future occupancy and a goal-dependent readout (Dayan, 1993; Schaul et al., 2015; Barreto et al., 2017); and the value-equivalence principle formalizes when a model that is inaccurate as a simulator remains exact for control (Grimm et al., 2020). We take this principle a step further: an imagined latent only needs to land on the right level set of the value field, so the representation worth learning for planning is value-predictive rather than state-predictive . Offline goal reaching makes this directly actionable. Because the relabeled reward is sparse, the Monte Carlo (MC) return from any state to any future goal on its trajectory is a closed-form function of their temporal distance. Value learning thus simplifies from temporal-difference bootstrapping to direct regression, and that regression target can supervise the latent dynamics end to end, acting as an implicit regularizer against representation collapse. A model trained this way may be an imperfect simulator yet remain an effective planner. Motivated by this view, we introduce Value-Predictive Flow (VPFlow) , which plans for offline goal-reaching tasks with a deliberately imperfect world model. VPFlow jointly learns an observation encoder, a goal-agnostic latent dynamics model, and goal-conditioned value heads alongside a distilled flow-matching policy with action chunks. Imagined latents are trained purely to be value-predictive : rolled forward with dataset actions, the value predicted at each imagined latent is regressed towards the closed-form return of the corresponding future state. No observation is reconstructed, no target latent is matched, and no bootstrapped target network is required. Figure 1 contrasts alternative latent-predictive architectures with ours. To translate a value-predictive model into reliable control, the planner must propose feasible, in-support actions. Standard zeroth-order planners such as the cross-entropy method (CEM) and model predictive path integral (MPPI) (Rubinstein, 1999; Williams et al., 2017) perturb raw action sequences, which in offline settings quickly leave the data support and expose the value function to out-of-distribution (OOD) queries. 

VPFlow instead searches in the noise space of its one-step flow policy (Park et al., 2025c), where each candidate noise vector decodes into action chunks at the current latent state. Because of behavior-cloning 2regularization, the noise search warm-starts at multi-modal, in-support proposals and stays anchored to the data manifold. We guide this noise steering using the generalized advantage estimator (GAE) (Schulman et al., 2016) over imagined rollouts, instantiated as an evolution strategy via annealed mutation and elite selection. Across 30 locomotion and manipulation datasets from OGBench (Park et al., 2025a), VPFlow raises the mean success rate from 32 .9% to 63 .2% over 17 state datasets and from 27 .5% to 37 .6% over 13 pixel datasets relative to the strongest non-hierarchical baselines, while substantially outperforming the latent state-predictive planners LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026b), PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025), and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., 2024). Crucially, these gains hold while the model’s next-latent prediction error sits orders of magnitude above that of its state-predictive counterpart; conversely, enforcing state prediction closes that gap at the cost of lowering the success rate. This demonstrates that offline planning does not require a faithful state simulator, but rather a value-predictive one. Our contributions are as follows: 1. We argue and demonstrate that value-predictive latent world models, trained solely via closed-form Monte Carlo return regression, avoid representation collapse and suffice for offline planning even as state-prediction error grows, challenging the pervasive assumption of learning state-predictive world models for planning. 2. We propose a test-time planning algorithm tailored to generative flow policies in their noise space, steering initial noise towards higher imagined generalized advantage while avoiding the out-of-distribution action queries of existing action-space planning methods. 3. We show across 30 state and pixel datasets on OGBench that VPFlow surpasses model-free baselines and latent state-predictive planners, and scales favorably with test-time compute on long-horizon tasks. 

# 2 Related Work 

We position VPFlow within three lines of work with broader literature in Appendix A.  

> Offline goal-conditioned RL.

Offline RL learns a policy from pre-collected datasets without interacting with the environment, and needs techniques to keep actions on the data manifold during value learning (Levine et al., 2020; Kostrikov et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2020; Fujimoto and Gu, 2021; Park et al., 2025c). In the goal-conditioned setting, an agent aims to reach any goal from any state (Kaelbling, 1993), and hindsight relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) can convert any trajectories, even failed ones, into successful ones with the goal actually reached. With high-coverage datasets, this may enable learning a universal value function that empowers the policy to generalize to unseen goals in zero shot (Schaul et al., 2015; Park et al., 2024).  

> Representations for planning with world models.

World models seek a compact representation and transition dynamics from observations, where value can be learned in imagination (Sutton, 1991; Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2020; Hansen et al., 2022). Yet what representations are more effective for planning is still an open question. Reconstruction-based methods harness the dense supervision from pixels and have achieved remarkable success (Hafner et al., 2019, 2025a). Self-predictive representation holds promise for a more powerful model (Schwarzer et al., 2021; LeCun, 2022; Bagatella et al., 2026), but needs stop-gradient, EMA, or regular-ization to prevent collapse (Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026b). More critically, it fails to exploit the rich temporal-distance supervision naturally available from goal reaching. This makes its usefulness for planning not directly obvious. Our work aims to reveal what is essential and necessary for planning-oriented representations.  

> Test-time search.

With a learned world model, previous studies primarily plan actions by sampling-based optimization (Williams et al., 2017; Janner et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022; Park et al., 2026) or search (Schrittwieser et al., 2020). Yet action-space planning may suffer heavily in the offline setting, especially with low-coverage data, in that off-manifold actions can severely exploit the planning objective. Advances in generative policies have turned to steering the noise at test time to keep actions in-distribution, but they need reward or other external signals and have yet to show scaling effectiveness (Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Patil et al., 2026). In contrast, VPFlow can improve itself at test time by noise steering 1 with imagined rollouts, even with imperfect world models.

We motivate VPFlow through the value equiva-lence principle (Grimm et al., 2020), which states that two dynamics and reward models are value-equivalent with respect to a set of value functions if they induce the same Bellman operator. Conse-quently, one can learn another set of dynamics and reward models beyond the training examples but still keep the same effect for policy learning. We extend this view to world modeling for offline goal reaching: two predictive representations ˆs, ˆs′ ∈S are value-equivalent with respect to a goal if they are evaluated identically by the goal-conditioned value function, i.e. , Vϕ(ˆ s, g ) = Vϕ(ˆ s′, g ) (Figure 2). In this setting, the Bellman operator can be written in closed form with sparse reward given a goal state, reducing recursive temporal-difference learning (Oh et al., 2017; Schrittwieser et al., 2020) to direct regression on fixed targets. Noise space steering. Planning has been widely conducted by directly optimizing 

open-loop actions on an unconstrained sup-port. For long-horizon rollouts, actions tend to drift away from the data distribu-tion, making the planning objective unre-liable. One of the key benefits of a gen-erative policy is that after training, its noise space can be optimized to search for better actions while still being close to the training distribution (Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025). We adopt this strategy to steer the noise to optimize Equation 10 that uses the imagined states unrolled from the learned dynamics. 

ε-ES: evolution strategy in noise space. 

CEM planners used in model-based meth-ods maintain an elite set of actions, from which an online mean and standard de-viation are updated (Janner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022; Maes et al., 2026b). However, this design choice does not directly translate to the noise space, as their distribution differs. Inspired by a previous gradient-free optimizer for diffusion noise (Yang et al., 2024), we instead develop an evolution strategy that mutates samples of each elite in noise space and greedily keeps the ones that score higher by the objective in Equation 10. This allows the policy prior learned during training to improve itself by adding more compute at test time without weight updates. We present the search process, which runs for K iterations, in Algorithm 1 with more details in Appendix B.6. 

# 5 Experiments 

We aim to address four questions in this section: Q1 : How does VPFlow compare with model-free baselines and model-based methods that similarly plan with latent world models for offline goal-reaching tasks? Q2 :Are state-predictive representations useful for planning, or do the value-predictive ones play a more essential role? Q3 : Does test-time planning help, and does the search space matter more than the search algorithm? 

Q4 : How does the learned latent actually behave during rollouts? 

Setup. We use the official OGBench datasets and tasks (Park et al., 2025a): 17 state-based datasets and 13 pixel-based datasets. The metric is the goal-reaching success rate ( %, ↑), averaged over the five tasks within each dataset. Model-free baselines are the non-hierarchical methods reported by OGBench: goal-conditioned behavioral cloning ( GCBC ) (Ghosh et al., 2021), goal-conditioned implicit value learning ( GCIVL )and goal-conditioned implicit Q-learning ( GCIQL ) (Kostrikov et al., 2022), quasi-metric RL ( QRL ) (Wang et al., 2023), and contrastive RL ( CRL ) (Eysenbach et al., 2022). 5.2 Q2: Value-Predictive or State-Predictive Latents? 

This experiment aims to verify which representations are more favor-able for planning. The default model trains the rollout with the value-prediction loss LVP (Equation 6); the state-predictive model trains it with the latent-prediction loss LLP (Equation 13), which matches the predicted latents to the encoded states. Other configurations stay the same. Figure 5 tracks the success rate and the next-latent prediction error during training on four datasets.  

> Lower state prediction error, worse planning.

The state-predictive controller reduces the next-latent error by one to eight orders of magnitude, yet its success does not improve on any of the four datasets and falls most sharply on scene-play (78 .4% to 28 .0% , Figure 5a) and visual-cube-single (79 .2% to 0.0% , Figure 5c). A model that predicts its own next latent almost perfectly is therefore not necessarily a model that plans well. Predicting the value is more essential for planning.  

> The failure mode is collapse, and value prediction prevents it.

On visual-cube-single (Figure 5c), the latent prediction term collapses to near zero: the encoder finds a trivially predictable latent and the agent never reaches the goal, the degeneracy that self-predictive objectives prevent through explicit anti-collapse techniques, none of which our architecture has. In contrast, value prediction in Equation 6 promotes temporally ordered latents that implicitly prevent collapse. Appendix D.2 provides ablations on explicit regularization over latent prediction.

5.4 Q4: How Do the Latents Behave in Rollouts? 

Figure 7 depicts the geometry of imagined rollouts on Visual AntMaze to demonstrate the behavior of imperfect yet planning-effective learned world models. Under value-predictive training, the dynamics model evolves latents to where the correct goal-reaching value is forecasted by the value function, without being constrained to match the corresponding embeddings from the encoder. This helps explain why the value-predictive controller plans effectively despite the large next-latent prediction error demonstrated in Section 5.2. It also suggests how value prediction harnesses exploratory play data more effectively: closed-form value regression structures the latent space by temporal proximity to the goal, allowing it to leave out irrelevant routes and discover a latent geometry suited to reaching the goal. In contrast, a state-predictive world model is trained to faithfully fit latent transitions in the dataset, which may limit its generalization to unseen goals. Taken together, these results demonstrate that accurate latent-state prediction is not strictly necessary for successful planning. Imperfect world models can remain effective and even yield superior performance (Figure 12), provided that they preserve value equivalence. The evidence that the agent can successfully reach the goal despite substantial latent prediction error suggests that latent error is an unreliable indicator of planning success; instead, value prediction should serve as a more informative metric. 

# 6 Conclusions 

We have challenged the pervasive assumption that planning requires a state-predictive world model. VPFlow 

trains its observation encoder and latent dynamics solely via closed-form Monte Carlo return regression—a supervision signal naturally available in sparse-reward offline goal reaching—and plans at test time by steering a distilled one-step flow policy within its noise space to maximize imagined generalized advantage. Across 30 state and pixel datasets on OGBench, VPFlow substantially improves upon model-free baselines and latent-predictive planners despite much higher next-latent prediction error. In contrast, enforcing state prediction closes that error gap at the cost of degraded planning performance. Moreover, test-time noise steering reliably elevates the reactive policy prior, demonstrating that searching within an in-support noise space matters more than the specific optimization algorithm. More broadly, these findings indicate that effective latent planning favors value-predictive representations for world models and an in-distribution search space for planning, rather than a faithful state simulator. # A Extended Related Work 

This appendix expands upon the three lines of work discussed in Section 2, positioning VPFlow within the broader literature. 

A.1 Offline and Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning 

Offline RL learns policies from static datasets without environment interaction, mitigating distribution shift either by biasing the policy towards the behavior distribution (Fujimoto et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2019; Fujimoto and Gu, 2021) or by regularizing critic updates (Kumar et al., 2020; Kostrikov et al., 2022). To capture multi-modal play data and improve long-horizon credit assignment, Flow Q-learning (FQL) distills a conditional flow-matching policy into a one-step network (Lipman et al., 2023; Park et al., 2025c), and action chunking introduces temporal abstraction (Li et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025b). In goal-reaching settings (Kaelbling, 1993), universal value function approximators (Schaul et al., 2015) and hindsight relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) extract supervision from reward-free trajectories, which offline methods combine with iterated supervised learning (Ghosh et al., 2021), latent plans from play (Lynch et al., 2020), contrastive learning (Eysenbach et al., 2022), quasi-metric representations (Wang et al., 2023; Myers et al., 2025), and subgoal hierarchies (Park et al., 2023), standardized by OGBench (Park et al., 2025a). VPFlow adopts a distilled action-chunk flow policy as its behavior prior (Park et al., 2025c; Li et al., 2025) but departs from these static, model-free methods in two ways: it replaces TD, contrastive, or quasi-metric representation learning with closed-form Monte Carlo return regression over multi-step imagined rollouts, and it scales test-time compute via receding-horizon noise steering rather than acting greedily at deployment. 

A.2 Model-Based RL and World Models 

Model-based RL dates back to Dyna (Sutton, 1991) and Gaussian-process policy search (Deisenroth and Rasmussen, 2011), with deep variants learning dynamics via pixel reconstruction (Watter et al., 2015; Finn and Levine, 2017; Hafner et al., 2019) or latent imagination (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2020, 2025a,b) and limiting compounding error through short rollouts (Janner et al., 2019; Park et al., 2026). Offline model-based methods such as MOPO (Yu et al., 2020), MOReL (Kidambi et al., 2020), COMBO (Yu et al., 2021), and MBOP (Argenson and Dulac-Arnold, 2021) penalize or truncate rollouts where the model is uncertain, or plan with an ensemble model and a behavior prior like MBOP (Argenson and Dulac-Arnold, 2021). Yet they still assume that accurate state prediction is a prerequisite for planning. Value-equivalent models take an alternative position (Grimm et al., 2020; M. Moerland et al., 2023): value prediction networks (VPN) (Oh et al., 2017) and MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) train dynamics purely through predicted rewards, values, and policies. VPFlow belongs to the value-equivalent family, but unlike online methods VPN and MuZero, it exploits closed-form hindsight Monte Carlo returns in offline goal reaching as the sole supervision for both the encoder and dynamics, eliminating target networks, EMA, state-matching loss, and TD learning from training. 

A.3 Predictive Representations 

Predictive representations characterize a state by its future outcomes rather than its sensory appearance (Littman and Sutton, 2001; Carvalho et al., 2024). One line of work builds on successor representations and features (Dayan, 1993; Barreto et al., 2017, 2018; Borsa et al., 2019), forward-backward representations (Touati and Ollivier, 2021; Blier et al., 2021), and behavior foundation models (Pirotta et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024) to predict discounted future occupancy for zero-shot reactive readout. A parallel line learns with a self-predictive objective that forecasts future latent embeddings, relying on target encoders, stop-gradients (Schwarzer et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2024; Fujimoto et al., 2025; Bagatella et al., 2026), or explicit regularizers (LeCun, 2022; Dawid and LeCun, 2024; Assran et al., 2025; Van Assel et al., 2025) to prevent collapse. Whereas occupancy-based representations omit multi-step lookahead search at test time and self-predictive world models require explicit anti-collapse heuristics without exploiting temporal-distance 16 signals, VPFlow bridges both perspectives: it supervises multi-step latent rollouts with goal-conditioned values, which implicitly prevents collapse and organizes the latent space around temporal proximity to the goal. 

A.4 Planning in Latent Space 

Sampling-based optimizers such as CEM and MPPI (Rubinstein, 1999; Williams et al., 2017) are standard planners in world models (Hafner et al., 2019; Janner et al., 2019; Hansen et al., 2022), complemented in visual and offline domains by energy-based or functional-distance costs (Du et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2021), hierarchical subgoal search (Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026), and generative adversarial behavior priors in GOPlan (Wang et al., 2024). Closest to our setting, PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025) and LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026b) train latent-predictive dynamics with VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022) or SIGReg (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025) regularization and plan in action space via CEM to minimize Euclidean distance to the goal embedding, whereas recent generative policy methods steer diffusion or flow noise at test time using external rewards or online interaction (Salimans et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2024; Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Patil et al., 2026). In contrast, VPFlow avoids both the out-of-distribution action queries of open-loop action-space CEM and the external reward requirements of prior noise-steering methods: it searches directly in the noise space of its distilled flow policy to maximize imagined generalized advantage under its value-predictive world model, keeping planned actions on the data manifold.

B.1 Datasets and Preprocessing  

> Datasets.

We use the official OGBench datasets (Park et al., 2025a) covering both locomotion and robotic manipulation tasks without modification. The state suite comprises 17 datasets: pointmaze-{medium, large, giant, teleport}-navigate , antmaze-{medium, large, giant, teleport}-navigate , cube-{single, double, triple, quadruple}-play , scene-play , and puzzle-{3x3, 4x4, 4x5, 4x6}-play . The pixel suite comprises the 13 visual- counterparts of the AntMaze, Cube, Scene, and Puzzle datasets, with 64 × 64 × 3 observations. Each dataset has five evaluation tasks specified by start-goal pairs.  

> Training samples and goal relabeling.

A training sample is a length-H segment (st, a t, . . . , a t+H−1) drawn uniformly from the dataset trajectories, with the chunk length H in Table 4. The value goal gV (g in Section 4.3) is sampled from a mixture of three sources, with the probabilities in Table 4: the current observation st; a future observation st+d on the same trajectory, with the offset d drawn from the geometric distribution Geom(1 − γ); and a uniformly random dataset observation. For each offset h ∈ { 0, . . . , H },the MC target is Rt+h = (1 − γdh )/(γ − 1) with dh as the number of temporal steps from st+h to gV , and the mask Mt+h is one if and only if gV is on the same trajectory at or after index t + h. Random goals therefore contribute no value target. The policy goal gπ (g in Section 4.4) is randomly sampled from a future observation on the same trajectory.  

> Data augmentation.

For pixel-based tasks, we apply random-crop augmentation (edge padding, then cropping back to 64 × 64 ; Table 4) using one shared crop per sample for the sampled observation and both goals, except for the Visual AntMaze environment, following the practice in OGBench.

Zeroth-order steering. ε-ES (Algorithm 1) uses forward evaluations of A, allowing search without informative gradients. It initializes Ne elites at the policy mode and generates N Gaussian mutations per iteration, distributed evenly among the elites. The mutation scale is annealed as σk = max( σmin , σ max ρk) for k =0, . . . , K − 1. The final plan is sampled from the elites with probabilities proportional to exp( βA e), retaining several high-scoring alternatives rather than selecting only the maximum. B.7 Planner Variants 

The CEM variants in Table 2 optimize the same advantage A as ε-ES, but refit a diagonal Gaussian at each iteration rather than retain and mutate individual elites. Each iteration scores N candidates and refits the mean and standard deviation using the top Ne candidates with normalized weights we ∝ exp( βA e). This distribution refit does not preserve ε-ES’s monotonic elite-score guarantee. 

Noise-space CEM. ε-CEM (Algorithm 4) applies the CEM refitting scheme in the policy’s noise space, initializing the sampling distribution as N (0, I ). Candidate noises are decoded through Dec of Algorithm 1 and ranked by A. After each refit, the coordinate-wise standard deviations σ are clipped between σmin and the annealed upper bound σk = max( σmin , σ max ρk), with k indexed from zero. The final plan is decoded from an elite sampled with the same softmax weights. 

Action-space CEM. A-CEM (Algorithm 5) searches directly over action sequences, warm-starting the initial mean at the policy’s direct output μa = Dec( 0) and setting the initial standard deviation to σa = σmax 1.This initialization is recomputed at each planning step from the current observation, without carrying over the previous plan. Candidates are clipped to [−1, 1] before scoring, and the sampling standard deviations use the same annealed bounds. The executed plan is the final fitted action mean. Both variants execute the first Hexec actions, return the direct policy output when K = 0 , and add no execution noise. They reuse ε-ES’s planning hyperparameters on each dataset (Tables 8 and 9). > Goal-conditioned TD-MPC2.

To support OGBench’s reward-free datasets and five evaluation goals per dataset, we add goal conditioning and hindsight reward relabeling to stable-worldmodel’s TD-MPC2. A shared CNN encodes the current and goal images into zt and zg , each normalized by SimNorm. The reward head, Q-heads, and policy additionally receive zg , while the dynamics fϕ remains goal-independent. Hindsight rewards r(s, g ) use OGBench’s success criteria, computed from the joint positions and button states stored in the dataset. For Visual AntMaze, the reward is zero within the goal tolerance and −1 otherwise. For Visual Cube, Puzzle, and Scene, it equals the number of satisfied subgoals minus their total. These subgoals concern cube positions in Cube, button states in Puzzle, and cubes, buttons, the drawer, and the window in Scene. TD-MPC2 thus receives simulator-state reward supervision and partial subgoal credit, providing more informative signals than VPFlow ’s sparse goal reward.  

> Adaptations to OGBench.

We use the shared CEM settings in Table 3 and execute the full planned actions before replanning. The horizon spans five action chunks ( 25 environment steps) for LeWM and PLDM, and five environment steps for TD-MPC2. We align the data, training budget, and task evaluation with VPFlow 

as follows: • Data. We convert the official pixel datasets to HDF5 format, preserving observations, actions, and the simulator states needed for hindsight rewards. Training samples are trajectory subsequences with frames spaced by the baseline’s frameskip; a random 90% of these samples forms the training split. • Training. Each optimizer receives 500 ,000 updates, matching VPFlow ’s training budget on pixel inputs. • Evaluation. We replace stable-worldmodel’s dataset-based start-goal sampling with OGBench’s five built-in tasks. Each episode is reset with its task identifier and retains the task’s goal image throughout. Success follows the environment’s native indicator within its time limit, as for VPFlow .23 B.9 Software and Hardware

GAE instantiates the MPC objective in Equation 2 by using temporal-difference (TD) errors as potential-based shaped rewards. Sparse goal-conditioned rewards provide no ranking signal among candidate trajectories that do not reach the goal within the planning horizon. The TD error in Equation 9, δt+h = rt+h + γV t+h+1 − Vt+h,provides a densified reward for credit assignment in planning, where Vt+h := Vϕ(ˆ zt+h, z g ). 31 F Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions 

We discuss several limitations of VPFlow and outline promising avenues for future research. 

F.1 Discussion  

> Trajectory smoothness and step efficiency.

While VPFlow reliably reaches specified goals across diverse environments, the resulting trajectories are not always smooth or time-optimal. Because the closed-form Monte Carlo return supervises the value function using hindsight temporal distances from suboptimal play trajectories, the learned value landscape reflects the behavior distribution rather than shortest-path optimality. Furthermore, receding-horizon replanning over discrete action chunks can introduce minor discontinuities between consecutive plans. Devising new representations or abstractions for actions or shortest-path planning objectives to encourage completing tasks in the minimal number of steps remains an important next step.  

> Advantage over-optimization under extended search.

As shown in Section 5.3, increasing the number of steering iterations K generally improves planning performance, yet we also notice that on certain datasets the success rate peaks and subsequently declines even as the imagined generalized advantage increases monotonically. This divergence indicates that aggressive test-time search may eventually exploit approximation errors in the learned dynamics and value heads rather than discovering better behaviors. Combining noise-space steering with uncertainty quantification or pessimistic penalties is a natural extension to prevent over-optimization.  

> Short planning horizons.

VPFlow currently plans over a relatively short lookahead horizon (typically a single action chunk of length H = 16 ), relying on the terminal value estimate to measure longer-term progress towards goals. While effective when the learned value landscape is well-shaped, a short planning horizon can limit performance on complex, multi-stage tasks where local value gradients are flat or deceptive. Extending the effective lookahead to a horizon of hundreds of steps without incurring prohibitive rollout variance or computational overhead is a valuable direction.  

> Per-dataset hyperparameter tuning.

Both training and test-time steering hyperparameters, such as learning rates, distillation weights, and planning iterations, are currently tuned per dataset (Tables 5 and 9). Although this protocol reveals the full capability of value-predictive planning across distinct locomotion and manipulation tasks, manual per-dataset tuning is laborious. 

F.2 Future Directions  

> Unified training across heterogeneous datasets.

An appealing step towards eliminating per-dataset hyperparam-eter tuning is to train a single generalist value-predictive agent across heterogeneous datasets, e.g. , combining diverse OGBench datasets such as exploratory play and noisy trajectories from different environments. Be-cause closed-form hindsight return regression does not rely on fragile temporal-difference bootstrapping or task-specific reward scales, it is well suited for multi-task pre-training under a single, unified configuration.  

> Scaling value-predictive world models.

Our results demonstrate that compact, value-predictive world models already outperform state-predictive counterparts on standard benchmarks. Investigating the scaling laws of value-predictive representations, with respect to model capacity, dataset size, and task diversity, is a compelling future direction to explore their limits.  

> Synthesizing long-horizon experience at test time.

Unlike Dyna-style methods that synthesize imagined rollouts to update the value function or policy during training, VPFlow focuses on planning within imagination at test time. Extending this paradigm to generate reliable, long-horizon synthetic experience during deployment—for instance, by chaining multi-chunk imagined rollouts or performing lightweight test-time adaptation and self-improvement in imagination after offline pre-training—could bridge the gap between short-horizon action-chunk steering and long-horizon reasoning. 32
