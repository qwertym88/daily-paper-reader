# SocialCoAct: Enabling and Benchmarking Egocentric Social Co-Action for VLM-Based Humanoid Robots 

Jiancong Ma 1 Kun Li 1 Jin Jiang 1 Shengcai Liao 1∗

Abstract — Enabling humanoid robots to participate effec-tively in social interactions is important for their integration into human-centered environments. Existing methods, however, typically map pre-generated speech directly to continuous motion trajectories but have limited awareness of complex social environments and lack explicit high-level semantic action planning. We introduce SocialCoAct, a unified agent-centered framework for VLM-based egocentric social co-action. We for-mulate egocentric social co-action as an end-to-end agent task and develop SocialCoAct-Agent, which transforms egocentric observations and dialogue history into verbal responses and atomic action sequences, aligns each action with its corre-sponding utterance span, and executes the resulting behaviors in MuJoCo and on a physical humanoid robot. To evaluate the VLM capabilities required by this task, we construct SocialCoAct-Bench from continuous egocentric recordings of natural multi-party interactions. The benchmark comprises 423 human-verified multiple-choice questions across three subtasks: Long Episode Understanding (LEU), Speaker Intention Un-derstanding (SIU), and Coordinated Action Planning (CAP). Across a diverse set of closed-source and open-weight VLMs, including general and egocentric models, the highest overall accuracy is 77.54%, while accuracy on the hard subset reaches only 46.51%. Extended reasoning improves Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B by 19.62 and 17.02 percentage points, respec-tively, but reduces inference throughput by 53.6 × and 20.2 ×.Visual-input ablations further show that the benefits of visual context vary across models. SocialCoAct provides a foundation for developing and evaluating VLM-based humanoid agents that coordinate context-aware speech and action in complex social interactions. The codebase and benchmark will be made publicly available upon publication. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid robots are increasingly expected to participate in everyday social interactions. An ideal humanoid robot should be able to visually comprehend complex social scenes and ongoing activities, infer the intentions conveyed through human language, and respond naturally through coordinated verbal and bodily behaviors [1], [2]. Such responses should not only be semantically appropriate but also align with the interaction context, timing, and intended addressee, thereby resembling the integrated way in which humans communi-cate through speech and action [3], [4]. Recent systems can generate synchronized co-speech ges-tures for physical robots, while a few can jointly produce ver-bal and embodied responses [5]. However, their gestures are typically conditioned on pre-generated text or speech rather than grounded in an online visual understanding of evolving

Consequently, existing evaluations do not jointly assess whether verbal and bodily responses are appropriate given scene dynamics, conversational history, speaker intention, ongoing activities, and addressee relations in multi-party interactions. Evaluating this capability poses a parallel challenge. Social appropriateness cannot be judged from an isolated frame or utterance; it depends on conversational history, speaker intention, turn-taking dynamics, addressee relations, and the norms of the ongoing activity. Moreover, because humans communicate through coordinated speech and bodily behav-ior, an evaluation must consider not only the appropriateness of each modality but also their semantic alignment [6]. Ex-isting evaluations of robotic social intelligence, however, pri-marily focus on human–robot interaction or the recognition and correction of observed social behaviors [7]. Although these tasks assess the perception of social cues and inap-propriate behavior, they rarely examine whether a humanoid agent can actively generate a contextually appropriate verbal response together with semantically aligned bodily actions. To address this gap, we introduce SocialCoAct (Social Co ordinated Act ion), a unified VLM-based framework for egocentric social co-action in humanoid robots. The frame-work includes SocialCoAct-Agent , which reasons about the ongoing interaction and the current speaker’s intent from egocentric observations and dialogue history, generates a verbal response together with an ordered sequence of atomic actions, aligns each action with its corresponding utterance span, and executes the resulting co-speech behavior in MuJoCo and on a physical humanoid robot. RELATED WORK 

A. Embodied Social Response Generation 

Existing research on embodied social response genera-tion spans facial reaction generation, gesture synthesis, and multimodal conversational behavior. Responsive Listening Head Generation introduces a benchmark and baseline for synthesizing listener head movements and facial expressions from a speaker’s audiovisual behavior, while REACT de-fines online and offline settings for generating diverse and appropriate facial reactions in dyadic conversations [8], [9]. More recent systems capture richer interaction dynamics. PolySLGen generates speaking and listening reactions for a target participant based on the speech and motion history of polyadic interactions [10], whereas ProAct combines long-horizon social reasoning with streaming nonverbal behavior to enable proactive embodied interaction [11]. Collectively, these studies have advanced response-generation benchmarks, generative architectures, and inte-grated interactive systems. However, their evaluations are generally not designed to isolate the upstream decision-making capabilities required of VLMs, including long-horizon contextual understanding, speaker-intent inference, and the construction of language-aligned action plans for humanoid robots. 

B. Multimodal Social and Egocentric Benchmarks 

Existing benchmarks evaluate different aspects of social intelligence. Social-IQ and Social Genome assess multimodal reasoning about social situations, whereas SIV-Bench further evaluates social scene understanding, latent-state reasoning, and interaction dynamics prediction. SocialGesture focuses on gesture recognition, temporal localization, and vision-language reasoning in multi-person interactions [12], [13], [14], [15]. EasyCom, EgoCom, and Ego4D provide first-person recordings of natural communication, whereas JRDB-Social captures individual behaviors and group dynamics from the perspective of a mobile robot [16], [17], [18], [19]. Recent benchmarks have increasingly emphasized interac-tive decision-making. HoloAssist evaluates mistake detection and intervention-type prediction in egocentric task assistance, whereas OmniMMI benchmarks proactive reasoning and Unitree-SDK Unitree-SDK MuJoCo Sim ASR Teleimager Client Audio Input Image Input Trajectories User Client

A. SocialCoAct-Agent Architecture 

In this section, we present the architecture of SocialCoAct-Agent. The agent is built on the vLLM codebase [23]. Fig. 2 illustrates its main components and overall workflow. At each interaction turn, SocialCoAct-Agent receives both speech and visual observations. The user’s speech is recorded through the microphone as an audio input at and transcribed by the Whisper-based ASR service [24]: 

xt = fASR (at),

where xt denotes the transcribed instruction. In parallel, the head-mounted camera continuously streams images from the robot PC through Teleimager. The client retrieves the most recent RGB frames It when an interaction request is issued. The client then combines the instruction xt, the images It, and the conversation history Ht into a multimodal planning request with: 

Rt = {xt, I t, H t}.

The request is processed by SocialCoAct-Agent. The model jointly interprets the textual instruction, visual observation, and dialogue context and produces a structured response. The output parser separates the model output into a natural-language response st, an ordered execution sequence Et, and a set of atomic action plans Pt:

(st, E t, P t) = fθ (Rt) = fθ (xt, I t, H t).

Here, Et preserves the ordering between speech segments and their associated actions, while each element of Pt

specifies an atomic action and its semantic anchor. The agent does not directly execute joint values generated by the language model. Instead, each predicted action is vali-dated against a predefined action whitelist and mapped to a corresponding trajectory. The resulting response, containing 

response text and plans , is returned to the client as a structured JSON message. After receiving the planning result, the client dispatches the response to separate speech and motion branches. In the speech branch, the natural-language response is synthesized by Qwen3-TTS [25]: 

ˆat = fTTS (st),

where ˆat is the generated audio returned to the interface for playback. In the motion branch, the ordered action plans are converted into predefined joint trajectories. The lower body remains fixed in the standing configuration, while the atomic trajectories are processed using interpolation, joint-velocity limiting, and smooth transition functions. In the current simulation setting, the resulting trajectory is rendered in MuJoCo and returned to the interface. The same structured action plan can optionally be forwarded to the robot-side controller for physical execution. The semantic anchors in Pt maintain a turn-level corre-spondence between speech content and gestures. The cur-rent implementation generates the complete planning result before dispatching speech synthesis and action execution.

Tag-only events, unintelligible fragments, fillers, and isolated low-information acknowledgments are excluded as annotation targets. Episode durations are restricted to 12–60 seconds. All videos are standardized to a resolution of 1280 × 720 at 10 FPS and paired with timestamped transcripts. 

2) Human-in-the-loop Labeling: Given the distinct char-acteristics of these tasks, we adopt a hybrid annotation strat-egy. The visual understanding tasks are annotated through model-assisted pre-labeling followed by expert validation, whereas the action-planning task currently relies more heav-ily on human judgment because of its subjective and context-sensitive nature. We first select representative turns and episodes that are suitable for annotation. For each selected video segment, we employ GPT-5.6 [26] to generate initial annotations, including a high-level description of the ongoing activity and an inference of the current speaker’s intention and expression. Each prediction is accompanied by a confi-dence score c, and predictions with c < τ , where τ = 0 .7,are discarded. Domain experts then independently review the remaining pre-labeled outputs to verify their factual correctness and semantic appropriateness. To annotate appropriate humanoid actions, we rely ex-clusively on human annotators. These annotations involve inherently pragmatic and subjective judgments that require a nuanced understanding of social context, conversational dynamics, and cultural norms. Each short conversational turn is evaluated by three human annotators, who select the most appropriate expressive action from a predefined vocabulary of 23 atomic action types. Each action type has a distinct communicative purpose and affective meaning, such as whether an action conveys a positive or negative attitude or serves to inquire, acknowledge, encourage, reject, or express another intention. When disagreements arise, the final annotation is determined by majority vote among the three annotators. To promote cross-cultural recognizabil-ity and broad acceptability, the action vocabulary was co-designed with participants from diverse cultural backgrounds to minimize conflicting interpretations across cultural con-texts. Importantly, the annotated actions do not necessarily reproduce the ego agent’s observed real-world behavior. Instead, they represent broadly understandable expressive signals that communicate the intended social meaning within the benchmark setting. 

3) Fine-grained MCQ Generation: Based on the human-verified annotations, we construct three task-specific sets of four-option multiple-choice questions (MCQs): LEU, SIU, and CAP. For each sample, the question and correct answer are derived directly from the corresponding task annota-tion. We use Qwen3-VL-8B [27] only to generate distrac-tor options that remain contextually relevant but contain identifiable semantic errors with respect to the ground-truth annotation. Such errors include, for example, reversing the expressed sentiment from positive to negative or replacing an inquiry with a clearly different communicative intention. All generated distractors are manually reviewed alongside their corresponding questions and correct answers. This review ensures that each MCQ is factually grounded in the episode and has a single, unambiguous correct answer. It also verifies that each distractor is sufficiently related to the episode context to be meaningful while remaining clearly incorrect because of a specific and identifiable discrepancy with the annotation. Distractors that lack an explicit error, introduce unsupported information, or create ambiguity are revised or removed. After manual verification, each MCQ is independently answered by four expert models drawn from the DeepSeek and Qwen model families [34]. We use the number of expert models that answer each question cor-rectly as an empirical indicator of its difficulty. Specifically, questions answered correctly by at least three models are categorized as Easy , those answered correctly by two models as Medium , and those answered correctly by at most one model as Hard . This procedure yields difficulty-stratified subsets for the final evaluation suite.

A. Comparison across Models 

Table II shows substantial variation across tasks and difficulty levels. Qwen3.8-Max and Claude-Sonnet-5 obtain the same overall accuracy of 77.54%, but their task-specific results differ. Qwen3.8-Max performs better on LEU and SIU, reaching 94.38% and 81.05%, respectively, whereas Claude-Sonnet-5 obtains higher CAP accuracy at 71.55%. Among the remaining models, Claude-Sonnet-5 records the highest accuracy on the easy and hard subsets, at 92.59% and 46.51%, while GPT-5.6-Luna obtains the highest medium-subset accuracy of 81.76%. These results show that aggregate accuracy alone does not fully characterize a model’s task-specific capabilities or its robustness to question difficulty. Performance also varies with each model’s temporal in-put capacity and training domain. EgoVLM-360M pro-cesses only one frame per inference and therefore can-not aggregate information over long video contexts, which likely contributes to its weak LEU performance. In con-trast, egocentric-domain models perform strongly on SIU: EgoThinker-v1 achieves 63.16%, outperforming Qwen3-VL-4B by 9.48 percentage points. This result is consistent with a benefit from egocentric adaptation for recognizing short-range speaker behaviors and intentions. However, the same advantage is less evident on CAP, which requires joint visual understanding, utterance comprehension, and action-sequence generation. This pattern suggests that domain adap-tation does not consistently compensate for limited general visual-language reasoning. Among the remaining models, most exhibit a marked performance decline from the easy subset to the medium and hard subsets. Closed-source models generally retain higher accuracy on the hard subset: Claude-Sonnet-5 achieves 46.51%, followed by GPT-5.6-Luna at 40.70% and Gemini-Flash-3.8 at 38.37%. Among open-weight models, Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct and EgoThinker-v1 achieve 37.21% and 36.05%, respectively. These results indicate that aggregate accuracy can mask substantial differences in model robust-ness as difficulty increases. B. Ablation Study

We conduct two ablation studies to examine the effects of reasoning mode and visual input. First, we compare the Instruct and Thinking variants of Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B under identical input settings. As shown in Table III, Thinking improves the overall accuracy of Qwen3-VL-2B from 39.48% to 59.10%, an increase of 19.62 percentage points. The largest improvement occurs on SIU, where accuracy increases by 32.63 percentage points, while CAP improves by 15.90 percentage points. A similar trend is observed for Qwen3-VL-4B: its overall accuracy increases from 56.03% to 73.05%, with the largest task-specific in-crease of 20.50 percentage points on CAP. These consistent improvements across model scales and tasks demonstrate that extended reasoning benefits long-context understanding, speaker-intention inference, and embodied action planning. These gains, however, introduce substantial computa-tional overhead. For Qwen3-VL-2B, inference throughput decreases from 1.60 to 0.030 samples/s, making the Thinking variant approximately 53 .6× slower. For Qwen3-VL-4B, throughput decreases from 1.51 to 0.075 samples/s, corre-sponding to a slowdown of approximately 20 .2×. Although long-form reasoning substantially improves accuracy, its re-duced inference efficiency limits its applicability to online social agents that require timely responses. The Instruct variants therefore offer a more practical trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. We further investigate the contribution of visual observa-tions to CAP by comparing the text-only and vision–text settings across three VLMs. As shown in Table IV, adding sampled frames improves Qwen3-VL-2B by 1.26 percentage points and EgoMind-7B by 8.79 percentage points, but reduces InternVL2.5-2B performance by 4.18 percentage points. The larger increase achieved by EgoMind-7B indi-cates that this model benefits more from visual input in the evaluated setting. Nevertheless, the inconsistent effects across models show that adding visual observations does not consistently improve CAP. These results indicate that the usefulness of visual context depends on the model’s ability to integrate multi-frame visual and linguistic information. 

C. SocialCoAct-Agent Deployment 

We deployed SocialCoAct-Agent on two RTX 5090 GPUs. Specifically, the agent integrates Whisper-Tiny for ASR, Qwen3-VL-8B for response and action planning, Qwen3-TTS for speech synthesis, and MuJoCo or a Unitree G1 for action execution. We evaluated each setting over ten runs using a 3.66 s speech input and visual frames. All trials successfully produced both a verbal response and an executable gesture. The median end-to-end latency was 12.63 s in simulation. The measured processing latency in the robot-deployment configuration was 7.57 s, excluding camera transmission, robot communication, and physical execution. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce SocialCoAct, an agent-centered framework for egocentric social co-action with VLM-based humanoid robots. We have formulated egocentric social co-action as an end-to-end task and developed SocialCoAct-Agent, which transforms egocentric observations and di-alogue history into verbal responses and atomic actions aligned with corresponding utterance spans. The resulting ac-tions are executable in MuJoCo and on a physical humanoid robot. To evaluate the VLM capabilities required by this task, we have constructed SocialCoAct-Bench, an egocentric mul-timodal benchmark comprising video and timestamp-aligned transcripts from complex, multi-participant, and multi-scene interactions. Its three tasks, LEU, SIU, and CAP, assess long-context understanding, speaker-intention recognition, and coordinated action planning, respectively. In particular, CAP represents embodied responses as human-interpretable atomic actions explicitly aligned with given utterance spans. Our experiments show that performance varies across model scales, temporal input capacities, and training do-mains, while CAP and hard social-interaction questions remain particularly challenging. Reasoning ablations demon-strate substantial trade-offs between accuracy and efficiency, while modality ablations show that the benefits of visual context vary across models. By connecting multimodal social understanding with interpretable, utterance-aligned action planning and execution, SocialCoAct provides a unified foundation for evaluating and developing socially responsive VLM-based humanoid agents.
