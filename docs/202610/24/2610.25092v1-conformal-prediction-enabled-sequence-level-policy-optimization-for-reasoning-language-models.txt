## ABSTRACT 

Reinforcement learning improves the reasoning capability of large language mod-els through verifiable rewards for mathematical and coding tasks. In token-level methods such as GRPO, clipping acts on individual token ratios while the re-ward and advantage apply to a complete response. Recent sequence-level meth-ods align these units but still use fixed clipping bands, whose tail probabilities change as the score distribution evolves. We propose Conformal Prediction-Enabled Sequence-level Policy Optimization (CSPO), which adaptively calibrates sequence-level clipping bounds using recent training statistics. The resulting band targets a specified marginal tail budget and adapts to policy drift; calibration con-ditional on response length requires additional assumptions. We bound the prob-ability that a fresh score falls outside the band by the target miscoverage level plus explicit CDF discrepancy and drift terms. Under additional moment condi-tions, this bound also controls the objective and gradient error relative to the un-clipped GSPO power surrogate. Experiments with the Qwen3-1.7B series show improvements over GSPO on difficult mathematical reasoning benchmarks and ALFWorld, with clipping endpoints that adapt during training. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning from feedback has become a key component of large language model post-training. Early reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) pipelines optimize policies against learned reward models derived from human preferences, enabling instruction-following and preference alignment beyond supervised fine-tuning (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). More recently, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as an especially effective paradigm for reasoning tasks, where correctness can often be automatically checked by final-answer matching, symbolic verification, unit tests, or external graders. Such rewards have enabled large-scale reasoning-oriented post-training, as exem-plified by recent reasoning models and open RL systems (Guo et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). In this setting, the reward is naturally defined at the sequence level : a complete solu-tion is judged by whether the final answer and, in some cases, the reasoning trajectory satisfy the verification criterion. Most RL post-training methods build on trust-region policy optimization. TRPO constrains the policy update through a KL trust region, while PPO replaces the hard constraint with a clipped sur-rogate objective that is simpler and more scalable (Schulman et al., 2015; 2017). Clipping limits the incentive to move probability ratios away from one, reducing the risk of overfitting sampled trajec-tories during repeated optimizer updates. For language models, GRPO simplifies PPO by removing the learned value function and estimating advantages through group-relative rewards, which sub-stantially reduces memory and implementation complexity for long-context reasoning (Shao et al., 2024). However, GRPO inherits a token-level clipping design: each token ratio is clipped separately,

This creates a mismatch between the token-level control variable and the sequence-level reward signal. A natural response to this mismatch is to optimize at the sequence level. GSPO constructs a response-level power multiplier from the likelihood ratio of the whole generated sequence and per-forms sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization (Zheng et al., 2025). This aligns the unit of policy correction with the unit of reward and avoids treating individual tokens as independently rewarded decisions. Yet sequence-level clipping introduces a new challenge: the sequence log-ratio is a sum over tokens, so its scale depends strongly on the response length. FSPO identifies this length fairness issue and clips the sequence log-importance ratio with a band that scales as √L, where L

is the output length (Mao et al., 2025). This is an important step toward fair sequence-level RL, but the clipping width remains determined by fixed hyperparameters. In long-chain-of-thought RL, the distribution of log-ratios can vary substantially across training stages as the policy improves, the entropy changes, and the model discovers new reasoning strategies. A fixed clipping band may be too conservative during exploration, suppressing rare but valuable responses, and too loose during unstable phases, allowing harmful updates. We therefore calibrate the clipping band from recent policy shifts to target a specified marginal tail probability. This lets the numerical width change with the score distribution. Conformal prediction motivates this approach: empirical quantiles define intervals with controlled miscoverage under exchangeability, and adaptive conformal methods address calibration under distribution shift (Vovk et al., 2005; Shafer & Vovk, 2008; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023; Gibbs & Cand` es, 2021). We use quantile calibration to select the sequence-level clipping band. Because training reuses data and changes the policy, our bounds explicitly retain CDF discrepancies and local drift. They are marginal over the response-length distribution; normalization by L alone does not imply calibration conditional on length. We propose Conformal prediction-enabled Sequence-level Policy Optimization ( CSPO ). Before each training step, CSPO uses a rolling buffer of retained training scores W = exp( S/L ), where 

S = log[ πθ (y | x)/π θold (y | x)] is the sequence-level log-ratio and L = len( y). Given a target miscoverage level α, CSPO estimates lower and upper empirical quantiles (qlo , q hi ) from completed blocks of retained scores, anchors the resulting interval at W = 1 , and holds it fixed throughout the optimizer updates for that rollout batch. Scores computed on the training batch are retained for subsequent steps. The resulting sequence-level surrogate targets a marginal tail budget for fresh scores. Our contributions are threefold: 1. We identify the need for adaptive sequence-level clipping in RL post-training for reasoning language models, complementing prior work on sequence-level optimization and length-fair clipping. 2. We propose CSPO, which clips GSPO power scores using rolling empirical quantiles of retained training scores to target a specified marginal tail budget. 3. We bound tail probabilities for the mixture of scores within each training step in terms of α,CDF discrepancies, and local drift. Under additional moment conditions, these bounds con-trol the objective and gradient error relative to the unclipped GSPO power surrogate. Em-pirically, CSPO improves Pass@1 over GSPO on difficult mathematical reasoning bench-marks and achieves the highest overall success rate on ALFWorld among the evaluated methods.

We evaluate CSPO on two settings: multi-step embodied decision making using ALFWorld (Shrid-har et al., 2021), and difficult mathematical reasoning using DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025). ALFWorld is a text-based embodied benchmark in which the agent completes household goals through multi-step interaction. ALFWorld consists of six goal-conditioned task types: Pick & Place (Pick), Examine in Light (Look), Clean & Place (Clean), Heat & Place (Heat), Cool & Place (Cool), and Pick Two & Place (Pick2). For mathematical reasoning, we train on DAPO-Math-17k and evalu-ate on AIME (MAA, 2024; 2025), AMC (MAA, 2023), MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). Both settings provide verifi-able rewards, obtained directly from the environment for ALFWorld and from answer checking for mathematical reasoning. We use Qwen3-1.7B for ALFWorld and Qwen3-1.7B-Base for mathematical reasoning (Yang et al., 2025). We compare CSPO with several policy optimization baselines: GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025). We generate G = 8 responses per prompt for mathematical reasoning and G = 8 trajectories per task for ALFWorld. Detailed training settings are provided in Appendix C.2. 4.1 MAIN RESULTS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

CSPO adapts sequence-level clipping bounds using quantiles of recent training scores. Its tail bounds account for data reuse and policy drift, and its per-update bounds control the objective and gradient error under the stated moment conditions. In our experiments, CSPO improves over GSPO on mathematical reasoning benchmarks and achieves the highest overall success rate on ALFWorld. The changing endpoints provide a direct view of how the clipping band adapts during training. 

Limitations. The population tail bounds depend on discrepancies between the CDFs of retained scores and their population mixtures, as well as local CDF drift. Reusing a rollout batch does not yield a sampling rate for these discrepancies from the number of retained entries alone. The gradient bound also assumes a bound on the normalized score gradient that need not hold for a neural network. The results quantify error relative to the GSPO power surrogate; they do not establish a KL constraint or reward improvement. 9REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

Group sequence policy op-timization. arXiv preprint arXiv:2507.18071 , 2025. 12 A RELATED WORK 

Related work relevant to CSPO spans three complementary directions: reinforcement learning for language-model post-training, the transition from token-level to sequence-level policy optimization, and conformal prediction and adaptive calibration. We review these strands in turn to position CSPO as a sequence-level policy optimization method that replaces a prespecified clipping band with one calibrated from retained training scores. 

Reinforcement learning for language model post-training. RL-based post-training has a long history in aligning models with human or task-specific preferences. Early preference-based RL showed that complex objectives can be learned from pairwise human comparisons (Christiano et al., 2017). In language modeling, RLHF pipelines train reward models from human preference data and optimize policies to maximize the learned reward while constraining deviation from a reference model (Sti-ennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022). Constitutional AI reduces direct human labeling by using principle-guided AI feedback for harmlessness training (Bai et al., 2022). In parallel, direct pref-erence optimization methods bypass explicit reward-model RL by deriving a supervised objective from the KL-regularized RLHF optimum (Rafailov et al., 2023). These methods focus primarily on preference alignment, while our work targets policy optimization for verifiable reasoning rewards. RLVR has recently become a central approach for improving mathematical and coding reasoning. When rewards are automatically verifiable, the supervision signal can be scaled without dense hu-man annotations. DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO to improve mathematical reasoning while re-ducing PPO memory overhead (Shao et al., 2024). DeepSeek-R1 further demonstrated that rea-soning behaviors such as reflection and verification can emerge through large-scale RL (Guo et al., 2025). DAPO highlights several practical ingredients for large-scale long-CoT RL, including de-coupled clipping and dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025). Our method is complementary to these systems: CSPO modifies the clipping geometry of the policy objective and can be combined with different reward functions, sampling strategies, and KL regularizers. 

From token-level to sequence-level policy optimization. PPO stabilizes policy-gradient updates by clipping the probability ratio at the action level (Schulman et al., 2017). In autoregressive language models, applying this design token-by-token is convenient, but the reward and advantage are often defined for an entire generated response. GRPO keeps this token-level clipping structure while replacing the value model with group-relative sequence rewards (Shao et al., 2024). This design is efficient, but it implicitly assigns a single response-level advantage to many token-level ratios. As a result, the objective may over-penalize locally off-policy tokens or distort the contribution of a high-value response. Sequence-level methods address this mismatch by moving the clipping multiplier to the response level. GSPO constructs a power multiplier from the sequence likelihood ratio and performs sequence-level clipping and optimization (Zheng et al., 2025). FSPO further studies the length fair-ness problem of sequence-level clipping and shows that fixed clipping bands can reweight short and long responses differently; it introduces a √L-scaled clipping band for the sequence log-ratio (Mao et al., 2025). CSPO extends this approach by calibrating the clipping band from the recent empirical distribution of normalized sequence scores, allowing the endpoints to change during training. 

Conformal prediction and adaptive calibration. Conformal prediction provides distribution-free uncertainty quantification by calibrating prediction sets using held-out nonconformity scores (Vovk et al., 2005; Shafer & Vovk, 2008; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). Its central guarantee is finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability, independent of the internal form of the predictive model. Conformalized quantile regression combines conformal calibration with quantile regression to obtain adaptive prediction intervals under heteroscedasticity (Romano et al., 2019). Adaptive conformal inference extends calibration to online environments where the data distribution may vary over time (Gibbs & Cand` es, 2021; 2024). We use quantile calibration to assign a tail budget to fresh sequence-level scores outside the clipping band. The GSPO power score W = exp( S/L ) plays the role of the calibration score: values in the tails indicate unusually large policy shifts. By maintaining a rolling training-score buffer, CSPO adapts the clipping interval to the current training regime while targeting a marginal tail budget with explicit CDF discrepancies and local drift. 13 B THEORETICAL DETAILS

approaches zero, the clipping interval becomes increasingly loose and CSPO gradually approaches an effectively unclipped policy update, which departs from the trust-region intuition underlying TRPO and PPO and may increase the risk of unstable or collapsed training. Figure 5 shows the rewards, clip fractions, and endpoints for these choices of α. The clip fraction is an empirical training diagnostic; interpreting it as a population tail probability requires accounting for data reuse and distinguishing band exceedance from saturation, which also depends on the advantage sign.
