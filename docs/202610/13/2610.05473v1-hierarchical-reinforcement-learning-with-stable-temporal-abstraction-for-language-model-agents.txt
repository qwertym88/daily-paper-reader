## Abstract 

Hierarchical reinforcement learning improves long-horizon control by organiz-ing primitive actions around persistent subgoals and assigning credit at multiple temporal scales. Recent hierarchical language agents bring these benefits to in-teractive tasks by explicitly separating subgoal planning from action execution. We observe, however, that an explicit hierarchy does not by itself determine how stable the resulting temporal abstraction is: the learned boundary policy may re-place the subgoal almost every turn, making it effectively transient, or retain a subgoal after it has stopped being appropriate. We call this temporal abstraction instability . We propose Stable Temporal Abstraction via Constrained Optimiza-tion (STAC), a constrained boundary-policy optimization method that represents premature replanning and stale persistence as constraint costs. STAC applies the resulting Lagrangian costs only to the sampled boundary decision, leaving the underlying algorithm’s rewards, critic targets, subgoal advantages, and primitive-action advantages unchanged. Across two backbones and two benchmarks, STAC improves success over a strong hierarchical baseline by 8.1 and 7.9 points on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen3-0.6B, and by 23 .5 and 15 .8 points with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. 

## 1 Introduction 

Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) has long provided a principled approach to long-horizon decision making [22, 4, 14, 12, 25, 7, 11, 2]. Rather than selecting every primitive action at a single temporal scale, HRL introduces temporally extended subgoals or options and decomposes their realization into lower-level actions [1]. This decomposition can shorten effective planning horizons, reuse behavior across states, preserve intermediate intent, and separate credit for high-level planning from credit for low-level execution. Recent work has begun transferring these benefits to language-model agents [27, 9, 26, 10, 3, 16, 17]. For example, HiPER [17] represents each interaction turn through an explicit Plan–Execute hierarchy: a high-level component produces a textual subgoal, a boundary decision determines whether that subgoal should persist or be replaced, and a low-level component selects an environment action. Its hierarchical advantage estimator further assigns distinct learning signals to subgoal, boundary, and

action tokens. Such methods demonstrate that explicit hierarchical structure and multi-timescale credit assignment can improve long-horizon language-agent performance. We observe, however, that the architecture alone does not determine how stable the learned temporal abstraction will be. Useful temporal abstraction requires a subgoal to persist long enough to coordinate multiple actions, but also to terminate once it is completed or no longer appropriate. The learned boundary policy can drift away from either requirement. It may replace the subgoal almost every turn, reducing the subgoal toward a transient one-step label and moving the hierarchy toward a flat policy. Alternatively, it may retain the same subgoal too long, delaying state-dependent replanning after that subgoal stops being appropriate. We call this tendency temporal abstraction instability . It matters most in multi-phase tasks, where success requires both persistence within a phase and a well-timed transition between phases. To address this, we propose Stable Temporal Abstraction via Constrained Optimization (STAC), a constrained boundary-policy optimization method. STAC represents premature replanning and stale-subgoal persistence as constraint costs and optimizes their Lagrangian relaxation. It consumes the normalized boundary advantage supplied by the hierarchical optimizer it is applied to, which we call the host , and modifies only the coefficient of the sampled boundary decision. Environment rewards, critic targets, subgoal advantages, and primitive-action advantages remain unchanged. This decision locality distinguishes STAC from reward shaping and makes it independent of the host’s particular actor architecture or return estimator. We evaluate STAC using the publicly released HiPER Plan–Execute architecture [17] as a strong hierarchical host, whose boundary policy replans early on more than half of all turns. STAC instead maintains multi-turn subgoals and nearly perfect structured-control validity. The improved hierarchy is accompanied by an 8.1-point ALFWorld gain over the released host recipe on Qwen3-0.6B, rising to 23 .5 points on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. Our contributions are: 

• We identify and characterize temporal abstraction instability : a hierarchy can be architecturally explicit yet still learn subgoals that are too transient or too persistent for useful temporal structure. 

• We formulate stable temporal abstraction as a constrained boundary-policy problem and derive a decision-local Lagrangian optimization rule with separate costs for premature replanning and stale-subgoal persistence; our reported experiments optimize its fixed-multiplier relaxation, and we report the violation rates it induces. 

• We show that constraining the boundary yields longer, valid multi-turn subgoals relative to a released hierarchical host, and that this accompanies improved ALFWorld and WebShop performance on two model backbones.

Subgoals and temporal segments. A subgoal zt is a textual intermediate objective whose comple-tion normally requires several primitive actions. For a tool-using language agent, this means several tool calls with concrete arguments. Behavioral criterion and instability. Let dt ≥ 1 count the subgoal-creating turn and all subsequent turns since the most recent valid switch. Useful temporal abstraction requires state-dependent, non-degenerate duration: the subgoal should span multiple actions when coordination is needed and terminate when it becomes stale. As πb moves toward always switching, dt approaches 1, segment-level returns approach per-turn returns, and the subgoal approaches a transient scratchpad. As it moves toward always keeping, the agent replans less often than the task requires. ## 4 Experiments 

Environments and model. We evaluate on two text-based interactive benchmarks with sparse success rewards. ALFWorld [21] contains long-horizon household tasks whose multi-phase cate-gories require several coordinated actions per objective, and WebShop [24] requires navigating a simulated e-commerce site to satisfy a natural-language shopping instruction. Both reward persistent subgoals while still requiring well-timed replanning. The actor and critic use Qwen3-0.6B [23] unless stated otherwise; to test whether the effect depends on a single pretraining lineage, we repeat both benchmarks with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct [6] under identical hyperparameters and seeds. We instantiate STAC on the public HiPER Plan–Execute implementation [17], whose structured actor emits a boundary decision, textual subgoal, and admissible action, and whose estimator supplies Ab;STAC leaves the subgoal/action streams and all critic targets unchanged (Appendix B). 

Training. Each run uses 128 independent environments per update, 150 updates, at most 50 environment turns, training temperature 1.0, and validation temperature 0.4; actor and critic learning rates are 10 −6 and 10 −5 and the KL coefficient is 0.01 . We evaluate 128 held-out episodes every five updates, and the base checkpoint before training. Appendix A gives the full configuration and checkpoint protocols. 

Benchmarks. We compare against two flat baselines that optimize a single-timescale policy: GRPO [20], which normalizes trajectory-level rewards within a sampled group, and GiGPO [5], which adds step-level relative advantages for denser credit. Our hierarchical host is HiPER [17], whose Plan–Execute actor and hierarchical advantage estimator provide the boundary advantage that STAC modifies.

Scope and remaining limitations. For the matched Qwen3 and Llama local HiPER and STAC arms, we set HiPER’s keep_penalty and keep_consistency_penalty to zero and retain its environment reward and −0.1 malformed-output term. Thus the comparison does not establish whether a separately tuned host KEEP penalty or a hard first-turn S WITCH block could recover the same gains. Nor do we report a dmin /dmax sweep: the selected duration values are pre-specified heuristics, not uniquely justified optima. The short cost captures most standalone success gain at three seeds, while the stale cost selectively reduces stale persistence; its incremental task-success benefit remains unresolved. Broader hosts, more seeds, and these alternative controls are future work. 

## 7 Conclusion 

Explicit subgoal syntax alone does not determine how stable the learned temporal abstraction will be. We identified temporal abstraction instability and proposed STAC, which prices premature replanning and stale persistence as constraint costs on the sampled boundary decision alone, leaving rewards, critic targets, and the subgoal and action advantages untouched. Over the released hierarchical host it improves best-checkpoint success by 8.1 and 7.9 points with Qwen3-0.6B and 23 .5 and 15 .8 points with Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, seven of these eight comparisons significant at pW < 0.05 . 9A Implementation, Experimental Details, and Broader Impact 

Standalone integration. STAC requires a binary boundary decision, a subgoal-age counter, the base boundary advantage, and estimates of the two constraint rates. It can therefore be inserted after advantage normalization in any on-policy hierarchical optimizer. The host policy need not use the subgoal text, value heads, or return estimator used in our experiments. Our experimental structured-output adapter additionally supplies the parsed boundary-token mask described in Appendix I. An implementation should expose the constraint-cost functions gi and the multipliers μi, with the budgets 

ρi and a dual optimizer needed only for the adaptive variant; setting both multipliers to zero is the behavior-preserving default for constrained boundary credit. 

Artifacts and statistical protocol. Our experiment configuration fixes all non-treatment settings across arms and stores the complete resolved trainer configuration, per-update metrics, validation generations, and sampled training episodes. We recommend reporting switch rate, segment-length distribution, both constraint violation rates, and valid-format rate alongside task return. Violation rates and segment statistics are computed only over turns with a parsed valid boundary-token mask; malformed or missing boundary blocks are excluded from those denominators and reported separately through the valid-format rate. For the main result, we follow HiPER and select each seed’s highest-overall-validation checkpoint, then report every category from that checkpoint. Appendix H separately reports step-150 and late-window summaries to expose checkpoint-selection sensitivity. Figure 4 reports the arithmetic mean across the three seed-level validation trajectories without uncertainty intervals. 

Training configuration. The released ALFWorld host enables the truncation bootstrap in Eq. (14); the WebShop host disables it. ALFWorld uses a 2048-token prompt limit; WebShop uses 4096 tokens because its released observation template is longer. The matched Qwen3 and Llama local HiPER and STAC runs set HiPER’s keep_penalty and keep_consistency_penalty to zero, while retaining the environment reward and the host’s −0.1 malformed-output reward term. We use all the HiPER baseline numbers in our experiments. The base-model row evaluates the untrained checkpoint under the same Plan–Execute prompt, temperature, episode limit, and splits.

A.1 Heuristic choice of the two duration thresholds 

Neither threshold in Eq. (12) is selected by validation. They are pre-specified duration heuristics, not guarantees about the appropriate duration of every individual subgoal. 

The lower threshold discourages uniform one-turn collapse. The cost gshort = I[dt < 2] is active exactly when dt = 1 . A subgoal that terminates after a single turn in every state induces the same action distribution as a policy with no subgoal layer at all: the option reduces to a primitive action and the hierarchy is vacuous [22]. We therefore use dmin = 2 to discourage this uniform collapse. It does not imply that every one-turn segment is undesirable, and we do not claim that 2 is the only admissible threshold.

Compute. Every run trains on a single node of eight NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs, using vLLM for rollout generation and co-locating actor and critic on the same node. Broader impact. Temporal abstraction can make language agents more efficient and interpretable by exposing the subgoal that persists across actions. However, the chosen constraint-cost functions encode a behavioral prior. Costs that are too restrictive can suppress legitimate rapid replanning or force stale subgoals, especially under distribution shift. For deployment, boundary credit should be monitored, capped, and disabled when the structured interface is not semantically validated. It does not replace task-level safety constraints.

Locality. Potential-based reward shaping [15] replaces the trajectory reward rt with r′

STAC instead adds the sampled Lagrangian cost terms after host advantage estimation, only to the boundary span at turn t. Thus it changes neither the reward stream, critic targets, nor another turn’s advantage. Moreover, the mutually exclusive boundary decisions and bounded costs imply that the valid-decision perturbation is bounded by max( μshort , μ stale ).

One update. Each update (1) rolls out the hierarchical policy, (2) tracks dt and computes Eq. (6), (3) computes and normalizes the host advantage streams, (4) forms Eq. (11), and (5) applies the unchanged on-policy actor and critic updates. Step (6) applies only to the adaptive primal–dual variant, which additionally updates the multipliers using Eq. ## D Classical Termination Regularization 

Classical hierarchical reinforcement learning represents the keep/switch decision as a termination function. Option-Critic learns termination policies end to end [1], while deliberation costs discourage 12 frequent termination [8]. A one-sided termination cost can favor indefinitely long options, and both formulations assume a closed, indexed decision space. STAC instead constrains both premature replanning and stale persistence at the boundary-policy level. It is compatible with GAE [19], PPO [18], and other on-policy hierarchical objectives. 

## E Related Hierarchical Language-Agent Methods 

Recent hierarchical language-agent methods differ in what constitutes a level of the hierarchy, how those levels are trained, and where credit is assigned. This section summarizes the methods cited in the introduction from their primary papers and official implementations. 

ArCHer. ArCHer is a two-level actor–critic framework for multi-turn language-model interac-tion [27]. Its high level applies off-policy temporal-difference learning to utterance- or turn-level transitions, thereby propagating reward across an entire dialogue, while its low level applies token-level policy optimization within each utterance using the high-level value function for cross-turn credit. This separation allows the high and low levels to run RL algorithms with different update rules and substantially improves sample efficiency relative to fully on-policy multi-turn optimization. 

GLIDER. GLIDER uses a two-level planner–controller hierarchy for grounding language models in sequential environments [9]. The high-level policy generates abstract chain-of-thought plans or subtasks, while a parameter-efficient low-level controller executes environment actions conditioned on the current plan. Training proceeds from behavior-cloning initialization to offline hierarchical RL with an advantage-weighted actor–critic objective, followed by offline-to-online adaptation; the paper evaluates transfer and generalization on ScienceWorld and ALFWorld. 

STEP-HRL. STEP-HRL is the method name used in the paper formally titled Hierarchical Rein-forcement Learning with Augmented Step-Level Transitions for LLM Agents [26]. It introduces three functional components: a high-level policy that predicts the next subtask from the instruction and completed-subtask history, a low-level policy that acts from the current observation, subgoal, and compact local progress, and a local-progress module that iteratively folds intra-subtask interactions into that compact representation. The components share policy parameters but use distinct critics; training combines behavior-cloning initialization with offline RL. The compact progress state reduces dependence on full interaction histories and is evaluated on ScienceWorld and ALFWorld. 

HiMAC. HiMAC decomposes control into a shared-parameter Macro-Policy and Micro-Policy [10]. The Macro-Policy generates a structured blueprint of natural-language subgoals, and the Micro-Policy generates atomic actions conditioned on each subgoal. Its critic-free hierarchical optimization extends group-relative policy optimization to produce separate blueprint-level and execution-level relative advantages. An alternating co-evolution schedule updates macro exploration with the executor fixed, then adapts the executor under a fixed high-confidence blueprint; experiments cover ALFWorld, WebShop, and Sokoban. 

HIPIF. HIPIF combines hierarchical planning with information folding for long-horizon interac-tion [3]. At each step, a reflection operation decides whether the active subgoal is complete. When complete, the method folds that subgoal’s interaction history into a compact summary and generates the next subgoal; otherwise, it generates another primitive action under the current subgoal. Training uses a GRPO-style clipped objective whose step-level advantages combine terminal environment reward with subgoal-oriented process rewards, avoiding an ever-growing raw interaction context. 

Multi 2. Multi 2 assigns the two hierarchy levels to separate role-specialized agents [16]. Its high-level System 1 agent generates context-aware subgoals and is trained by supervised fine-tuning on a subgoal dataset. Its low-level System 2 agent executes atomic actions, first learning from offline data with actor–critic RL and then receiving online RL fine-tuning while System 1 remains fixed. The method targets objective drift in long-horizon interaction and introduces hierarchical datasets spanning multiple interactive environments.

Relation to STAC. The hierarchical methods above demonstrate several benefits of hierarchical language-agent training, including cross-turn credit assignment, transferable low-level skills, compact progress representations, macro–micro optimization, folded histories, and role-specialized planning and execution. STAC addresses an orthogonal issue: regardless of how a host defines or trains its hierarchy, the learned boundary can still switch too frequently, persist after becoming stale, or leave the parsed optimization support. It therefore consumes a host-provided boundary advantage and modifies only boundary credit rather than prescribing a new planner, executor, trajectory representation, or base return estimator.

## I Boundary-Mask Representation 

One turn. Let the response generated at environment turn t contain Lt tokens, yt =(yt, 1, . . . , y t,L t ). The parser locates the structured block that expresses the boundary decision, and M bt,j = 1 exactly for token positions belonging to that block; every other position receives zero. For example, suppose a tokenized response is schematically 

⟨switch ⟩ KEEP ⟨/switch ⟩

| {z }

> boundary block

⟨subgoal ⟩ · · · 

| {z }

> subgoal block

⟨action ⟩ · · · 

| {z }

> action block

.

If the boundary block occupies the first three token positions, its mask is 

M bt = [1 , 1, 1, 0, 0, . . . , 0] .

The exact number of ones depends on tokenization and on whether delimiter tokens are included in the configured mask. If the boundary block is missing or malformed, the parser selects no boundary tokens and 

M bt = [0 , 0, . . . , 0] , ∥M bt ∥0 = 0 ,

so that turn contributes no boundary-level term. 

A complete task trace. The mask is reconstructed from the response at every environment turn, not only when the agent creates a new subgoal. Thus a T -turn task produces M b

, . . . , M bT . After padding each response to a common width L, the masks form
