Attention Residuals (AttnRes) enables content-dependent retrieval over network depth, which can be viewed as single-stream softmax attention along the depth axis. Inspired by the success of multi-head attention along the sequence axis, we introduce Multi-Stream Attention Residuals (MSAR) , an extension of AttnRes that generalizes this mechanism to multi-head depth attention, yielding substantial performance gains with minimal additional overhead. MSAR draws stream diversity from sequence context through multi-scale causal convolutions, allowing different heads to retrieve historical representations through distinct contextual views while preserving the integrity of the hidden representation. The retrieved streams are combined through lightweight adaptive mixing to generate the single-stream layer input. By retaining single-stream sources and expanding them on demand, MSAR supports multi-stream computation without requiring persistent multi-stream storage or communication. Across all 12 downstream benchmarks at the 18B and 28B MoE scales, MSAR consistently outperforms prior residual variants, including standard residual connections, manifold-constrained Hyper-Connections (mHC), and AttnRes. Scaling-law experiments yield an iso-loss compute speedup of 1.58 × over the standard residual baseline, compared with 1.35 × for AttnRes and 1.30 × for mHC. These results make MSAR a promising residual architecture for large-scale pre-training. 

## 1. Introduction 

Scaling depth has been a primary lever for improving Transformer-based large language mod-els (LLMs) [22, 34, 41]. In modern PreNorm architectures [40], however, adding layers does not always yield the expected gains [13, 24]. The residual stream [14] aggregates preceding computations through a single hidden-state trajectory with fixed additive updates; as the stack grows deeper, features formed in early layers can be progressively diluted and become difficult for later layers to retrieve. This depth-wise information dilution limits the effective use of additional layers, making depth scaling less efficient than its representational capacity would suggest [6, 32, 35]. Motivated by this, recent work has begun to challenge the standard residual connection design itself. Representatively, Hyper-Connections (HC), together with its manifold-constrained variant mHC, expands the single residual stream into multiple parallel streams with learned stream mixing [39, 50], while Attention Residuals (AttnRes) replaces fixed residual accumulation with learned depth-wise softmax retrieval over earlier representations [35]. Inspired by the success of multi-head attention along the sequence axis [36], a natural question emerges: can we realize multi-head depth attention , bringing greater expressiveness to residual retrieval over depth? However, previous attempts indicate that achieving this goal is non-trivial. AttnRes already tried channel-splitting the hidden state to form multi-head depth aggregation, which instead degraded performance [35]: in depth-wise attention the retrieved value is essentially the hidden state itself and should be treated as a coherent whole, so slicing it into low-dimensional fragments breaks this integrity. Another intuitive route is to derive several candidate streams from a single layer output using the H post -style write-back of (m)HC, and then treat them as depth-attention heads. This keeps each candidate full-dimensional, but the candidates are effectively scalar reweightings of the same vector; normalization largely removes these magnitude differences, yielding nearly identical keys and limiting the diversity of source representations across heads. Taken together, these observations point to a central design challenge: how to construct diverse depth-attention heads from a single layer output without compromising its integrity. We therefore propose Multi-Stream Attention Residuals ( MSAR ), a low-cost extension of AttnRes that couples contextual stream expansion with depth-wise retrieval to address this challenge. Starting from a single residual source, MSAR draws diversity from the sequence axis: it applies a small set of multi-scale causal depthwise convolutions to the layer output, producing 

𝑚 full-dimensional streams whose receptive fields encode different local-context scales. These streams serve as distinct source views for depth attention, so each stream can perform its own softmax retrieval over depth instead of sharing one depth distribution. The 𝑚 retrieved states are then fused by a lightweight H pre -style mixer into the input of the target layer. In this way, MSAR turns residual retrieval into a multi-head depth-attention primitive: different heads can select different depth histories through distinct sequence-context views while preserving the integrity of the hidden representation. At the system level, MSAR is fully compatible with AttnRes block mode, cache-based pipeline communication, and the two-phase computation strategy. In our 28B MoE setting, MSAR increases training FLOPs by only 0.5% and activated parameters by 0.3%. Notably, during the forward pass, all streams are deterministically generated from the main residual state through causal depthwise convolutions, allowing MSAR to keep only the main stream in memory and expand it on demand when computing depth attention. The convolution-expanded source streams are intermediate values used only by depth attention and are immediately consumed by 2the following attention computation; in practice, this allows stream expansion and consumption to be handled by a fused kernel, avoiding write-back of these 𝑚 intermediate streams. The same principle applies in block mode: each block accumulates layer outputs into a single-stream summary, and the 𝑚 streams are recomputed from these summaries when they are consumed by depth attention. With this design, for 𝑁 stored sources, MSAR reduces pipeline communication and prefill activation storage from 𝑂 (𝑚𝑁𝑑 ) to 𝑂 (𝑁𝑑 ), matching the storage cost of AttnRes. Compared with AttnRes, the remaining overhead mainly comes from the memory access needed to write the retrieved multi-stream outputs and aggregate these streams. Empirically, MSAR consistently improves both training loss and downstream performance in large-scale pre-training. As summarized in Figure 1, in our 18B MoE setting, MSAR achieves the lowest final training loss, reaching 1.750 compared with Vanilla (1.799), mHC (1.776), and AttnRes (1.769). This training-loss gain consistently translates into higher average downstream scores throughout training across representative benchmarks covering knowledge, reasoning, math, and code. Scaling-law experiments further confirm that MSAR reaches the same loss with substantially less compute: relative to the Vanilla baseline, MSAR achieves an iso-loss compute speedup of 1.58 ×, compared with 1.35 × for AttnRes and 1.30 × for mHC. These results suggest that enriching depth-wise residual retrieval with multi-stream sequence-context views is an effective route for improving depth scaling. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 

1). Multi-Stream Attention Residuals. We introduce MSAR, which generalizes AttnRes from single-stream residual retrieval to multi-head depth attention. MSAR uses low-cost multi-scale causal depthwise convolutions to construct multiple streams, performs per-stream depth attention, and aggregates the retrieved streams with lightweight scalar mixing. This yields stream diversity without breaking the full-dimensional coherence of the hidden representation. 

2). Efficient design. MSAR stores only single-stream summaries and expands multi-stream representations on demand, reducing pipeline communication and prefill activation storage from 𝑂 (𝑚𝑁𝑑 ) to 𝑂 (𝑁𝑑 ) and matching the storage cost of AttnRes. It remains fully compatible with AttnRes’s cross-stage caching and two-phase inference strategy, with only minimal training FLOPs and parameter overhead. 

3). Large-scale validation. We validate MSAR in large-scale pre-training, where it delivers significant and consistent downstream gains in both 18B and 28B MoE settings. Scaling-law experiments and ablation studies further verify the effectiveness of the design, showing that the gains come from the proposed inductive bias rather than from a model-size-specific artifact. 

## 2. Related Work 

Multi-State Residual Recurrence. One approach improves depth-wise information flow by maintaining multiple residual states [21, 23, 42, 46, 48]. Hyper-Connections (HC) [50] implement this through learned stream aggregation, output distribution, and inter-stream mixing. Its manifold-constrained variant, mHC [39], stabilizes multi-stream recurrence through doubly stochastic residual mixing, while its Single-Pass variant [8] removes the dependency on current-state read coefficients to enable single-pass fused execution. xHC [44] enriches the write-back signal and uses sparse stream operations to support larger residual expansion. Gated Residual [28] uses elementwise gated reads and input-dependent scalar writes over multiple residual streams, without an explicit inter-stream residual-mixing operator. 

Cross-Layer Access. Another line of work gives later layers explicit access to earlier representa-tions [10, 25, 27, 47, 49]. DenseNet [17] connects layers by concatenating preceding feature maps. 3DenseFormer [26] aggregates earlier hidden states using learned, input-independent depth weights. MUDDFormer [38] introduces input-dependent, multiway aggregation of historical layer representations. Mixture-of-Depths Attention (MoDA) [51] jointly attends to sequence key-value pairs and same-position key-value pairs from preceding layers. Attention Residuals (AttnRes) [35] replaces residual accumulation with softmax-weighted retrieval over earlier layer outputs, using block-level sources and specialized execution strategies to reduce storage and communication costs. Representative methods from both lines, mHC and AttnRes, have also been validated in trillion-parameter-scale pre-training through DeepSeek-V4-Pro [9] and Kimi K3 [19], respectively. 

Position of MSAR. MSAR instantiates the intersection of the two residual-design axes above. It preserves the explicit cross-layer access of AttnRes, while introducing a multi-stream retrieval structure inspired by the capacity expansion of HC-family methods. Rather than widening the persistent residual recurrence, MSAR widens the depth-attention primitive itself by exposing historical hidden states through multiple sequence-context views.

3.4. Efficiency Analysis Single-Stream Storage and Communication. Since the expanded streams are deterministic functions of the single-stream block summaries defined in Section 3.3, MSAR only needs to retain and communicate single-stream sources. These sources are expanded through lightweight causal convolutions only when depth attention is computed. To preserve this property in Block MSAR, we deliberately accumulate layer outputs before stream expansion, rather than expand each output and accumulate streams separately. For 𝑁 summaries, this reduces persistent source storage and the corresponding pipeline communication payload per token from 𝑂 (𝑚𝑁𝑑 ) to 

> 𝑂

(𝑁𝑑 ), matching Block AttnRes under the same pipeline schedule. The single-stream represen-tation also supports AttnRes’s cross-stage caching [35], allowing previously received summaries to be reused across virtual pipeline stages without retransmission. 

Two-Phase Computation. All target layers within a block access the same completed sources, and their learned pseudo-queries are available before the block executes. This enables a two-phase computation strategy for MSAR, analogous to AttnRes, with each stream processed independently. In the first phase, queries for all target layers in the block are batched against the completed sources to compute per-target, per-stream retrieval results and softmax normalization statistics. Batching allows the targets to share source reads, reducing repeated memory access across layers. In the second phase, each layer computes the contribution of its current partial and merges it with its precomputed result through online softmax. This recovers the attention in Eq. 17; the first layer of a block uses only the completed-source result. Stream fusion is applied after the merge, so the first phase retains 𝑚 retrieved streams per target, separately from the single-stream source storage. 

Fused Stream Expansion. On-demand expansion can introduce additional memory traffic if its outputs are written out and subsequently read by attention. The expanded streams are consumed immediately by the subsequent depth attention and have no other consumers in the forward pass. This allows causal convolutional expansion and per-stream attention to be combined in a fused kernel, avoiding writes of these intermediate source streams to memory. This complements two-phase computation: batching reduces repeated source reads across targets, while fusion removes intermediate writes and reads during stream expansion. The final retrieved multi-stream outputs are still written and subsequently read for H pre aggregation, contributing additional memory traffic relative to AttnRes. 4.3. Scaling Laws 

To quantify the compute efficiency of MSAR across model scales, we compare its training-loss scaling with Vanilla, mHC, and AttnRes. We evaluate four model scales, matching training-token budgets across methods at each scale. Our FLOPs accounting excludes the LM head and includes method-specific residual overhead. Training compute is normalized by the Vanilla training FLOPs at the largest evaluated model scale, using the same reference for all methods. We fit a shifted power law to the final training loss: 

L( 𝐶 ) = 𝐴𝐶 −𝛽 + 𝐸 , (18) where 𝐶 denotes normalized training compute, 𝐴 and 𝛽 are fitted separately for each method, and 𝐸 = 0.72 is the estimated irreducible loss, which represents the asymptotic loss floor as training compute increases. We estimate the parameters by linear regression in log space. Detailed model configurations are provided in Appendix B. Figure 3 shows that MSAR achieves the lowest training loss at all four scales, and its fitted curve remains below all three baselines over their shared compute range. At the largest model scale, MSAR reduces training loss by 2.7% relative to Vanilla, compared with reductions of 1.6% for mHC and 1.8% for AttnRes. Averaged across the four model scales, MSAR achieves an iso-loss compute speedup of 1.58 × relative to Vanilla, compared with 1.35 × for AttnRes and 1.30 × for mHC. These results support a consistent improvement in the loss–compute trade-off across the evaluated scales. 

4.4. Ablation Studies 

We evaluate the key design choices of MSAR on a 10B MoE model with 15 Transformer layers, comprising 30 residual sublayers. Table 2 reports Pile loss [11], with 𝑆 denoting the number of residual sublayers per block. Detailed model configurations are provided in Appendix A. 

Comparison with Prior Methods. Table 2, rows (1)–(5), compares MSAR with standard residual connections and prior residual architectures. mHC and AttnRes reduce loss from the Vanilla 10 baseline’s 1.898 to 1.871 and 1.867, respectively, while MSAR achieves a substantially lower loss of 1.847. We also evaluate a hybrid residual architecture, AttnRes + mHC, that simultaneously maintains AttnRes’s historical sources for cross-layer access and mHC’s multi-stream recurrent state, summing their readouts to form the layer input. This direct combination reaches 1.861, an additional reduction of only 0.006 over AttnRes. With the same 𝑚 = 4 and 𝑆 = 4, MSAR lowers loss by a further 0.014. These results suggest that MSAR’s advantage stems from its specific design, rather than simply adding the states produced by cross-layer access and multi-stream recurrence. 

Component Design. Rows (5)–(7) isolate the contributions of contextual source expansion and adaptive stream fusion. Removing the convolutions replicates each source into four identical streams while retaining four independently learned pseudo-queries per target layer, yielding a multi-query depth-attention variant over shared source representations. The independent queries can still produce distinct depth-attention distributions, allowing this ablation to distin-guish the benefit of contextual source expansion from that of multiple retrieval heads alone. This increases loss from 1.847 to 1.862, supporting the importance of source diversity beyond query diversity alone. Replacing H pre with mean aggregation produces a relatively small loss degradation to 1.851, indicating that adaptive fusion provides an additional benefit while uniform averaging retains much of the gain. This modest contribution is consistent with the HC component ablations reported in mHC [39], and our experiments also indicate that H pre 

contributes to training stability. 

Hyperparameter Sensitivity. Rows (5) and (8)–(12) examine the number of streams and block granularity. Two streams already capture most of the improvement over Vanilla, achieving a loss of 1.850. Increasing 𝑚 to four lowers loss to 1.847, while eight streams yield only a marginal further improvement to 1.846. We therefore use 𝑚 = 4 by default. For block granularity, 𝑆 = 2, 4, 8 correspond to 𝑁 = 15, 8, 4 blocks, respectively, excluding the embedding source. Their losses are 1.845, 1.847, and 1.852, compared with 1.843 for Full MSAR. Thus, retaining approximately eight blocks recovers most of the benefit of full depth access, with diminishing gains from finer partitioning. Consistent with the block-size ablations in AttnRes, we use 𝑁 ≈ 8 as the default. 

## 5. Conclusion and Outlook 

We introduce Multi-Stream Attention Residuals (MSAR), which extends single-stream residual retrieval to multi-head depth attention. MSAR draws source diversity from the sequence axis through lightweight multi-scale causal convolutions, retrieves independently over depth for each stream, and aggregates the retrieved states with adaptive scalar mixing. By storing single-stream summaries and expanding them on demand, Block MSAR matches Block AttnRes’s persistent source storage and pipeline communication costs while supporting cross-stage caching and two-phase computation. Experiments on 18B and 28B MoE models show consistent downstream improvements over prior residual variants, while scaling-law experiments yield an iso-loss compute speedup of 1.58 × relative to the standard residual baseline. These results establish MSAR as an effective residual architecture for improving the quality and compute efficiency of language model pre-training. More broadly, our results suggest that the benefits of multi-state representations and cross-layer access are not fully overlapping, motivating more elegant residual designs that combine their complementary strengths with efficient execution across diverse model architectures and large-scale pre-training settings. 11 References

Diverse Patterns across Streams. Within the same sublayer type, different streams develop distinct source preferences. In Pre-MLP reads beyond the first block, prominent attention to the embedding is concentrated in Stream 2 (Conv-4) and Stream 3 (Conv-8), while Stream 1 (identity) and Stream 4 (Conv-12) assign relatively little weight to this source. The streams also differ in which blocks they repeatedly access: Stream 2 (Conv-4) and Stream 4 (Conv-12) exhibit strong vertical bands at block 6, whereas Stream 3 (Conv-8) shows a pronounced preference for block 7. These differences produce distinct vertical attention patterns across streams, showing that MSAR learns diverse source-selection distributions rather than repeating a single depth mixture.
