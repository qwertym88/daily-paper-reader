# Stability-Shaped Deep Graph Learning 

## Junyou Zhu, Langzhou He, Fenying Cai, Christian Nauck, Ping Xiong, Chao Gao, Philip S. Yu, Life Fellow, IEEE ,Klaus-Robert M ¨ uller, J ¨ urgen Kurths, Frank Hellmann 

## ✦

Abstract —In deep graph neural networks, increasing depth enlarges the receptive field but often leads to over-smoothing, where node rep-resentations tend to align. We develop a unified, mode-wise stabil-ity framework for deep GNN propagation that provides a principled characterization of over-smoothing. By interpreting layer depth as time and layer updates as graph-coupled dynamics, over-smoothing can be understood as an undesirable dynamical synchronization of features, for which the master stability curve provides a theoretical tool to assess the stability of synchrony. Guided by this theory, we further propose Stability-

Shaped Deep Graph Learning (SDGL) to mitigate over-smoothing in deep GNNs. SDGL has two complementary instantiations: one induces controlled Turing instability to replace synchronization with spatial pat-tern formation, and the other maintains stable near-critical propagation. Experiments on diverse node- and graph-level benchmarks demonstrate the improved depth scaling and consistent accuracy gains over strong baselines, including graphs exhibiting long-range dependencies. 

Index Terms —Graph neural networks, Deep graph learning, Over-smoothing, Spectral analysis, Stability analysis.

This phenomenon is commonly referred to as over-smoothing ,where after many layers embeddings become nearly con-stant across nodes and the model loses discriminative power [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. For linear message passing, the mechanism behind over-smoothing is explicit. A growing line of work views deep GNN propagation through the lens of dynamical systems [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Motivated by this, we study over-smoothing as a sta-bility phenomenon around the synchronized state. The key point is that over-smoothing is not about the absolute value of embeddings, but about the decay of non-synchronous devi-ations (node-to-node differences) under repeated propaga-tion. If the synchronized state is strongly attracting, any non-synchronous component is rapidly suppressed, lead-ing to collapse (over-smoothing). If it is weakly attracting, useful variation can persist at large depth. If it is repelling, deviations will grow exponentially, either without bound or until they are stabilized by nonlinear behavior far from the synchronous state. Fig. 1(a) illustrates this intuition. This reframes the depth problem as a stability question: 

how fast do deviations from synchrony contract under repeated propagation, and how does this contraction rate determine the practically usable depth before informative variation is lost? 

The key challenge is to understand which graph-frequency components of node-to-node differences, equiv-alently which spectral modes of deviations from synchrony, decay fastest as depth increases. For linear message passing, this analysis is straightforward. The propagation operator has an eigenbasis, so any signal can be decomposed into in-dependent spectral modes, and each mode decays at its own rate [20]. For nonlinear graph-coupled dynamics, however, the situation becomes more subtle. The node-wise nonlinear update mixes feature channels and interacts with the graph coupling, so the spectral components of the differences are no longer obviously independent. Consequently, it becomes unclear which parts will be suppressed or amplified by deep propagation. To address this, we develop a decoupled, spectral mode-wise stability theoretical framework inspired by the master stability formalism from dynamical systems [21]. The core idea is to linearize the dynamics around the synchronized state and express deviations in the eigenbasis of the graph operator. This reduces the full coupled nonlinear system to a collection of linear systems parameterized by the graph eigenvalue. As a result, each graph mode is associated with its own stability margin. Treating the graph eigenvalue as a free parameter, the stability margin provides a single stability curve that is independent of the specific graph. This curve separates the contribution of graph topology from that of the local nonlinear update, and it predicts whether non-synchronous structure at a given frequency will be rapidly damped, selectively amplified, or only weakly contracted (Fig. 1(b)). Importantly, the curve directly suggests how to design architectures that either create a band-limited am-plification window for pattern formation, or keep all non-synchronous modes stable but near-critical, so that useful variation persists at large depth. 

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:  

> •

To the best of our knowledge, we develop the first unified master stability-inspired theoretical frame-work for nonlinear dynamical system-based GNNs that casts over-smoothing as a mode-wise stability problem in deep propagation.  

> •

Building on this theory, we propose two deep archi-tectures that mitigate over-smoothing in complemen-tary ways: one selectively amplifies a band of graph frequencies, while the other keeps non-synchronous components stable and weakly damped for deep propagation.  

> •

We validate our approach on homophilic and het-erophilic node classification and on graph classifica-tion with long-range dependencies, and observe con-sistent gains from stability-shaped deep propagation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 Deep GNNs and depth-related degradation. 

While graph neural networks excel at learning representa-tions by fusing local structure and node features [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], standard shallow message passing struggles to capture long-range dependencies due to its restricted receptive field. Scaling to deeper architectures naturally ad-dresses this limitation by enabling information flow across distant nodes [7], [27]. However, this depth introduces a severe trade-off: repeated neighborhood aggregation pro-gressively dilutes node-specific variations, leading to over-smoothing and a critical loss of discriminative power. To counter this depth-induced degradation, significant effort has been invested in optimizing the training and archi-tectural design of deep GNNs. For instance, extensive em-pirical benchmarks reveal that carefully orchestrated train-ing techniques can partially recover the performance of deep message-passing networks [3]. Other architectural interven-tions, such as the learnable generalized aggregation in Deep-erGCN [12] or decoupled greedy training frameworks [28], explicitly aim to mitigate optimization hurdles and improve depth scalability. Yet, these practical advances collectively point to a more fundamental issue: the bottleneck in deep graph learning extends beyond empirical optimization. It is intrinsically tied to the long-horizon asymptotic behavior of the propagation operator itself. This realization directly motivates the need for theoretical tools capable of explicitly characterizing how information evolves and persists at large depths. 

2.2 Spectral filtering and mode-aware control. 

A complementary perspective interprets graph propagation as a form of spectral filtering [29], where repeated mes-sage passing typically acts as a low-pass operator. This inherent bias suppresses high-frequency variations, heav-ily favoring globally smooth representations. To counteract this and adapt to structural heterogeneity, a rich literature has emerged around designing flexible graph filters. For instance, learning node-dependent filters can alleviate the mismatch between global low-pass operations and local heterogeneous homophily conditions [30]. Crucially, these developments highlight that the impact of deep propagation is governed not just by the number of layers, but by pre-cisely which graph frequencies are amplified or attenuated. Parallel to modifying the filter response, other ap-proaches aim to improve representation quality by funda-mentally enriching the structural information available to the model. This includes augmenting standard neighbor-hood aggregation with subgraph isomorphism counting to strictly increase expressive power [31], or extending graph learning to simplicial complexes to capture both direct and indirect higher-order relations [32]. 3

Generally, these advancements highlight that scaling deep graph models demands a delicate balance between expressive structural encoding and controlled propagation behavior. While our work is rooted in this spectral view-point, we depart from the common objective of simply designing another expressive filter. Instead, we investigate a more fundamental mechanism: how each graph frequency mode inherently evolves, persists, or vanishes as depth increases. By introducing a graph-frequency-dependent sta-bility curve, we explicitly characterize the long-term fate of deviations from synchrony. This reframing elevates mode-wise propagation behavior from an implicit consequence of a chosen filter into an explicitly analyzable and tunable object, forging a principled link between spectral structure, dynamical stability, and deep information preservation. 

2.3 Continuous dynamics and dynamical systems per-spectives. 

Another compelling alternative to discrete message passing recasts graph propagation as the evolution of a continuous dynamical system, where layer depth naturally corresponds to time [15], [33]. This continuous perspective gives a rig-orous mathematical way for analyzing representation col-lapse. For instance, framing deep graph learning through the lens of coupled oscillators demonstrates that mitigat-ing over-smoothing is intrinsically linked to the stability of the underlying continuous ODEs [15]. Expanding on this, a broader class of continuous mechanisms, encom-passing diffusion, wave, and reaction equations, provides a principled arsenal to counter the standard smoothing-dominated paradigm [34]. Theoretical investigations further corroborate this view: analyses of deep GCNs via Gaussian processes reveals that over-smoothing is not an inescapable fate, but rather depends on critical phase transitions within the limiting network dynamics [35]. Although these works establish the value of dynami-cal systems theory for understanding deep GNNs, many existing analyses remain tied to a particular architecture, discretization, or propagation rule. Our work is inspired by the classical master stability formalism for synchronized coupled systems [21], yet its goal is different from merely reinterpreting a given GNN through dynamical language. We develop a decoupled mode-wise description that sepa-rates graph topology from local update dynamics through a stability curve over graph frequencies. This decoupling is important because it elevates stability from an architecture-specific observation to a transferable design principle, en-abling stability prediction and stability shaping across dif-ferent graphs and deep propagation operators. 

2.4 Reaction diffusion and graph pattern formation. 

PDE-inspired graph models enrich the dynamical perspec-tive by coupling simple diffusion with counteracting re-action or transport mechanisms [16]. This class of models is particularly well-suited for scaling network depth, as it introduces explicit physical priors to prevent indiscrimi-nate smoothing. For instance, incorporating flexible reaction terms can actively sustain spatial variation, improving ro-bustness against over-smoothing across diverse homophily conditions [36]. Similarly, introducing directional transport alongside nonlinear channel interactions broadens the class of achievable propagation behaviors [37]. Beyond pure PDE analogies, disentangling the propagation flow over relevant versus irrelevant connections, such as through edge split-ting, further reinforces the consensus that naive smoothing indiscriminately mixes both harmful and useful signals [38]. Our work is closest in spirit to these dynamical and PDE-motivated approaches, since we also aim to control how deep propagation acts on nontrivial graph structure. The key distinction, however, is that prior models typically obtain their stability behavior implicitly from a chosen transport, diffusion, or reaction mechanism, whereas we make the mode-wise stability profile itself an explicit design target. In other words, our goal is not only to propose a new propagation dynamics, but to specify and shape in advance which graph-frequency bands should decay, persist, or be selectively amplified as depth grows. This viewpoint yields a qualitatively different level of control over deep graph learning. It supports both controlled band-limited instability for graph pattern formation and near-critical yet stable propagation for preserving informative nonsynchronous components over long depths. Thus, our framework provides a unified stability-centered perspec-tive that goes beyond architecture-specific PDE design and directly connects spectral modes, dynamical behavior, and deep representation quality.

This perspective is useful for analyzing stability and describing how node rep-resentations evolve under repeated propagation. Our theory targets general nonlinear graph-coupled dynamics. As a run-ning example, we employ reaction–diffusion dynamics [16] throughout the paper.

## 4 THEORY FOR DEEP PROPAGATION 

We develop a spectral mode-wise stability theory for deep graph propagation. Our goal is to predict when re-peated propagation suppresses, preserves, or amplifies non-constant structure, and to translate this understanding into design objectives. 4.4 MSF regimes and stability-oriented objectives 

The sign and magnitude of Λ( μ) in Equation (9) define three regimes that we target in our deep GNN layer designs. 

4.4.1 (I) Dissipative regime (diffusion-only). 

When f ≡ 0, we have J = 0 and therefore Λ( μ) = μΛ(1) ≤

0 for all μ ≥ 0. Larger μ yields more negative margins, so higher-frequency non-synchronous modes decay faster. This recovers the over-smoothing behavior of a heat-kernel-style message passing, such as GCN. As, for example, the analysis in [40] and [41] shows, many typical activation functions are 1-Lipschitz and thus do not materially change this smoothing dynamic. 

4.4.2 (II) Diffusion-induced (DI) instability regime. 

A diffusion-induced instability occurs when the local re-action is stable at μ = 0 but becomes unstable over an intermediate range of μ

Λ(0) < 0, ∃ μ ∈ [μa, μ b] such that Λ( μ) > 0, (10) and Λ( μ) < 0 outside this interval. On a graph, μ is instantiated by the discrete spectrum {μk} of −G. Pattern-forming growth occurs when some non-synchronous mode enters the positive-MSF band, meaning that there exists 

k ≥ 2 with Λ( μk) > 0. To make this criterion verifiable and trainable, we specialize J − μC to a 2 × 2 activator–inhibitor block with anisotropic diagonal diffusion, where instability reduces to a determinant test and yields an explicit unstable interval (μ−, μ +) (Corollary B.1 in Appendix B.3). 

4.4.3 (III) Near-critical (NC) stable regime. 

To enable deep propagation without collapse, we want all non-synchronous modes to be stable while remaining only weakly contractive 

−δMSF ≤ Λ( μk) ≤ − ϵMSF < 0, k ≥ 2, (11) with small δMSF and potentially very small ϵMSF . In discrete time, this corresponds to layer maps whose spectral radii are close to one on modes with k ≥ 2. This mitigates over-smoothing while preserving stability.

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We empirically validate SDGL from four complementary perspectives. First, we evaluate whether stability-shaped propagation improves predictive performance on diverse real-world node-classification (Section 6.2) and graph-level classification (Section 6.3) benchmarks. Second, we test whether SDGL exhibits better depth scaling at the task level than diffusion-dominated baselines as the number of layers increases (Section 6.4). Third, we examine whether the ob-served empirical behavior is consistent with our theoretical picture through mechanism-oriented diagnostics, including Dirichlet energy evolution (Section 6.5) and spectral vali-dation of near-critical propagation (Section 6.6). Fourth, we assess whether SDGL remains parameter-efficient as depth increases (Section 6.7).

6.7 Parameter count scaling with depth 

Although SDGL maintains two coupled fields and outputs a 

2d-dimensional representation, its propagation core remains parameter-efficient. In particular, each layer introduces only a small number of channel-wise reaction parameters to-gether with a few diffusion scalars, whereas the dominant components of the model, namely the input lifting layers and the output classifier, are independent of depth. As a result, increasing depth has only a limited effect on the total parameter count. To quantify this effect, we compare the number of trainable parameters under varying depths 

L ∈ { 3, 10 , 30 , 60 } on the Photo dataset. For all models, we fix the hidden width to 128 (and use 8 attention heads for GAT) and vary only the number of propagation layers. Table 6 shows that the parameter count of SDGL grows only mildly with depth. From L = 3 to L = 60 , the total number of parameters increases from 0.196 M to 0.255 Mfor SDGL-NC and from 0.195 M to 0.239 M for SDGL-DI. In contrast, GCN and GAT exhibit much steeper growth because they require layer-specific weight matrices, and in the case of GAT, repeated multi-head projections. This difference becomes substantial in the deep regime. At 60 

layers, SDGL-NC and SDGL-DI use 4.14 × and 4.40 × fewer parameters than GCN, respectively, and 242 × and 258 ×

fewer parameters than GAT. It is worth noting that at very small depth, such as 

L = 3 , GCN may have fewer parameters because SDGL incurs an upfront cost to lift the input into two interacting fields. However, this overhead is nearly depth-independent, and its relative impact quickly diminishes as the number of layers increases. These results indicate that SDGL sup-ports substantially deeper propagation without the param-eter growth typically associated with conventional deep message-passing architectures.

C.1 Dataset statistics 

We evaluate SDGL on two tasks. (i) Transductive node classification. We use widely adopted single-graph benchmarks, including homophilic citation and co-purchase/co-authorship networks (Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed, Amazon-Computers, Amazon-Photo, Coauthor-CS, Coauthor-Physics) and heterophilic web/actor graphs (Actor, Chameleon, Squirrel). For these datasets, we follow the standard public splits and protocols used in prior work. 

(ii) Graph classification via propagation structure. We use FakeNewsNet with its two standard subsets, PolitiFact 

and GossipCop . Each instance corresponds to one news ar-ticle paired with a diffusion graph derived from its social context, including related Twitter posts and user engage-ments/interactions. Nodes represent users/posts (depend-ing on preprocessing), edges encode observed interaction links, and the label indicates whether the article is Real or 

Fake based on expert verification. We preprocess the data following [45] and use a 75%/25% train–test split.
