# Physics-Residual Dynamics and Reduced-Order Whole-Body Planning for Obstacle-Aware Human–Robot Cloth Co-Transportation 

Moein Forouhar, Kosar Behnia, Anirvan Dutta, Hamid Sadeghian, Ville Kyrki, Sami Haddadin, Gokhan Alcan, Eckehard Steinbach 

Abstract — Human–robot co-transportation of deformable ob-jects requires predicting object deformation during motion, since obstacle clearance depends on both the grasp points and the unactuated interior. We present a hierarchical planning framework that combines a learned cloth model with a reduced-order whole-body model of a dual-arm mobile manipulator. A physics-residual conditional recurrent variational autoencoder (p-cRVAE) predicts the full cloth configuration from grasp-point observations by learning a residual correction to a com-putationally efficient linearized physics model, limiting error accumulation over 40-step planning horizon. The predicted cloth dynamics are embedded in a model predictive path integral (MPPI) planner using a reduced-order representation of a dual-arm mobile manipulator that preserves the non-holonomic base constraint and arm workspace limits. An MPC layer subsequently refines the sampled motion into smooth, executable references for whole-body control. The reduced-order formulation achieves tracking performance comparable to the full 17-DoF model while reducing computation time by approximately 80%. Across four co-transportation scenarios and two carrying speeds, the proposed framework maintains cloth-obstacle clearance where a corner-following baseline re-sults in collisions, while whole-body refinement reduces final cloth deformation from 0.93 m to 0.28 m. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Human–robot co-transportation of deformable objects is an important capability for service robotics, with applications in handling textiles, bedding, and flexible loads that are difficult for a single agent to manipulate. By sharing the task, the human and robot can distribute the support of the object while guiding it toward the desired configuration. Unlike rigid objects, deformable objects change shape under gravity and variations in their boundary conditions. Consequently, for obstacle-aware transportation, collision avoidance based only on the grasping points and initial object shape is insufficient; the evolving shape must also be considered during planning. Developing a suitable predictive model is therefore challenging due to the high-dimensional, nonlinear, and coupled dynamics of deformable objects [1]. Physics-based models provide a physically grounded ap-proach to capturing deformation dynamics, but their compu-tational cost makes them impractical for motion planning.

Data-driven methods offer inexpensive surrogates, but re-quire careful strategies to achieve reliable generalization [2]. This challenge is further complicated during execution, where only partial observations may be available. Although dense visual point clouds provide detailed surface informa-tion, occlusions and large deformations make reliable point correspondence and state estimation difficult [3]. A practical deformable object model should therefore combine physical grounding with computational efficiency while inferring the evolving full-object configuration from sparse observations that can be reliably obtained during transportation, ideally the grasped corner points. Beyond object modeling, co-transportation requires a mo-tion planner that accounts for the robot model to generate feasible trajectories while maintaining the desired object geometry and avoiding environmental obstacles. Addressing these requirements in a single high-fidelity optimization problem can be computationally demanding. Sampling-based predictive methods, such as model predictive path integral (MPPI) control, offer an attractive alternative by evaluating candidate action sequences through parallel rollouts without requiring derivatives of the dynamics or cost function, and are well-suited to high-dimensional search spaces. However, finite-sample variability can produce noisy action sequences, which are undesirable when smooth reference trajectories are required. Conversely, optimization-based methods provide trajectory refinement but become increasingly challenging as the planning dimensionality and nonlinearity grow. Furthermore, effective co-transportation of large de-formable objects benefits from a dual-arm robot with a mobile base: the two arms provide distributed support, while arXiv:2610.06641v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 base mobility extends the workspace beyond the arms’ stationary reach. We study this problem on GARMI [4], which has two torque-controlled, seven-degree-of-freedom arms and a velocity-controlled, nonholonomic differential-drive base. Thus the developed planner must coordinate arm and base motions while respecting joint, workspace, and nonholonomic constraints. To address these challenges, we propose a hierarchical framework that combines a learned physics-residual dynam-ics model with sampling- and optimization-based planning. The planning problem is deliberately divided between the two paradigms to exploit their complementary strengths. An upper MPPI layer combines the deformable-object model with a reduced-order robot model to efficiently explore candidate transportation motions while accounting for the evolving cloth configuration, environmental obstacles, and robot constraints. A lower MPC layer then refines the result-ing trajectory into smooth and feasible task-space references for the robot’s whole-body controller. The key contributions of this work include:  

> •

Physics-residual cloth dynamics (p-cRVAE): Adeformable-object model combining a coarse physics-based model with a learned residual predictor to im-prove prediction accuracy and generalization beyond the training distribution, building on [5].  

> •

Reduced-order whole-body robot model: A reduced-order representation of GARMI that captures the rele-vant arm and mobile-base workspaces and constraints, enabling efficient MPPI planning and MPC refinement.  

> •

Hierarchical planning: A sampling- and optimization-based architecture that jointly coordinates the de-formable object, robot arms, and mobile base while generating smooth reference trajectories for real-time whole-body control. We extensively evaluate the proposed framework in simula-tion and further assess its feasibility on the physical GARMI platform. II. R ELATED WORK 

Human–robot collaborative transportation of deformable ob-jects has largely been approached without a representation of the object’s configuration. Kruse et al. [6] keep a sheet taut through joint torque sensing and add a depth camera that detects folds, reducing the cloth to a single corrective direction. Nicola et al. [7] train a convolutional network on segmented depth images to estimate the relative pose between the robot’s grasp and the operator’s hands, assuming the material between the hands to be rigid, and drive the robot to hold that pose near a rest configuration. De Schepper et al. [8] track the operator’s skeleton alongside force–torque measurements, inferring intention from human key points rather than from the material. Sirintuna et al. [9] blend an admittance reference with the operator’s hand velocity through an adaptive index that summarises the object’s deformability in a single scalar, and apply the same interface to a multi-robot team [10]. These methods do not use a model of the deformable object within the control framework. A separate line of work models the configuration of deformable objects directly. Several approaches use dense visual observations, such as point clouds or RGB images, to encode the object into a compact latent state and predict its future motion, as in [11], [12]. These models can capture shape details such as wrinkles and curvature, but they require visual observations of the deformable object during inference. However, obtaining a continuous and unobstructed view of the material can be difficult because of occlusions caused by the robot, the human, or the object itself. Behnia et al. [5] avoid this requirement by predicting the full configuration of a rope and fabric using only the measured positions of their corner nodes. Their conditional recurrent variational autoencoder (cRVAE) is used as the forward model of a receding-horizon controller for obstacle-aware manipulation. At the robot level, whole-body control coordinates arm and base motions to track grasp-point trajectories while managing redundancy. For safe human–robot interaction, impedance controllers are used for both fixed-base [13] and mobile manipulators [14] to enable compliant behavior during physical interaction with the environment. For mobile robots with nonholonomic constraints in the base, Iskandar et al. [15] first derived the reduced-order dynamic model of the robot that is constraint-free and then built the whole-body control framework upon it. For redundant robots, task-priority control [16] organizes tasks hierarchically, projecting lower-priority commands into the null space of higher-priority tasks to avoid interference with their execution. Any remaining redundancy can be exploited to accommodate ad-ditional control objectives [17]. Alternatively, optimization-based approaches formulate the control framework as an optimization problem that achieves multiple task objectives through weighted cost functions [18]. In addition to task execution, the controller must maintain the robot within its admissible operating region. To this end, potential-based control terms have been developed to incorporate safety-related objectives into whole-body control [19]. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider the problem of co-transporting a deformable object, such as a rectangular piece of cloth, by a human and a robot in an environment containing obstacles, while preserving the initial shape (i.e., minimizing deformation from its nominal carried configuration). The human leads the motion by holding two corners of the object, while the robot holds the opposite two and follows the human. We assume that the human trajectory is fixed over the task duration and does not account for environmental obstacles. The objective is to determine a robot grasp trajectory that maintains the desired object configuration while avoiding collisions with the environment and satisfying the robot’s kinematic and workspace constraints. A. Cloth Model 

The cloth is represented as a regular Nu × Nv particle mesh, with N = NuNv particles having positions Xk ∈ RN×3 and velocities Vk at time k. The grasped particles C follow gk =[grk, ghk ], while the remaining particles evolve under internal forces and gravity. We use a mesh-based Hookean mass-spring model [21], which is sufficient for the considered regime: the cloth is suspended at its four corners, remains approximately single-valued without self- or ground contact, and undergoes sufficiently slow motions such that gravita-tional draping dominates inertial and wrinkling effects. The model thus captures the bulk deformation relevant to obstacle clearance. Neighboring particles are connected by structural, shear, and bending springs. B. p-cRVAE Structure 

The mass-spring model in Sec. IV-A is too expensive for the sampling-based planner, requiring ∼ 10 6 force evalu-ations over the prediction horizon. We therefore leverage the conditional recurrent variational autoencoder (cRVAE) of [5] as a learned surrogate for predicting the full cloth configuration from sparse grasp-point observations. We in-troduce key architectural modifications and augment it with a coarse physics backbone to improve rollout accuracy and generalization (see Sec. V), referring to the resulting model as p-cRVAE. Instead of directly predicting the cloth state, p-cRVAE learns a residual correction to the coarse physics prediction. As shown in Fig. 2, it consists of five components: acoarse physics backbone, recognition network, conditional prior, recurrent state, and residual decoder. The recognition network infers a stochastic latent variable from the observed state during training, while the conditional prior provides it during deployment when the full state is unavailable. The recurrent state accumulates past measurements, and the residual decoder predicts a per-particle correction to the coarse prediction.

C. Hierarchical Planning 

The coupled cloth–robot planning problem requires both global exploration of collision-free motions and local re-finement into smooth, executable trajectories. We therefore adopt a hierarchical strategy that combines sampling- and optimization-based methods. MPPI performs task-level ex-ploration using the learned cloth model, while a subsequent MPC layer refines the resulting trajectory subject to the robot’s kinematic constraints. Both stages use the proposed reduced-order model of the mobile dual-arm robot, avoiding the high-dimensional full robot model during planning while retaining the relevant base and end-effector motions.

E XPERIMENTS 

A. Training of p-cRVAE 

The p-cRVAE is trained on 930 episodes generated using the mass-spring model of Sec. IV-A, each 60 s long with 

∆t = 0.1 s. Grasp points are driven kinematically to decouple the data from a specific robot and cover configurations beyond the reduced-order robot’s reachable set. For every non-empty subset of the four corners, motions follow one of three patterns: sign , with shared motion and per-corner sign changes; phase , with shared motion and phase offsets; or 

independent , with separately driven corners. This produces both coordinated and highly sheared configurations (Fig. 5a), while slow grasp motions maintain the quasi-static regime. We train with Adam for 300 epochs using a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 10 −3 with cosine decay, holding out 15% of episodes for validation. The rollout length increases from 12 to 40 steps, with scheduled sampling reducing the grounded fraction from 1 .0 to 0 .3. The KL weight is annealed to β = 0.3 with a free-bits threshold of 

λ = 0.05 nats per latent dimension. The latent and memory dimensions are dz = 16 and dh = 256, respectively, and the coarse physics model uses four sub-steps per control period. Figure 5b evaluates open-loop prediction on held-out episodes with periodic measurement injection. We compare p-cRVAE with the same architecture without the coarse physics backbone. Without the backbone, error increases throughout the rollout, whereas p-cRVAE reaches an early peak and converges to a lower plateau. Despite its initial approximation error, the physics backbone improves long-horizon prediction, with spatial error maps showing reduced error near the measured grasp points and residual error concentrated in the less-constrained cloth interior. 

B. Evaluation in simulation 

We evaluate the complete framework in closed-loop MuJoCo simulation (Fig. 6a), using the mass-spring model of Sec. IV-A as the cloth plant and prescribing the human grasp points along a straight-line trajectory. The MPPI planner, whole-body motion planner, and controller run as separate processes using the same interfaces as the physical system; only the plant is simulated. 

Scenarios. We consider four increasingly challenging sce-narios at slow and fast carry speeds within a fixed time budget (see Fig. 6(a)). Scene 1 (S1) requires primarily forward and vertical motion, Scene 2 (S2) adds diagonal motion subject to the non-holonomic constraint, Scene 3 (S3) requires lateral motion and base reorientation, and Scene 4 (S4) adds an obstacle to the lateral carry. Clearance is evaluated only in S4. 

Baselines. We consider two baselines isolating the contri-butions of p-cRVAE and whole-body motion planning. Base-line I replaces the predicted-shape cost with corner following and removes the cloth–obstacle cost while retaining robot collision avoidance, following the corner-following formula-tion in [9]. Baseline II retains the proposed MPPI planner and cost but bypasses the whole-body motion planner, passing its reference directly to the whole-body controller.

Baseline II produces larger deformation because the sam-pled references are not reconciled with the full robot’s kine-matic and non-holonomic constraints. Ablations. Table II evaluates Scene 4. A shorter prediction horizon provides insufficient reaction time, reducing obstacle clearance, while longer horizons offer limited benefit at increased computational cost. Increasing the obstacle weight improves clearance at the expense of shape tracking, whereas increasing the shape weight improves tracking but can cause collision. The selected weights provide a balance between the two objectives. 

C. Real-robot validation 

The four scenarios introduced in Sec. V-B are further evalu-ated in real experiments (see Fig. 6(b)). In the real setup, the whole-body motion planner and controller operate in closed loop, whereas the high-level co-transportation tra-jectories generated by the MPPI planner are replayed from simulation and therefore executed open loop. As shown in Fig. 6b, the robot successfully coordinates with the human to transport the blanket in all four scenarios, exhibiting behavior consistent with the simulation. In the obstacle-avoidance scenario S4, the robot deforms and lifts the blanket over the obstacle while simultaneously coordinating its two arms and non-holonomic mobile base to keep the end-effectors within the feasible arm workspace and satisfy the base non-holonomic constraint. Despite the high-level planner being executed open loop, these experiments demonstrate that the reference trajectories generated by MPPI using the p-cRVAE cloth model and the reduced-order robot model are feasible for the real robot–cloth system. VI. D ISCUSSION & C ONCLUSION 

This work introduced a framework for human–robot co-transportation of a deformable object using a dual-arm nonholonomic mobile robot. Closed-loop simulations show that the upper-layer MPPI planner combines the physics-residual cloth model (p-cRVAE) and reduced-order robot model to generate trajectories that preserve the reference cloth configuration while satisfying robot constraints. The lower-layer MPC refines these trajectories into smooth and feasible references for whole-body control. While perfor-mance is comparable to the baselines in obstacle-free sce-narios, the proposed framework provides improved obstacle avoidance. The sim-to-real gap of the mass-spring model has been studied in [5], and corner-point perception provides a relatively simple observation interface. However, closing the remaining sim-to-real gap and achieving reliable closed-loop operation on the physical system remain important directions for future work. We will also investigate learning a family of deformable-object models and incorporating latent-state filtering to improve robustness to model uncertainty and partial observations.
