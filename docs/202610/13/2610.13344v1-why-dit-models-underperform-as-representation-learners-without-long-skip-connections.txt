# Abstract 

Diffusion feature — intermediate activations extracted from diffusion model back-bones — has emerged as a promising approach for unifying generation and vision understanding (discrimination). Despite the advancement of diffusion backbones into Diffusion Transformers (DiT), it has been found in previous literature that DiT models tend to yield weaker features despite their stronger generation capability. This poses a threat to good generation-understanding unification. In this work, we show via controlled from-scratch training that long skip connections (LSCs) affect feature quality: a DiT with LSCs can produce better features. Furthermore, through detailed diagnosis, we reveal the mechanism causing this effect. Specifically, the input to a diffusion backbone contains both the latent image and the diffusion noise, where the diffusion noise, though appearing in the form of noises, actually reflects the information of the diffusion trajectory. To make the final prediction of a noisy variable ( v or ϵ), the diffusion backbone needs both signals. However, carrying both signals through the main backbone effectively “pollutes” the image signal with noises, leading to poor feature quality. In comparison, LSCs can shortcut diffusion noises directly to where they are needed, leaving much weaker noise signals in the main backbone, thus improving feature quality. To name the broader impact, this reveals that LSCs are not a mere trick but closely related to the diffusion objective. The code is here. 

# 1 Introduction 

Diffusion models are a trending type of generative models nowadays [16, 10, 30, 29]. Beyond generation, it is also an important and emerging topic to explore the unification of generation and vision understanding tasks, such as semantic segmentation [9], depth estimation [4], or semantic correspondence [26] with diffusion models [2, 51, 45, 17, 19, 20, 7]. Diffusion feature is an important sub-family of this research trend. Every diffusion model pre-trained for generation , e.g. , Stable Diffusion v1.5 [30], has a neural network backbone for ϵ- or v-prediction [16, 33], and many intermediate activations are created in the computation process. If we extract these activations from the backbone, they could serve as versatile vision features, essentially similar to how people extract activations from ResNet [13] backbones as features. High-quality diffusion features can enable better generation-understanding unification [40, 28].

When studying diffusion features, the basic belief and hypothesis are that the advancement of generative capability naturally brings the advancement of understanding capability [25]. Here, the generative capability can be measured with FID score [15], CLIP score [14], or user study [29], and the understanding capability can be measured using the performance on standard dense perception tasks [9, 4, 26]. With this belief in mind, and considering that the mainstream architecture for diffusion models is turning to Diffusion Transformers (DiT) [27] from U-Nets, it is important that we can extract high-quality DiT features. However, it is reported that DiT models tend to yield weaker features despite their stronger generation [11] (Figure 1(a)). This both poses a threat to generation-understanding unification and suggests a representation degradation phenomenon hidden behind the good generation, which may manifest one day and limit the ceiling of generation. Notably, although the phenomenon is reported [11], the cause is still only preliminarily understood: the massive activation explanation appears to be applicable mostly to correspondence tasks (Sec-tion A.1), while the feature degradation exists universally (Section A.3). This indicates that the massive activation explanation is not exhaustive, and there should be a more universal cause. On the other hand, many recent studies report that adding long skip connections (LSCs), a structure in U-Nets that shortcut information from the downsampling stage directly to the upsampling stage, can enhance DiT generation. Inspired by this, we confirm via a controlled comparison experiment that the wide absence of LSCs from standard DiTs is a more universal cause, or at least part of it. We do not stop at knowing LSCs are good but further ask why . It is common sense that such skip connections can carry high-frequency information like edges, and this is also used to explain the effectiveness of LSCs for DiT generation in some literature [1]. However, suppose LSCs only diverge some high-frequency semantics like edges from the middle part; why are features here improved simply by removing some high-frequency semantics from them? Through further hypothesizing and validating, we reveal a deeper mechanism that is fundamentally new compared to commonsense. Specifically, [25] shows that diffusion backbones do not directly predict a noisy variable from a noisy input, but instead reconstruct a semantic representation of the clean image z0 in the middle of the network, based on which the final noise prediction is made. To predict the final noisy variable, both the reconstructed representation and the diffusion noise carried in the noisy input should be transported to the final noise prediction section. Here, the diffusion noise is not a random noise: it appears in the form of noises but reflects certain information, i.e. , the current denoising trajectory. Without LSCs , the only possible passage for the diffusion noise is through the entire target reconstruction section, thus “ polluting ” the features extracted from this section, since it still takes the form of noises (Figure 1(b)). With LSCs , however, the diffusion noise can take the shortcut and thus bypass the target reconstruction section, maintaining high feature quality in this section (Figure 1(c)). Hence, we add to the knowledge that LSCs can carry high-frequency semantics that they also carry diffusion noises. This reveals that LSCs are not a mere trick to boost generation but are closely related to the diffusion objective. In general, our contribution is as follows: 2• We provide one of the first systematic studies focusing on the discriminative quality of DiT features and notice a significant feature quality discrepancy between DiTs and U-Nets. • We attribute the feature quality discrepancy to the LSC structure and provide a detailed, rigorously supported mechanistic explanation. • Despite the initial representation learning motivation, we hope our mechanistic findings could make LSCs in DiTs a more widely adopted practice rather than an optional heuristic. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 DiT Feature 

There are currently mainly three approaches to exploiting the understanding potential from a gen-erative diffusion model: diffusion classifier [20, 7], generative modeling [17, 19, 40], and diffusion feature [2, 51, 45, 25, 35, 11]. Diffusion feature extracts intermediate activations from a diffusion backbone as features, regarding the diffusion backbone as an information bottleneck that compresses an image into a latent embedding. These features are dense and rich in information, but not directly interpretable by humans. Thus, a downstream model is required to process these features and produce the final predictions. Most previous knowledge about diffusion feature is based on U-Net diffusion backbones. [11] is the first to extend this methodology to DiT and surprisingly find that DiT features are generally worse. The explanation proposed in [11] is massive activations [34], where the scale of a small number of elements in the feature is extremely large. To some extent, diffusion feature can be viewed as a representation learning method. However, the model is not directly trained for representation learning, but a generatively trained diffusion model is adapted to this task. Compared to typical representation learning methods like DINO [5], diffusion feature performs especially well in scenarios where the background is relatively identical and the information is sparse [44]. 

2.2 LSC-Augmented DiT Models 

Conventionally, long skip connections (LSCs) are a typical structure in U-Nets. Since U-Nets adopt a downsampling-upsampling architecture, it is believed mandatory to utilize such skip connections to shortcut low-level/high-frequency semantics like edges, which will otherwise be lost during downsampling. At first, when the diffusion model community shifts from U-Net backbones to DiT backbones, LSCs were naturally discarded, since DiTs have identical resolution across blocks. However, quite a few recent studies find that adding LSCs to DiT can enhance generation [1, 6, 41]. Compared to them, our findings are fundamentally different in two ways: (i) We find LSCs are beneficial not only to generation but also to representation learning. (ii) We reveal the diffusion noise 

bypassing behavior of LSCs, which is different from the high-frequency semantic shortcutting [1] and loss smoothing [6] explanations. Additionally, there are also LSC-augmented DiT models with actual resolution changes [38, 37]. To isolate the contribution of LSCs, we use isotropic DiTs as the backbone to add LSCs onto. For further comparison with models with actual resolution changes, please refer to Section A.2. 

# 3 Preliminaries 

3.1 Diffusion Models 

Based on [22], the diffusion process starts at a point sampled from the source distribution, progres-sively moving it towards the target distribution, where the two distributions are typically noises and images. Each step during this process is called a timestep t ∈ [0 , 1] . At each timestep, a backbone network is used to predict the velocity of the movement. We can denote this network as vθ ,parameterized by θ. This network used to be U-Nets, but now it is mostly ViTs, known as DiTs. To actually train the model, we need to sample an image x0, its corresponding user prompt c, and a timestep t, calculating a ground truth based on them. First, the image is compressed into the latent 3space by a pre-trained VAE as z0 = VAE (x0), and we sample a Gaussian noise z1. Then, we have 

zt = tz 0 + (1 − t)z1

vt = dz t

dt = z0 − z1

(1) Afterward, we can train the backbone network to predict the velocity: 

L = E(z0,c )∼D ,z 1,t || vθ (zt, t, c ) − vt|| 2 (2) The formulation above is denoted as flow matching , and the more conventional DDPM [16] and score matching [33] frameworks can be seen as special forms of flow matching. Notably, although the difference in the theoretical framework may not be that important, the prediction objective of the backbone model, i.e. , ϵ-, v-, or x-prediction, does matter. In the main body of this manuscript, we will use ϵ-prediction for experiments, and we will show in Section A.4 how our findings extend to v-and x-prediction. 

3.2 Diffusion Feature 

To extract a feature from a diffusion backbone, we need an image, a selected timestep, and, optionally, a prompt describing the image content. Denoting them as x0, t, c , we have: 

z0 = VAE (x0)

zt ∼ q(zt|z0, t )

v = vθ (zt, t, c )

(3) Respectively, the three lines indicate encoding the image into latent space with a VAE, sampling a noisy latent corresponding to the timestep t, and sending all three inputs into the diffusion backbone. We discard the output v. Instead, we gather some intermediate activations as features. Such activations can be extracted from a variety of spots, such as the residual flow between two adjacent layers or the query vector used to compute the attention matrix in an attention layer [25]. In DiT models, these activations are patchified 1D features, and we can reshape them back to 2D features and resize them to the original image size using bilinear interpolation. Obviously, there are many legal feature extraction spots, but they cannot all be harvested due to limited computational resources, so there is a need to select a few near-optimal activations as features. To this end, a three-step strategy can be taken [25]. (i) Based on both PCA analysis and quantitative comparison within small ranges, study the properties of features in this backbone. (ii) Based on the understanding of feature properties, rule out low-quality features qualitatively. (iii) Perform a filtered quantitative comparison among the remaining features on a small and fast downstream task to find the most performative features. In this study, we adopt training-free semantic correspondence [26] as the task. After selecting activations as features, diffusion features can be used in a very straightforward way. Basically, suppose the original input to a visual perception task is a raw image; we could replace the image with the corresponding diffusion feature. Commonly, such replacement requires the dedicated training of a compatible downstream task head [2], and we could fine-tune the diffusion backbone on the task for better performance [51], or, in much rarer cases, use diffusion features in a totally training-free manner [49]. 

# 4 Long Skip Connections Matter 

4.1 Core Hypothesis 

With the recent emergence of LSC-augmented DiTs [1, 6, 41], it gradually becomes acknowledged that LSCs are beneficial for DiT generation . Furthermore, the basic belief of diffusion feature studies tells us a stronger generation model should generally lead to stronger features. Hence, it is natural to hypothesize that LSCs are beneficial for DiT features as well. For a visual illustration of the architectures of U-Net and DiT diffusion models, refer to Figure 2(a,b). 4Figure 2: (a) A typical U-Net architecture of modern diffusion models is not purely convolutional but contains many large embedded ViT modules. (b) A DiT backbone can, from a certain perspective, be viewed as extracting an embedded ViT module and greatly extending it. (c) We can fold the DiT chain in half to form an LSC-augmented DiT. As preliminary evidence, we test on pre-trained DiT-XL/2 [27] and U-ViT [1] models where the generative capability roughly aligns. The result that U-ViT (with LSCs) yields significantly better features than DiT-XL/2 (without LSCs) (53.53 v.s. 44.01 on SPair (a)) implies the benefit of LSCs. Nevertheless, the full results in Section A.5 also suggest a controlled experiment to exclude the confounders. 

4.2 Hypothesis Validation 

We further conduct a controlled from-scratch training experiment to validate our hypothesis above. Basically, we train two DiT [27] models from scratch, with the only difference being whether there are LSCs or not, and compare them on six downstream benchmarking settings. 

Training Dataset. We train our models on ImageNet [18] 256 ×256. This dataset contains 1,281,166 images in 1000 classes, all in 256 ×256 resolution. 

Models. We choose the DiT-XL/2 option in [27]. The only modification we make to the models is adding LSC structures. The LSC structure we use is formulated as 

x ← Linear (Norm (Concat (x, skip ))) (4) where x is the inter-layer residual flow activation, skip is the activation shortcut here through LSCs, Linear is a single fully connected layer, Norm is LayerNorm, and Concat is concatenation along the channel dimension. A standard DiT architecture is linear, and intuitively, our modification folds the architecture in half from the middle to enforce it into a conceptual “U” shape. For a visual illustration, please refer to Figure 2(c).

# 5 WHY Long Skip Connections Matter 

5.1 Why Do We Not Fully Understand LSCs Yet 

At first glance, the mechanics behind LSCs’ contribution should be already known. However, we will show that two competing explanations cannot fully explain our previous experiments. (i) Feature enhancement does not solely stem from better generation. In Table 2, even when we manually align the generation capability with w/ LSC 100k , the feature quality is still better than 

w/o LSC . (ii) LSCs shortcutting high-frequency semantics ( i.e. , edges) [1] cannot fully explain the observation. Since LSCs can shortcut high-frequency semantics, the features extracted from the middle part should have more concentration of low-order semantics, which means they might perform better for coarse-grained tasks like image classification. However, what we have tested the models on are fine-grained tasks such as semantic segmentation. Hence, there should be some additional mechanisms that co-govern the gain brought by LSCs. 

5.2 Target Reconstruction Behavior 

Our hypothesis to explain why LSCs have such a positive effect is based on a finding in [25]. Here, we first re-formulate the “diffusion noise” property found in [25] as “target reconstruction”. Note that we do not claim originality for this subsection’s content. It is here to smooth the narrative later. Based on the flow matching formulation (Section 3), both the input variable and the predicted variable of the diffusion backbone network are noisy variables. A natural interpretation of this would be that diffusion activations should be mostly noisy. However, the diffusion noise property states that, in the middle section of the network, intermediate activations are rather clean from noises. Actually, these activations are an important source of high-quality diffusion features. Now, considering that diffusion 6Figure 3: Correlation Decay Slope measuring results, where we report the negative slope of the linearly regressed lines. features extracted from the middle section in fact reflect the semantics of the target image z0, this middle section’s role is to reconstruct an internal representation for the target image based on the noisy input zt. Then, the network could encode its final prediction based on this reconstructed target representation. In conclusion, this target reconstruction behavior shows that diffusion backbones take 

two rather than one step to make their final prediction. To facilitate the narrative, we conceptually divide the diffusion backbone into three coarse sections based on their roles, which are indicated by three different colors, red, green, and blue, in Figure 4: (i) Noise suppression : To prepare for the later acquisition of the target representation, the noises in the inputs are gradually refined and suppressed to some extent. (ii) Target reconstruction : Next, the backbone reconstructs a latent representation of the target image latent within activations. (iii) Noise prediction : Finally, the model predicts the velocity, which is a noisy quantity. 

5.3 Diffusion Noise Bypassing Hypothesis 

Next, we develop upon this target reconstruction behavior to form our diffusion noise bypassing hypothesis. Consider the flow matching formulation: vt = z0 − z1. We hypothesize that, to make the final prediction, both the target information ( z0) and the diffusion noise ( z1) are required, just like how both the starting and terminating points are required to determine a direction. Of the two, the target information is provided by the target reconstruction section, and the diffusion noise is carried within the input variable. Furthermore, in order for the noise prediction section to make the prediction, both target and diffusion noise signals should be accessible to the noise prediction section. Since the target reconstruction section and the noise prediction section are directly adjacent, the target information can be transported to the noise prediction section simply through the main residual flow. However, how the diffusion noise is routed is where LSCs play their role. Without LSCs , as shown in Figure 4(right), the diffusion noise must also go through the main residual, but there is a target reconstruction section in between the noise suppression and noise prediction sections. Therefore, the activations in the target reconstruction section have to spare some capacity to carry the diffusion noise, which makes their original goal, i.e. ,target reconstruction, degrade. With LSCs , as shown in Figure 4(left), however, the diffusion noise can directly take the shortcut and bypass the target reconstruction section, thus maintaining strong feature quality in the target reconstruction section. 5.4 Hypothesis Validation 

First, we use mutual information probing to actually measure the amount of the target information and diffusion noise described in the noise bypassing hypothesis, hoping to provide correlation evidence. The target information contained in each activation is defined as the mutual information between this activation and the target latent ( z0), and the diffusion noise contained in each activation is defined as the mutual information between this activation and the noise added to the latent image ( z1). To measure the mutual information, we adopt the Mutual Information Neural Estimator (MINE) [3], with a setting of (MINE hidden channel as 512, projection head channel as 256, training steps as 600). The results are plotted in Figure 5, and we can see that LSCs can indeed reduce the amount of diffusion noise and increase the amount of target information in the target reconstruction section (roughly the middle section). We also provide an alternative probing using a different MINE setting in Section A.6. Next, to provide relatively causal evidence, we conduct a controlled study. Here, what we already know is that LSCs mainly carry high-frequency information. What we want to validate is that LSCs carry not only high-frequency semantics but also diffusion noises. This is a sign of representation collapse: injecting external signals creates some information shortcut and thus trivializes the task, making training worse. (ii) Settings (ii) and (iii) both show different degradation severity compared to setting (i), meaning that removing either of the two signals will affect the training process. This further confirms that both high-frequency semantics and diffusion noises play a non-negligible role in making LSCs work. Nevertheless, since the training process is highly complex, we cannot say for sure from the results that diffusion noises are more important than high-frequency semantics or vice versa. In conclusion, LSCs actually carry a mixture of high-frequency semantics and diffusion noises, where both of them are non-negligible. This effectively extends our previous knowledge that only covers high-frequency semantics but not diffusion noises. 

5.5 Discussion 

Our diffusion noise bypassing hypothesis serves as knowledge of the inner workings of diffusion model backbones. Based on this new knowledge, we can see that LSCs can enable diffusion backbones to work in a more optimized way: the activations in the target reconstruction section no longer need to spare much capacity to carry the diffusion noise. Hence, even if one’s goal is not related to features, but only generally better diffusion models, we believe LSCs are still beneficial .For example, Skip-DiT [6] and U-ViT [1] extensively show that LSCs can enhance generation , where we believe there is a certain causal relation. To be specific, REPA [48] finds that the quality of inner representations in diffusion backbones is essential to generation, where better representations lead to stronger generation and faster convergence. Hence, it is likely that the stronger generation brought by LSCs partially stems from the stronger feature they enable. Furthermore, compared to 9explicitly aligning diffusion features to an external representation model in REPA, LSCs introduce less constraint and can thus better exploit the advantage of the diffusion training objective itself. 

# 6 Conclusion & Limitation 

This work reveals that long skip connections (LSCs) are beneficial for DiT, with regard to not only generation but also feature quality. This can also explain why current large-scale DiT models tend to yield weaker features than U-Net diffusion models, despite their stronger generation capability. More importantly, we propose a mechanistic hypothesis termed diffusion noise bypassing to explain why LSCs have such an effect. In general, we show that LSCs are not merely an engineering trick, but actually have a close relation to the diffusion process itself. Thus, we hope this work can persuade more researchers and developers to consider adding LSCs back to the backbone models in the DiT era. Unfortunately, we were not able to effectively augment existing pre-trained DiT models with LSCs, which has been found to be very difficult (Section A.8). Additionally, our controlled experiments are based on the conventional DiT architecture rather than the MMDiT architecture, which remains underexplored. We also do not claim exhaustiveness of our LSC explanation as the cause of DiT feature degradation.

A.1 Relation to Massive Activation Suppression 

In the original [11], massive activations are found to be harmful to DiT feature quality. However, they mostly focus on correspondence tasks, which conventionally use training-free nearest neighbor for prediction, leading to a natural sensitivity to massive activations. Here, we evaluate the massive activation suppression method proposed in [11] on the semantic segmentation task, with the results in Table 4. It can be observed that the performance gain is much smaller on semantic segmentation than on semantic correspondence. This suggests that massive activations are not that harmful to tasks beyond semantic correspondence, especially when a trained downstream head that can refine the features is involved. Additionally, comparing the results on SPair (a), we can see that when adding massive activation suppression to w/o LSC and w/ LSC , their performance is both improved, with the gap between them remaining. This indicates that the effects of massive activation suppression and LSCs are roughly orthogonal. As additional evidence, we also measure the massive activation phenomenon when LSCs are added. From Table 5, we can observe that LSCs have a slight effect on suppressing massive activations, but the effect is limited. This shows LSCs have additional, separate mechanics. In general, massive activation as the cause of DiT feature degradation is not exhaustive.

A.7 Two Corollaries of Noise Bypassing Hypothesis A.7.1 Duo-Peak and Single-Peak Quality-Layer Curves 

If we plot a figure with the layer index of a diffusion backbone as the x-axis and the understanding capability of the feature extracted from this layer (evaluated under a certain discriminative task) as the y-axis, we obtain a quality-layer curve. We observe that diffusion models without LSCs tend to manifest a quality-layer curve with a single peak, and models with LSCs tend to manifest a duo-peak curve, as shown in Figure 8. Next, we provide a hypothesized explanation for this observation. Based on the three-section division of a diffusion backbone, features through the noise suppression section gradually grow in quality as they go into deeper layers in this section, as the noises are gradually suppressed, which forms the rising slope before the first peak. Then, in the noise prediction section, noise increases, causing the final descending slope. Importantly, the behavior of the target reconstruction section differs based on whether there are LSCs. According to another finding in [25], when trying to reconstruct an internal representation for the target image, activations capturing mostly abstract and coarse-grained 17 Figure 7: Mutual information between each layer’s activation and noise/target information, measured using MINE, under the second setting.

Notably, activations containing abstract and coarse-grained information, when used as a diffusion feature, tend to be inferior to the other features. Therefore, when there are LSCs, there will be a slight drop between the two peaks. In comparison, with no LSCs, the activations in the target reconstruction section would be relatively noisy, and the noises overrun the aforementioned detailed change, showing only one peak in the curve.

A.8 Attempts to Add LSCs to Pre-Trained DiTs A.8.1 Method 

We have tried several different methods to add LSCs to pre-trained DiTs, basically divided into two-stage and single-stage training types. 

Two-Stage Method . Following the adaptation method in Skip-DiT [6], we divide the training into two stages. At the first stage, we freeze the backbone and only train LSC connectors until they converge, indicated by roughly recognizable generation results. Then, we unfreeze the backbone and train the entire model altogether. Ideally, during the second stage, the generative capability would quickly restore and improve, but we did not observe such behavior. Moreover, we notice that the features in the upsampling stage become very noisy. Therefore, we conclude that the two-stage fine-tuning method might be applicable only to certain diffusion models. We hypothesize that the first-stage training introduces too much degradation to the model, which is hard to correct in the second stage. To tackle the drawback of this method, we further propose a new single-stage method. 

Single-Stage Method . Directly adding a vanilla LSC structure into a pre-trained DiT backbone will damage its original behavior and make single-stage fine-tuning hard, as we need to restore the generative capability from a low point. To tackle this, we propose the zero-init LSC design, which aims to make no impact on model behavior at initialization. In the zero-init LSC design, we put a residual connection in the LSC: 

x = x + Linear (Norm (skip )) (5) where Norm is layer normalization, and Linear is a linear layer. The weight and bias of this linear layer are both initialized to zero, making the residual connection degenerate to an identity projection at initialization. Thanks to zero-init LSCs, we can safely fine-tune the entire model from the beginning using the standard diffusion training objective:

In summary, we believe that the strong path dependence of DiTs’ learned representations makes it tough to fundamentally change the behavior of pre-trained DiT models. To be specific, the backbone model has already learnt to transport both noise and target information in the same activations, which might be the optimal behavior when there are no LSCs. Such an ability might conflict with the ability to separately transport the information of two aspects in different paths, making fine-tuning 21 Table 12: Parameter sizes of different LSC designs, compared to the original w/o LSC .LSC Type Parameter Size Percentage w.r.t. w/o LSC w/o LSC 675,129,632 100.0% w/ LSC 709,709,216 105.1% upper LSC 691,089,440 102.4% lower LSC 691,089,440 102.4% skewed LSC 701,729,312 103.9% particularly difficult. Therefore, fundamentally changing how the backbone functions via fine-tuning might require as much training as training from scratch, or even more. Currently, we believe that, to effectively fuse LSCs into a DiT, it is likely that the model needs training from the start, and direct retrofitting is likely to be hard in nature. Nevertheless, it is not impossible that, with a better fine-tuning strategy, we could really add LSCs to a pre-trained DiT model. 

A.9 Computational Cost of LSC 

Computational cost is essential nowadays [42]. We provide the parameter size of different LSC settings in Table 12. We can observe that, parameter-wise, LSCs only introduce a slight overhead, compared to their huge bonus to both feature quality and generation. Additionally, for more practical DiT architectures with more complex designs, this overhead will become even smaller. In our experiments, the running time of different LSC designs is almost identical. However, it is possible that LSCs might conflict with some acceleration techniques based on par-allelism by introducing additional shortcut computation. This may also be one of the reasons why LSCs are not commonly used in today’s DiTs. Nevertheless, there are also methods that utilize LSCs to accelerate diffusion inference, such as caching [6]. By adopting LSCs in future DiT designs, such methods would be enabled for application, which might make LSCs in DiT more appealing efficiency-wise.

A.11 Experimental Details Semantic Correspondence [12]: This task aims to predict the corresponding keypoint in the target image given a keypoint in the source image, where the two keypoints should be semantically similar. The dataset used is SPair-71k [26]. PCK@0.1 img (↑) and PCK@0.1 bbox (↑) are commonly used metrics, following the widely adopted protocol reported in [26]. These two metrics measure the percentage of correctly predicted keypoints, where a predicted keypoint is considered to be correct if it lies within the neighborhood of the corresponding annotation with a radius of 0.1 × max (h, w ). For PCK@0.1 img /PCK@0.1 bbox , h, w denote the dimensions of the entire image/object bounding box, respectively. In Setting#2, we train a small downstream network, which is a two-layer convolutional network. In the other two settings for this task, we directly perform nearest neighbor computation, so no downstream model is required. If training is required, we use a learning rate of 1e-4 for the 23 downstream network and 1e-5 for the diffusion backbone, and the optimizer is AdamW for smaller models and AdamW8bit for larger diffusion models (SD3.5L and Flux). The prompt used for feature extraction is “a highly realistic photo that may contain an aeroplane, a bicycle, a bird, a boat, a bottle, a bus, a car, a cat, a chair, a cow, a dog, a horse, a motorbike, a person, a plant within a pot, a sheep, a train, or a tv monitor”. 

Semantic Segmentation : This task aims to classify pixels into semantic categories [46]. The dataset used is CityScapes [8]. We use mIoU metric, which is the mean over the IoU performance across all semantic classes [12]. For each image, IoU (Intersection over Union, ↑) is defined by #(overlapped pixels between the prediction and the ground truth) / #(union pixels of them). In this setting, we train a relatively large downstream network, which is UPerNet [43]. We use a learning rate of 0.00016 and AdamW optimizer. The prompt used for feature extraction is “An urban street scene with multiple lanes, various buildings, traffic lights, cars in the lanes, and pedestrians, highly realistic.”. 

Label-Scarce Semantic Segmentation [2]: This is a special setting for semantic segmentation, where the labeled training data is very scarce (˜ 30 sample-annotation pairs). The dataset used is collected in [2], and we experiment on its Horse-21 subset, the data of which is sourced from LSUN [47]. In this setting, we train 10 pixel-classification MLP models and aggregate their predictions by choosing the majority. The learning rate is 0.001, and the optimizer is Adam. The prompt used for feature extraction is “a horse in the field running, high quality, photorealistic”. 

Depth Estimation [4]: This task aims to regress the depth value, i.e. , the distance of each pixel from the camera. The dataset used is NYU Depth v2 [31]. We use d1 as the evaluation metric. It computes the percentage of correctly predicted pixels, where a pixel is counted as correct if the difference between its value and the ground truth value does not exceed 25%. In this setting, we add a downstream model consisting of two regular convolutional layers and two 1 ×1 convolution layers, training this model along with the diffusion backbone itself. The diffusion backbone is trained with a 10-times smaller learning rate than the downstream network (1e-4/1e-5), and the optimizer is AdamW8bit. The prompt used for feature extraction is “a photo, focusing on the depth of the scene”.
