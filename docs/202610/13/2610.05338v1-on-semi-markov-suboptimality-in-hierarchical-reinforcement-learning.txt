## ABSTRACT 

Hierarchical reinforcement learning uses temporally extended subtasks for explo-ration, yet committing to their execution can restrict both deployment and policy learning. We identify and separate the resulting execution and policy suboptimality. Task and execution trees distinguish reward objectives from policy choices and decision interruption. A Unified Value Function for HRL and a four-stage Gen-eralized Hierarchical Bellman Equation then support a common analysis of both losses. Under bounded rewards and uniform termination, we establish hierarchical policy and execution improvement results. With the remaining node policies fixed, task-subtree compatibility and node-policy optimality under the original execution mode establish when Markov execution is optimal. The resulting decomposition leads to independent execution choices for behavior, targets, and deployment. We instantiate this principle through execution improvement and one-stage or two-stage policy improvement at arbitrary hierarchy depth. Option-based and goal-conditioned experiments demonstrate complementary gains from changing execution and changing the learning target. Controlled stochastic environments show how these gains depend on stochastic transition strength and spatial structure. This framework makes execution design an explicit component of hierarchical policy optimization. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Temporal abstraction gives hierarchical reinforcement learning a structured way to explore: commit-ting to a useful subtask produces transitions that would be difficult to discover through independent primitive actions (Sutton et al., 1999; Wen et al., 2020). Exploiting the learned hierarchy poses a different question: when should the agent reconsider its choice? After a stochastic transition, another subtask can become more valuable even while the current subtask remains unfinished. This distinction reveals two sources of loss within a fixed task hierarchy. Execution suboptimality 

concerns when a learned policy reselects subtasks; policy suboptimality concerns which execution process its training objective optimizes. Improving deployment execution and optimizing a policy for that execution are separate interventions. Here Semi-Markov Execution (SME) runs each selected subtask until its task termination event, while Markov Execution (ME) permits a new subtask decision at every environment frame. Their difference motivates a theory that treats task objectives and execution as independent design choices. The main contributions of this paper are the following three: 

We first formalize the two sources of suboptimality within a unified value model. Task and execution trees separate nested reward objectives and termination boundaries from policy choices and decision interruption. The Unified Value Function for HRL (UVFH) distinguishes the evaluated task from the node holding decision authority (Figure 1). Its four-stage Generalized Hierarchical Bellman Equation (GHBE) and optimality counterpart, GHBOE, support node-policy and execution improvement with the remaining policies fixed. Building on this decomposition, we decouple behavior, target, and deployment execution. ESIF addresses execution suboptimality by changing how a learned policy is deployed; PSIF addresses policy suboptimality by changing the execution process optimized during learning. Both PSIF procedures apply at arbitrary depth: PSIF-2S changes behavior and target execution together through bottom-up, node-wise stages, while PSIF-1S preserves SME behavior and forms local ME targets. This design retains temporal structure for exploration while optimizing policies and execution for exploitation. 

We then test the resulting frameworks from discrete control to continuous-control extensions. 

Tabular OC provides finite, discounted Markov control with the two-level compatible hierarchy of the theory, allowing us to evaluate ESIF and PSIF in a directly aligned setting. We extend this evaluation to continuous control: goal-conditioned HAC tests ESIF execution improvement, and option-based DAC tests PSIF-style policy improvement through a second-stage comparison. Finally, controlled local-slip and global-teleportation experiments vary both random-transition probability and type to analyze their distinct effects on execution and policy improvement. 

Setting. We use the task-based MDP formalization (S, A, T, R τ , α τ , β τ ): T is the Markov tran-sition kernel, Rτ the reward, ατ the initial-state distribution, and βτ the termination function. A frame is one primitive environment interaction. A node policy ˆπτ selects child tasks; successive child choices induce a primitive-action policy πτ . All comparisons keep the available task hierarchy explicit. Appendices D.1, E.1, and K provide extended preliminaries, execution examples, and the exact GridWorld construction. 

## 2 A UNIFIED THEORY OF TASK OBJECTIVES AND EXECUTION 

The source of the difficulty is that a task has two different roles: it defines an optimization objective, and it induces a transition when called. Its termination function β determines the first role, whereas its decision interruption function ˆβ determines the second. Binding these functions makes the optimization objective and the execution schedule change together. We separate them using task-tree and execution-tree formalization; the complete definitions and proofs appear in Appendix L.

## 3 DECOUPLING BEHAVIOR , TARGETS , AND DEPLOYMENT 

The design principle: three independently specified execution modes. The framework separates three roles: behavior determines which trajectories are collected; the target determines which 5Preprint execution process is optimized; deployment determines how the learned policy tree acts. The task tree, including termination β, fixes the reward objective and exit boundaries; interruption functions ˆβ

specify execution separately for each role. This separation combines temporally extended exploration under SME behavior with frame-by-frame decisions in ME targets and deployment. Applying ME to behavior can interrupt a learning subtask before its reward boundary and leave subsequent actions to another task (Figure 14). ESIF: change deployment execution. The Execution Suboptimality Improvement Framework keeps SME training and deploys the learned policy tree with ME. Its idealized comparison is 

JC → JB: the node policies retain their SME optimization objectives. The Optimal Execution Theorem establishes the ME guarantee under its optimal-node-policy and compatibility conditions. 

PSIF-2S: a bottom-up, node-wise training schedule. The Two-Stage Policy Suboptimality Im-provement Framework assigns two training stages to every task node , at any depth. In Stage 1, a node calls its children with SME and optimizes the corresponding SME objective. When all its children have completed Stage 2 and their policies are fixed, it enters its own Stage 2: it calls the children with 6Preprint ME and optimizes its MDP node policy on-policy. Only after this node policy has approximately converged is it fixed. The lowest task nodes, which call primitive actions directly, start in Stage 2 because their SME and ME objectives coincide. Higher nodes can therefore remain in Stage 1 while lower nodes train in Stage 2 or have already finished. The schedule propagates upwards until every node has completed Stage 2; the resulting tree is deployed with ME. In the OC instance, completing option learning permits the root to begin ME training with fixed option policies and termination functions. 

PSIF-1S: local ME targets under global SME behavior. The One-Stage Policy Suboptimality Improvement Framework calls every task with SME during data collection. When optimizing ˆπn(h),its target execution uses ME inside that task’s subtree , while the execution outside remains SME. GHBE supplies the single-frame bootstrap for this node-specific target; GHBOE specifies the MDP node-policy objective. This construction applies recursively at arbitrary depth. It retains on-policy execution outside the optimized subtree and confines the execution mismatch to its interior. All nodes may continue learning without a manual stage transition, and the whole policy tree uses ME at deployment.

## 5 SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK 

We have separated execution suboptimality from policy suboptimality within a fixed task hierarchy. UVFH and the four-stage Bellman recursion provide a common language for the task objective, policy choice, and decision timing. The resulting improvement criteria and loss decomposition lead to ESIF and two multilevel PSIF procedures, with behavior, target, and deployment execution specified independently. The discrete-control comparisons, continuous-control extensions, and controlled transition sweeps connect this design principle to its empirical effects. Appendix C.2 relates this execution-centered view to existing HRL approaches. 

Future work. We identify three directions that follow from this separation: learning interruption rules alongside node policies, adapting training to parent-induced changes in state–task visitation, and improving off-policy value estimation for the local ME targets of PSIF-1S. The improvement criteria in Section 2 and the learning conditions in Appendix C provide a basis for these extensions. Together, they open a route to execution-aware learning in deeper hierarchies and continuous-control implementations while preserving temporal abstraction for exploration. 9Preprint

An execution node pairs a node policy with a decision interruption function. The policy tree Π contains the former; the interruption tree B contains the latter. This separation permits changing execution without changing the task’s reward accumulation rule. It also distinguishes learning a task decomposition from learning a scheduler. For example, changing an option’s intrinsic termination rule can change the subproblem it must learn, whereas changing only its interruption rule changes when another node can take control. 

Why the extended state contains decision authority. In flat RL, a state value evaluates the process before selecting an action, and an action value evaluates it after an action is selected. In a hierarchy there are intermediate decision points: a parent can have selected a child task while that child has not yet selected its own child. The extended state (s, τ n(i)) identifies all such positions. The conditioning task τn(h) separately determines which reward and exit boundary are evaluated. Hence the two task indices serve different purposes. For a countable task tree with N nodes, the formalization describes 

|S| × N extended-state values for each conditioning task, and |S| × N 2 values across all conditioning tasks. This describes the value objects; it does not require estimating every one with a separate function approximator. 

One environment frame, four value stages. The decision stage follows the policy tree downwards; the interaction stage applies an action and obtains the evaluated task’s reward. The termination stage then either closes that task’s reward accumulation with an exit reward or passes its remaining value to the preparation stage. The preparation stage redistributes decision authority through the interruption tree. These four stages form a closed recursion. They distinguish transitions of task objectives, represented by γ, from transitions of decision authority, represented by ˆγ. In particular, knowing the probability that a task terminates does not by itself specify how frequently a target policy reselects subtasks. 

Why SME behavior can support ME targets. With SME behavior, if an ancestor terminates and decision authority leaves a descendant’s subtree, that descendant’s task also terminates. Its sampled trajectory can therefore extend to its own hierarchical boundary. With ME behavior, authority can leave a descendant while the descendant’s task objective has not terminated. Subsequent actions can then be selected by another task. Using them to update the interrupted task without accounting for the change introduces an off-policy discrepancy. Separating execution roles preserves temporal abstraction for behavior while allowing the target and deployment to exploit ME decisions. PSIF-2S resolves this scheduling issue through two training stages for each task node. Stage 1 uses SME to call children and optimizes the corresponding SME objective. Completion of every child’s Stage 2, with those child policies fixed, permits the parent to enter its own Stage 2. The parent then calls children with ME and optimizes its MDP node policy; it is fixed only after completing this optimization. Lowest task nodes start in Stage 2 because their children are primitive actions and their SME and ME objectives coincide. Different nodes may therefore be in Stage 1, Stage 2, or finished at the same time. It is the caller’s own stage that governs its execution of its children, and the bottom-up progression ends when all task nodes have finished Stage 2. These are node-local training stages, with behavior and target execution matched for the node being optimized. PSIF-1S instead keeps SME behavior throughout the hierarchy and forms a separate target for each updated node: descendants within that node’s subtree are called with ME, while the part outside remains SME. GHBE supplies its single-frame value recursion, and GHBOE specifies its MDP node-policy objective. The behavior can thus preserve the temporal structure useful for exploration without imposing the same execution mode on the learning target or final deployment. This construction concerns an arbitrary node and its descendants, not a fixed pair of upper and lower layers. The OC instantiation below is a two-level example of these general rules. 

Scope of the value guarantees and learning updates. The value-evaluation proof uses bounded rewards and a uniform positive probability of task termination. The optimal-execution result further requires a node policy optimal for its original interruption tree and the stated task-subtree compati-bility condition. The multilevel counterexample identifies why compatibility matters: independent descendant objectives can make a new scheduling opportunity worse for an ancestor. The idealized 18 Preprint framework endpoints compare the specified node-policy optima, without asserting finite-training convergence; partially trained agents are evaluated empirically. In PSIF-2S, SME visitation can leave rarely called subtasks undertrained, and subsequent parent improvement can shift the state–task distribution seen by fixed children. PSIF-1S can also encounter state–option distribution shift and function-approximation error. Multi-frame bootstrapping requires accounting for off-policy data, while single-frame bootstrapping can accumulate approximation error over long horizons. D.3 PROBLEM SETUP 

In this paper, we divide the algorithm into two phases: the training phase and the deployment phase. Training phase: The agent interacts with the environment to learn appropriate policies. 

Deployment phase: The agent needs to call the learned policies in a reasonable manner. We examine the final performance exhibited by the agent when interacting with the environment during the deployment phase while executing policies in a certain manner. 3The main motivation for this setup is that during training, the agent needs to explore the environment, and policy execution involves an exploration mechanism. However, during deployment, more emphasis should be placed on the optimal performance that the learned policies can achieve in terms of exploitation. We find that this exploration-exploitation tradeoff takes on a new form in HRL.

This paper also proposes a Generalized SMDP Suboptimality Theorem under more-than-two-level and arbitrary execution modes, which constitutes the core theoretical contribution of this work. We provide detailed formulations, descriptions and proofs in the Section L, where the narrow SMDP suboptimality theory is also proven as a special case. 

Proof Sketch for Theorem F.1. Conceptual Clarification: This paper notes that each task τ has two termination functions with different roles. One controls the optimization objective of ˆπτ , namely the 

termination function βτ introduced in Section D.2. The other controls the duration of τ ’s transitions when it is called as a subtask during interaction, defined as the interrupt function ˆβτ . In this context, we observe that when using ME, ˆβτ ≡ 1, while when using SME, ˆβτ ≡ βτ . In our proof, we further introduce the task tree and the execution tree to decouple the binding of ˆβτ and βτ in existing HRL, which respectively describe the complex task optimization objectives and task execution modes across multiple hierarchical levels. In simple terms, for each τ , the task tree corresponds to the task decomposition, which determines the termination conditions of τ as well as the boundary cases when returning to different upper-level tasks. The execution tree, on the other hand, acts as the policy scheduler, specifying how τ calls its lower-level descendant tasks. By defining these two types of trees, we can precisely formalize the complex situations in more-than-two-level hierarchical settings where the termination or interruption of any upper-level task of τ will cause τ itself to be terminated or interrupted. Unified Formalization: The value computation under different execution modes cannot be directly described by the Bellman equation, since action selection depends on both the current frame’s state and the task. At each frame during interaction, the hierarchical level of a task is jointly influenced by ˆπ and ˆβ. For instance, in a two-level HRL, under ME, decisions are made from ˆπhi downward at every frame, whereas under SME, downward decisions from ˆπhi occur only when a subtask ends, and the remaining frames are decided by ˆπlo . To address this, we describe the generalized interaction process of HRL using four stages: Preparation stage: Determine which task makes downward decisions based on ˆβ. Decision stage: The task determined in the previous stage makes downward decisions according to ˆπ until an action a is selected. Interaction stage: Each task receives rewards according to the agent’s transitions. Termination stage: Tasks in execution determine termination based on β, including boundary conditions for termination. Based on four stages, we propose a Unified Value Function for HRL(UVFH) . Using UVFH, we establish the 

Generalized Hierarchical Bellman Equation (GHBE) and the Generalized Hierarchical Bellman Optimality Equation (GHBOE) , which describe the dynamic value transitions across the four stages and serve as the foundation for subsequent theoretical analysis. Here "generalized" refers to arbitrary execution modes and task hierarchy configurations. Theoretical Proofs: We prove that the iterative operators of the two equations above are Contraction Mappings, thereby providing theoretical guarantees for the convergence of their solutions. On this basis, we further prove the 

Generalized Hierarchical Policy Improvement Theorem , which ensures that under the ME and SME execution settings, the optimal node policies induced by the GHBOE correspond to the MDP optimal node policies and the SMDP optimal node policies, respectively. Consequently, we establish that JA ≥ JB and JC ≥ JD . Next, we prove the Hierarchical Execution Improvement Theorem 

for execution modes controlled by ˆβ. Using this theorem, together with certain subtask conditions (satisfied by two-level HRL), we prove the Optimal Execution Theorem . This theorem guarantees that, under some conditions, the expected return of any execution mode is no greater than that under ME, i.e., JB ≥ JC . In particular, we also provide counterexamples for multi-level HRL settings 23 Preprint where the conditions are not satisfied for descendant task, demonstrating that the optimality of ME is not evident. F.2 LIMITATIONS OF ME Unlike SME, under ME, ˆβ and β are set differently, which may result in frequent interruptions of a task by higher-level tasks during its execution. During task optimization, deviations between the target policy and the behavior policy may occur, as indicated by the red marks in Figure 14. This makes it difficult for the boundary conditions controlled by β at task termination to be properly back-propagated along the interaction trajectory during dynamic programming. Therefore, if the agent directly interacts with the environment using ME during training, the node policies of all subtasks will face severe off-policy issues, making efficient training difficult. To ensure stable sampling and learnability of lower-level subtasks during training, the behavior policy should still use SME. F.3 THE EXPLORATION –EXPLOITATION TRADEOFF INDUCED BY EXECUTION MODES 

Given that we have already adopted a hierarchical architecture, what extra suboptimality is introduced purely by the SME, and how can we remove it by using a more suitable execution mode? Based on the theoretical analysis above, the problem can be reduced to an exploration-exploitation tradeoff caused by the execution mode in HRL. Overall, the common HRL design in which both target and behavior policies use SME is exactly the fundamental design flaw. During the training phase: 

Directly using ME to learn the MDP-optimal policy set results in low subtask sampling efficiency, leading to an exploration problem; directly using SME to learn the SMDP-optimal policy set results in policy suboptimality, leading to an exploitation problem. During the deployment phase: The optimal execution mode, ME, should be used to ensure exploitation. Therefore, a reasonable HRL algorithm should balance exploration and exploitation, interacting with the environment via SME to generate data while learning the MDP-optimal policy set in some way. 24 Preprint ME SME

## G FRAMEWORK IMPLEMENTATIONS 

Based on Section F, this paper designs three theoretically guaranteed frameworks for improving HRL algorithms by separating the task termination function from the interruption functions and independently specifying the execution modes of the behavior policy , target policy , and deployment policy : the Execution Suboptimality Improvement Framework (ESIF) , the Two-Stage Policy Subopti-mality Improvement Framework (PSIF-2S) , and the One-Stage Policy Suboptimality Improvement Framework (PSIF-1S) . Existing HRL algorithms can be improved through these frameworks to balance the exploration-exploitation tradeoff and mitigate the SMDP suboptimality of the original algorithms to varying degrees. G.1 FRAMEWORKS 

ESIF: This framework does not modify the training process of the algorithm and directly uses the original algorithm to learn π− (i.e., by sampling data through SME to solve the SMDP-optimal policy set). During the deployment phase, using ME can achieve the expected return JB. This framework is analogous to using ϵ-greedy SARSA (Rummery & Niranjan, 1994) to train the agent in RL, followed by greedy execution during deployment. PSIF-2S: Each task node in the task hierarchy has two training stages. Stage 1: A task node calls its children using SME and trains its node policy for the corresponding SME objective. Stage 2: Once every child has completed its own Stage 2 and its policy is fixed, the parent calls its children using ME and trains its MDP node policy. The parent policy is fixed only after this second-stage optimization has approximately converged. The lowest trainable task nodes, whose children are primitive actions, start in Stage 2 because their SME and ME objectives coincide; all other task nodes start in Stage 1. During training, each unfinished node solves the GHBOE for its current node-local execution mode. Stage transitions propagate from the bottom upwards, and nodes at different depths may be in different stages simultaneously. Training ends when every task node has completed Stage 2. ME deployment then corresponds to JA in the idealized comparison. Here stage numbers describe a node’s own training and its calls to children, consistently with the general framework in Section 3. PSIF-1S: Each task node is called using SME during data collection. When a particular node policy is optimized, its target calls descendants within its subtree using ME, while execution outside that subtree remains SME. GHBE supplies its single-frame bootstrap and GHBOE its MDP node-policy objective ˆπ∗ 

> τ

. The same construction applies at every depth. During deployment, the whole policy tree uses ME, corresponding to JA in the idealized comparison. This is analogous to using Q-Learning (Watkins & Dayan, 1992) to train the agent in RL, followed by greedy execution during deployment. Extending SMDP suboptimality improvement frameworks to policy-gradient-based HRL algorithms is a promising direction for future research.

## L FORMALIZATION AND THEORETICAL PROOF 

L.1 TASK TREE AND EXECUTION TREE 

This work observes that each task τ has two distinct termination functions. One controls the optimization objective of ˆπτ , i.e., the termination function βτ defined in Section D.2. The other controls the duration for which τ executes when it is called as a subtask during interaction, and is defined as the interrupt function ˆβτ . In HRL, both for the execution of the behavioral policy and the target policy, the number of frames a subtask is actually executed is simply tied to the subtask’s own duration, i.e., ˆβτ ≡ βτ . From the analysis in Section K, we recognize that this is unreasonable. To address this, we propose the task tree and execution tree , which independently describe, respectively, the complex task optimization objectives and task calling mechanisms in multi-level hierarchies. The most crucial aspect is the precise formalization and proper design of hierarchical boundary conditions when a task terminates. This paper introduces the concept of an execution tree to describe how an agent in HRL executes its hierarchical policies to interact with the environment. We observe a fundamental difference between 34 Preprint HRL and standard RL: during the execution process of HRL, in addition to each task node having its own node policy function, an extra execution mechanism is required to properly organize and call these node policies at every frame. This is the key point discussed in Section E.1 of the main text. 

The differences in execution mechanisms can significantly affect the agent’s expected return, its exploration capability, and the data efficiency of each node policy during the learning process. 

Therefore, the choice of execution mechanisms for the agent’s behavior policy during training, the target policy during training, and the policy during deployment are all crucial research questions that have been largely overlooked.

en(l). 2) Decision phase of each frame: The execution node holding the decision authority selects a subtask according to its node policy function and continuously passes the decision authority to the execution node corresponding to the selected subtask. If there is no corresponding execution node (e.g., for actions), the authority is not transferred. This process continues until an action is selected and executed. At this point, we have decoupled the original termination function β under the definitions of the task tree and execution tree: β now controls the intrinsic optimization objective of the task, while ˆβ controls the calling of node policies during execution. In the task tree, the optimal subtask set for facilitating task learning can be found by optimizing β and R. In existing algorithms, some β functions are fixed; for example, in the Goal-based framework, each task (goal) has a fixed 

β. Others are learnable; for instance, in the OC method, each task (option) has a β optimized via the termination gradient theorem. In the execution tree, the optimization of ˆβ must balance the exploration and exploitation of task τn(h) during both behavior policy and target policy calling. On one hand, it should consider interacting with the environment under a certain execution mode to achieve high sampling efficiency (exploration). On the other hand, it should consider planning an optimal policy tree under the given execution mode using existing data to ensure maximal expected return (exploitation) 11 . N.1 ENVIRONMENT SETTINGS 

Randomized version of AntReacher. A continuous task simulated in MuJoCo aims at controlling an ant robot to achieve a random goal on the map. When deploying a policy, an additional random transition event is engaged: For every 10 timestep, the ant will have a random state transition on the whole map with the probability of 10% , only involving coordinates x and y.

Fourrooms. A GridWorld environment of four rooms. The cells of the grid correspond to the states of the environment. The agent must start from a random starting cell and reach a fixed-end cell. In any state, the agent is feasible to perform one of four actions, up, down, lef t, right . And there is a random transition after the action is selected. With the probability of 23 , the agent moves one cell in the chosen direction. With the probability of 13 , the agent moves one cell in one of the other three directions, each with the chance of 19 . In either case, if the movement takes the agent onto a wall, the agent remains in the same cell.

52 Preprint N.2 ALGORITHMS USED 

HAC. This is a state-of-the-art goal-based HRL algorithm. For the goal-based HRL algorithms, the higher level of the hierarchy interacts with an SMDP induced by the environment dynamics and the behavior of the lower level. During training, the lower-level policy constantly changes with updates, making the higher-level interaction non-stationary. HAC proposed the hindsight action relabeling method to alleviate this problem for static environments by ‘pretending’ that the lower-level policy has already been optimal. In addition, HAC also proposed hindsight goal transitions and subgoal testing to accelerate the learning of sparse-reward tasks. 

OC. This is a classic option-based HRL algorithm. This algorithm learns the entire hierarchical policy tree in an end-to-end manner. The higher-level policy and all the options (i.e., intra-option policies and termination functions) are trained using policy gradient derived using the root task rewards (i.e., the original environmental reward). A limitation of OC is that it requires predefining the number of options. The policy gradients are heavily dependent on the root task reward.
