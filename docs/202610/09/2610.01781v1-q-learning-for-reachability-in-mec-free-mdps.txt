# Q-Learning for Reachability in MEC-Free MDPs 

Lu-Chin Chang, Suguman Bansal 

Georgia Institute of Technology luchin_chang@gatech.edu, suguman@gatech.edu 

Abstract 

Reinforcement learning (RL) for reachability specifications is fundamental to sequential decision-making. Prior work estab-lishes asymptotic convergence to optimal policies, but only through model-based methods that must explicitly estimate the transition probabilities of the underlying Markov Deci-sion Process (MDP). We present Quasar, the first model-free 

algorithm with asymptotic guarantees for reachability on the fragment of MDPs free of non-terminal maximal end com-ponents (MECs) , a building block to which every MDP re-duces by the standard MEC quotient. Our algorithm follows the classical Q-learning approach, using temporal-difference updates to converge to an optimal policy without ever learn-ing the transition probabilities. The resulting learner reduces the memory footprint from the O(|S|2|A|) that model-based methods require to O(|S|| A|). On the standardized Quanti-tative Verification Benchmark Set, our algorithm converges to the optimal policy with orders of magnitude fewer samples than the previous model-based state-of-the-art. Together these results are a concrete step toward the practical deployment of reachability learning and, with it, of specification-guided RL. 

1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) (Sutton and Barto 2018) is the standard framework for sequential decision-making in un-known Markov decision processes (MDPs), and cast as re-ward maximization it admits strong theoretical guarantees. The strongest is asymptotic convergence (Watkins and Dayan 1992), under which the learned policies are optimal in the limit almost surely, and it is on such guarantees that the prac-tical success of RL rests, from robotic locomotion (Collins et al. 2005) and dexterous manipulation (Andrychowicz et al. 2020) to visuomotor control (Levine et al. 2016). In this work we seek to carry this guarantee into the setting of formal specifications , where an agent must satisfy a tempo-ral objective rather than maximize a hand-tuned reward (Alur et al. 2026). Specifications in linear temporal logic (LTL) and related ω-regular formalisms express rich behaviours, such as safety and liveness, that are awkward to encode as re-wards (Hahn et al. 2019). It is worth noting that an asymptotic guarantee is close to the best one can hope for here, since the finite-sample (PAC) guarantees available for reward-based RL largely fail for specifications. In particular, general LTL objectives are not PAC-learnable (Yang, Littman, and Carbin 2022; Alur et al. 2022), and the PAC guarantees for restricted classes rest on an unobservable quantity of the MDP, such as the smallest transition probability (Ashok, Křetínský, and Weininger 2019; Perez, Somenzi, and Trivedi 2024; Svo-boda, Bansal, and Chatterjee 2024). Among all specifications, reachability is the most fun-damental, the primitive from which the richer LTL and ω-regular objectives are built, so that an algorithm learning it with a given guarantee immediately yields one for that entire class (Section 2.3). This makes reachability the linchpin of specification-guided RL, and it is the problem we take up. For reachability, asymptotic guarantees have been established only recently, and only by model-based 

means (Palasamudram et al. 2026b; Le et al. 2024). Build-ing a model is a genuine hindrance at scale. A model-based learner stores a transition estimate for every state-action-successor triple, at O(|S| 2|A| ) memory, and must refine it to uniform accuracy before planning, so its sample and time costs track the model size rather than the difficulty of the objective. On the large product MDPs that specifications in-duce, both are prohibitive. One naturally wonders whether reachability admits a model-free learner that still converges asymptotically. We answer this affirmatively. We present Quasar, the first model-free algorithm with asymptotic guarantees for reach-ability. We operate on the no-EC fragment of MDPs, those with no maximal end component (MEC) among the non-terminal states, a standard structural condition that prevents the agent from postponing termination forever. Every MDP reduces to the no-EC fragment by the standard MEC quo-tient (de Alfaro 1997), so the fragment is a building block for the general MDP setting rather than a restriction of it. Quasar is deliberately in the style of Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan 1992). It keeps a single O(|S| |A| ) table of value estimates, refines them from sampled transitions, and never forms a model. The difficulty lies not in the algorithm but in its analysis. Classical Q-learning converges because a dis-count factor γ ∈ (0 , 1) makes the Bellman operator a con-traction, and standard discounted-sum RL rewards a trajec-tory that first reaches the target at step k with γk. Reach-ability is precisely the undiscounted case γ = 1 of this scheme, where the contraction that drives Q-learning to con-verge disappears, so the classical proof does not carry over. The natural remedy, recasting reachability as some other discounted-sum reward, is ruled out by Alur et al. (Alur et al. arXiv:2610.01781v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 2022). No optimality-preserving reduction from reachability to discounted-sum rewards exists, and the gap must therefore be closed directly. We close it by replacing the missing discount factor with a quantity intrinsic to the MDP, the expected time to termi-nation. Measuring convergence in a norm weighted by this hitting time, we show that the undiscounted Bellman opera-tor contracts after all, with the expected hitting time playing exactly the role that the discount factor plays in the clas-sical analysis. This weight is a property of the MDP, not a parameter the learner must know or tune, so our guaran-tee rests on no numerical quantity of the MDP, only on the structural no-EC assumption. A stochastic-approximation ar-gument then lifts the contraction from the exact operator to the sampled updates, yielding convergence with probability one. The hitting-time weight may recall the stochastic short-est path problem, but the two are distinct and neither reduces to the other, so its contraction machinery does not apply off the shelf. We return to this in Section 2.3. These guarantees translate into practice. We evaluate two versions of Quasar, the online Quasar-Naive and a backward-replay variant Quasar-BR that preserves the guar-antee (Theorem 4). On the standardized Quantitative Verifi-cation Benchmark Set (Hartmanns et al. 2019), Quasar-BR converges to the optimal value on every benchmark, using or-ders of magnitude fewer samples than the model-based state of the art. The key contribution of this work is to establish the fol-lowing two results, formalized as Theorem 2 and Theorem 1. 1. On no-EC MDPs, the iterates of Quasar converge to the optimal reachability values almost surely (Theorem 2). It is worth emphasizing that the sole hypothesis is structural (Assumption 1), and the guarantee is conditional on no numerical parameter of the MDP. 2. Quasar runs in O(|S| |A| ) space (Theorem 1), aquadratic improvement over the O(|S| 2|A| ) that model-based methods spend on stored transition estimates. To the best of our knowledge, Quasar is the first algorithm to attain an asymptotic guarantee for reachability without either estimating a model or reducing the objective to rewards.

2.2 Problem Setup 

We aim to learn reachability objectives with asymptotic guar-antees (Definition 1) in a model-free way, i.e., from simulator access alone without building an explicit model of P . Prior algorithms with asymptotic guarantees for reachability are 

model-based (Palasamudram et al. 2026b; Le et al. 2024), explicitly estimating P and then computing the optimal pol-icy by classical optimization (de Alfaro 1997). In this work we restrict our attention to the no-EC fragment of MDPs, which we define through end components. 

Definition 2 (End Component and MEC) . An end compo-nent (EC) is a non-empty set C ⊆ S × A that is (i) closed ,i.e., if (s, a ) ∈ C and P (s′ | s, a ) > 0, then (s′, a ′) ∈ C

for some a′ ∈ A (s′), and (ii) strongly connected , i.e., the subgraph it induces links any two of its states via transi-tions within C. A maximal end component (MEC) is an EC contained in no larger EC. 

Intuitively, an MEC is a region in which the agent can remain forever. We exclude such regions from among the non-terminal states. Every MDP reduces to a no-EC instance by collapsing each maximal end component to a single state, the standard MEC quotient (de Alfaro 1997), so the no-EC fragment is a building block for the general MDP setting rather than a restriction of it. 

Assumption 1 (No-EC) . No end component contains a pair 

(s, a ) with s ∈ S nt . Equivalently, no MEC contains a non-terminal state. 

This frames the central question of the paper. Can reach-ability objectives on no-EC MDPs be learnt with asymptotic guarantees in a model-free manner? We answer this affirma-tively and constructively. Section 3 presents the algorithm, and Section 4 establishes its guarantee (Theorem 2). 

2.3 Related Work 

Every ω-regular specification (Thomas 1990), and hence every LTL specification (Pnueli 1977), reduces to optimal reachability, a reduction standard for both known (Baier and Katoen 2008) and unknown (Sickert et al. 2016; Hahn et al. 2020b,a) MDPs and simulable for reinforcement learn-ing (Hahn et al. 2020b). It passes through a canonical automa-ton form (Safra 1988) and a probability-independent graph analysis (Courcoubetis and Yannakakis 1995; Chatterjee and Henzinger 2011, 2014), and finite-horizon (LTL f ) goals re-duce likewise (Giacomo and Vardi 2013). Any advance in learning reachability therefore transfers to the full class, plac-ing reachability at the centre of specification-guided learning. The only prior algorithms with asymptotic guarantees, Palasamudram et al. (Palasamudram et al. 2026b) for reach-ability and Le et al. (Le et al. 2024) for full LTL, both estimate a model of the dynamics and reduce the objective to average reward. Quasar is the first model-free alternative, matching their guarantee with a single O(|S| |A| ) table in place of the 

O(|S| 2|A| ) a model demands. A separate line of model-free algorithms targets ω-regular objectives directly (Hahn et al. 2019, 2020b,a), translating the specification into a reward on an enlarged state space. That translation is faithful only past a threshold that itself depends on the transition probabilities the learner is trying to avoid estimating, so no fixed choice can be certified in advance. Quasar asks for no such parameter, resting only on the no-EC assumption (Assumption 1). Our hitting-time weight may recall the stochastic short-est path (SSP) problem, whose analysis also turns on a contraction and an asynchronous stochastic-approximation lemma (Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1991; Tsitsiklis 1994), yet the resemblance is confined to the machinery. SSP seeks a proper policy minimizing an additive path cost to a target reached with certainty, whereas reachability maximizes a target-reaching probability that need not equal one and ig-nores path length. Since reachability grants no proper policy in general, the SSP contraction does not transfer, and neither problem reduces to the other (Sup. mat. D).

Backward replay. Run online, Algorithm 1 is the version we call Quasar-Naive and analyze below. A second version, Quasar-BR, buffers a whole episode and then applies the very same updates in reverse order, from the terminal state back to the start (Lin 1992). The reversal changes neither which updates are made nor their sizes, only the order in which they land, so one expects it to reach the same values. The expectation is not automatic, since a reversed update reads entries that were rewritten later in the same episode, and we therefore prove separately that Quasar-BR attains the guarantee of Theorem 2 (Theorem 4, Sup. mat. C). Reversal earns this extra proof by making each episode count for far more. With the per-pair step size α = 1 /N ,a pair’s first update takes α = 1 and simply overwrites its estimate with the successor’s value, so a single backward sweep carries the terminal reward from a target all the way to

4 Proof of Convergence 

We establish that the iterates of Quasar-Naive (Algo-rithm 1) converge to Q∗ almost surely (Theorem 2), building the argument in stages. A learner that knew P could iterate the exact right-hand side of (4). We first cast this as fixed-point iteration of an operator H whose unique fixed point is Q∗, and show H is a contraction toward Q∗ in a weighted norm (Property 2), so the exact iteration converges geometrically. Our learner does not know P : it replaces the exact right-hand side by a single sampled successor (Line 12), making each update one exact step of H plus a mean-zero perturbation. A classical stochastic-approximation result (Lemma 1) shows the per-turbation washes out, provided every pair is visited infinitely often (Property 3) and the step sizes obey the Robbins–Monro rate (Assumption 2). This decomposition of a deterministic contraction perturbed by mean-zero noise is the classical route for discounted Q-learning, but it is closed to reacha-bility. A known factor γ < 1 makes the discounted operator contract in the supremum norm, whereas reachability is the undiscounted case γ = 1 where it does not contract at all, and the stochastic shortest path analysis does not transfer ei-ther, as its contraction presumes a proper policy reaching the target with probability one that reachability does not grant. Our contribution is to recover the contraction from within the MDP, with the expected hitting time supplying the role a discount factor plays classically. Full proofs are in the sup. mat.

Corollary 3. Under Assumptions 1 and 2, Algorithm 1 con-verges asymptotically in the sense of Definition 1: V πt (i0) →

V ∗(i0) almost surely. In fact the convergence is eventually exact, i.e., almost surely V πt (i0) = V ∗(i0) for all large t.

The argument has two steps—greediness eventually se-lects only Q∗-optimal actions, and every Q∗-greedy policy is optimal (again via Assumption 1)—and is given in full in the sup. mat. We stress that Corollary 3 concerns the greedy policies 

{πt}, not the ϵ-greedy behavior policy that Algorithm 1 fol-lows while learning. The distinction is material: with the fixed ϵ > 0 of Line 1, the behavior policy plays a uniformly random action with probability ϵ forever, so its value stays bounded away from V ∗(i0) and Definition 1 would fail for it. It is the estimates, and the policies they induce, that converge. 

5 Experiments 

We ask whether the model-free route yields a practical algo-rithm and how it compares against the model-based state of the art. Our evaluation answers the first affirmatively and, on the second, finds a substantial margin in our favor. 

5.1 Implementation Details 

We evaluate two versions of Quasar, Quasar-Naive and Quasar-BR. We implement both versions in Python with NumPy. Each maintains a single dense Q-table and so oc-cupies O(|S| |A| ) memory. We use the per-pair step size 

αt = 1 /N (s, a ), i.e., the inverse visit count, and the uniform exploration policy ϵ = 1 , i.e., we sample actions uniformly from A(s). An episode ends on reaching Sterm , or is truncated after H = 10 |S \ S G | steps. 

5.2 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate on all nine models from the standardized Quan-titative Verification Benchmark Set (Hartmanns et al. 2019) (Table 1). Three of the nine (csma, pacman, zeroconf) con-tain non-terminal MECs and hence fall outside Assump-tion 1. For these, marked † throughout, we first apply the standard MEC-quotient procedure (de Alfaro 1997) to ob-tain an MEC-free MDP. To measure progress, we precompute V ∗(i0) on every benchmark and record the initial-state value error , which Quasar reports as v-err i0 = |Vt(i0) − V ∗(i0)| and Staged-PAC as the error of its bound midpoint, | 12 (L+U ) − V ∗(i0)|.We report this error against the number of samples consumed. We compare against the prior algorithm with an asymp-totic guarantee for reachability Staged-PAC (Palasamu-dram et al. 2026b). Comparison to all other methods with-out such a guarantee offers no meaningful yardstick for a convergence result. Staged-PAC grows a partial model by simulation and runs bounded value iteration with bounds 

L(i0) ≤ V ∗(i0) ≤ U (i0), and we run their open-source implementation under their per-benchmark hyperparameter configurations (Palasamudram et al. 2026a). We run each algorithm on each benchmark with 5 random seeds under a 36 h wall-clock timeout, one task per run on a cluster with 2 CPU cores and 16 GB RAM each. Staged-PAC uses its authors’ staged stopping rule until the timeout. We stop a Quasar run once q-err max < 10 −3 has held for three consecutive checkpoints on a log-spaced grid. 

5.3 Results 

Table 1 and Figure 1 report the final error of each learner and the number of samples it needs to sustain the initial-state error below 10 −2.Quasar-BR converges on all nine benchmarks within the 

36 h budget, and its margin over the model-based state of the art is decisive. On the six instances with V ∗(i0) = 1 

it reaches numerically exact values, error at most 2×10 −11 ,within 5 to 215 samples, fewer updates than a single return path to the goal, and its final error is below Staged-PAC’s on every benchmark under identical budgets. In particular, Staged-PAC sustains error below 10 −2 on only two of the nine, and even there it spends 2.8×10 7 samples on ij.3 and 

9.3×10 8 on rabin.3 where Quasar-BR needs 5 and 7. On the remaining seven it halts at the timeout with error between 

2×10 −2 and 1.2×10 −1 and its bounds still open (Figure 1). This understates the gap on csma †, whose initial bounds 

[0 , 1] have midpoint 0.5 = V ∗(i0), so the baseline’s reported error starts near zero at full interval width and our reported speedup is, if anything, conservative. The two versions of Quasar part ways as the goal depth 

d grows. Quasar-Naive keeps pace on the shallow bench-marks but fails outright on the deepest, leaving the error at 1.0 on firewire ( d = 76 ) after 2.8×10 8 samples even though every episode reaches the goal, where Quasar-BR reaches error 0 within 215 . The update ordering is thus not a minor optimization but the difference between converging and not, and every Quasar entry in Table 1 is accordingly a Quasar-BR entry. A single mechanism accounts for both gaps. Under the online 1/N schedule each Q(s, a ) averages every target it has seen, so a corrected value advances one state per episode and its propagation time is exponential in d, whereas a re-verse sweep takes α = 1 on a first visit and runs exact value iteration along the sampled path in one pass. That same sweep costs essentially one goal-reaching episode, whereas Staged-PAC must first estimate the model to uniform accu-racy at a cost scaling with the model size and with 1/p min .Finally, these findings are not an artifact of averaging over seeds. Sup. mat. A disaggregates them, and every finding holds on every seed.

This is substantial progress towards making specification-guided RL practical. C Backward-Replay Proof 

Section 3 introduced Quasar-BR, which buffers an episode and applies the identical Line-12 updates in reverse chrono-logical order once the episode ends. This sup. mat. proves that the guarantee of Theorem 2 extends to this variant. It is worth being precise about why a separate argument is needed. The noise term of (6) is defined against the table the update reads, and the proof of Theorem 2 rests on that table being determined by the past of the sampled successor, so that E[nt | st, a t] = 0 . Under backward replay this fails: when the k-th transition of an episode is replayed, the table already contains the replayed updates of the chronologically 

later transitions, so the target consulted at time k is a function of draws made after sk+1 . The conditioning that pins down the table also pins down the sample it is supposed to predict, and no choice of filtration restores the martingale property of the raw noise. Two elementary observations repair the argument. First, every replayed target can be re-anchored at the table as it stood at the episode boundary; the re-anchored noise is a mar-tingale difference with respect to the ordinary sampling filtra-tion, and the anchoring error—the movement of the bootstrap row during the flush—is bounded by the total step size Ae the episode spends. Second, the reversal itself merely reorders a composition of affine maps, and reordering perturbs the composite only at second order in the step sizes, while every legitimate term enters at first order. Both prices vanish, since 

Ae → 0 almost surely, and the ceiling induction of Theo-rem 2 then goes through stepped at episode boundaries, with the deterministic envelope inflated by a vanishing amount. We first fix the analyzed object and the notation, then prove the four lemmas the induction consumes, and finally run the induction.

D Reachability versus Stochastic Shortest Path 

We give two small examples that separate reachability from the stochastic shortest path (SSP) problem. The first is a reachability instance that is not an SSP instance at all. The second shows that even where both are defined, their optima disagree. Consider the MDP with a single non-terminal state i0 and two terminal states, a target g ∈ S G and a non-target sink d ∈S¬G . Two actions are enabled at i0 (Figure 4). Action a moves to g with probability 12 and to d with probability 12 , and action

SSP is defined only when some policy is proper , that is, reaches the target with probability one, whereas here every policy sends positive mass to the sink d from which g is unreachable. Reachability remains well posed precisely because it asks for a probability rather than a path, and that probability 34 is the content the SSP formulation cannot express.
