# Adapting to Changes in Agent Behavior via Finite-Depth Policy Sensitivity 

Lan Shi, Daigo Shishika, and Xuan Wang 

Abstract — Adapting a reinforcement learning policy to changes in another agent’s behavior typically requires a large amount of new interaction data. Policy sensitivity provides a first-order prediction of how a locally optimal policy changes with a behavioral parameter, but its computation requires second-order derivatives whose effects propagate across future interactions. We develop a finite-depth framework to estimate this sensitivity by approximating the policy Hessian and mixed derivative using information from a reference environment. The method features an adjustable propagation depth which determines where derivative propagation along the trajectory is truncated. We characterize the derivative contributions omitted by finite-depth propagation and derive truncation-error bounds for the approximated derivatives and resulting policy sensi-tivity. The bounds are nonincreasing with propagation depth and vanish at full-horizon propagation. Using a belief-driven pursuit–evasion game as a validation scenario, the proposed method generally achieves lower derivative-estimation errors as the propagation depth increases and outperforms the baseline methods in both estimation accuracy and policy adaptation. The sensitivity-based initialization improves zero-shot return over direct transfer, and also shows advantages for the subsequent fine-tuning in the target environment. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement-learning policies deployed in interactive environments often need to operate with agents exhibiting different behaviors [1]. For example, an autonomous vehicle may encounter drivers with different levels of aggressive-ness, while a robotic agent may interact with teammates or opponents following different decision strategies. Because the behavior of an interactive agent may be unknown before deployment, a policy optimized for one behavior may not perform well against another. On the other hand, retraining a policy from scratch for every newly encountered agent can be costly. One approach to efficient policy adaptation is sensitivity-based adaptation, which characterizes how a locally optimal policy changes with respect to a problem pa-rameter. The resulting sensitivity can be used in a first-order prediction to adapt a previously learned policy to a nearby target environment without learning from scratch. However, computing this sensitivity generally requires higher-order derivatives of the expected payoff. In our setting, rather than directly parameterizing physical dynamics or reward [2], [3], the environmental variation is induced by a change in another decision-making agent’s policy. The behavioral parameter therefore affects the transi-tion dynamics experienced by the learning agent through the interactive agent’s decisions. Because this effect propagates through subsequent states and the coupled decisions of both agents, a change in the interactive agent can influence the

Literature review : Existing approaches to policy adaptation under environmental variation include robust reinforcement learning and domain randomization, which train a common policy across a prescribed range of environments or dis-turbances [4]–[6], and meta-reinforcement learning, which learns an adaptation mechanism from a distribution of related tasks [7], [8]. These approaches rely on training in multiple environments or task variations. In contrast, we consider a policy optimized for a single reference environment and how to adapt it to a particular change in the interactive agent. Our formulation is closely related to sensitivity analysis and implicit differentiation for parameterized optimization [9], [10]. Differentiable optimization and bilevel-learning methods characterize how an optimizer varies with problem parameters by differentiating its optimality conditions [11]– [13]. Related ideas have also been studied in reinforcement learning. For example, sensitivity arguments have been used to analyze policy-gradient methods [2], [14], while implicit differentiation has been applied in model-based reinforce-ment learning to differentiate Bellman-optimal value func-tions with respect to model parameters [15]. Differentiation-based ideas also appear in meta-gradient reinforcement learning, where meta-parameters for training are optimized according to their effect on subsequent performance [16], [17]. In our setting, implicit differentiation characterizes how a locally optimal policy changes with the behavioral parameter of the interactive agent. Computing this policy sensitivity requires both the policy Hessian and the mixed derivative between the learner’s policy parameter and the interactive agent’s behavioral pa-rameter. Prior work in reinforcement learning has primarily studied the computation or approximation of policy Hes-sians for policy optimization [18]. Policy Hessians can be arXiv:2610.07475v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 expressed in terms of trajectory-likelihood derivatives and estimated from sampled trajectories [19]. Higher-order score-function estimators, including DiCE-type methods, provide another trajectory-based route to estimating higher-order derivatives [20]. The work [21] also shows that Hessian– vector products can be estimated from trajectories with-out explicitly forming the full matrix. Alternatively, when a differentiable transition model is available, second-order derivatives can be obtained by differentiating through the model and propagating derivative information along the state-transition dynamics [13]. These approaches provide two complementary routes: trajectory-based estimation avoids full model propagation but can require many samples, whereas model-based differentiation propagates derivative information through the model but may require propagation over the full horizon. This motivates a finite-depth approxi-mation that combines reference-environment sampling with local model-based derivative propagation. While temporal truncation has also been used in approximate dynamic programming to reduce planning-horizon computation [22], our method retains the boundary value while truncating its parameter-derivative propagation. 

Contributions of this paper include: (i) Finite-depth sen-sitivity estimation. By combining state sampling with local state-transition propagation, we develop a Bellman-based derivative decomposition and a finite-depth approximation of the policy Hessian and mixed derivative. An adjustable derivative-propagation depth T controls how far parameter-derivative effects are propagated through future interactions. (ii) Truncation-error guarantees. We characterize the deriva-tive contributions omitted by finite-depth propagation and derive error bounds for the approximated derivatives and resulting policy sensitivity. The bounds quantify the depth– accuracy tradeoff. They are nonincreasing with propagation depth, and vanish at full-horizon propagation. (iii) Validation. 

We evaluate the proposed framework in a representative ex-ample (a pursuit-evasion game with a behavior-parameterized defender) through numerical studies, including sensitivity-estimation accuracy and adaptation performance. II. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

A. Parameterized Interactive-Agent MDP 

We consider a family of environments with two interactive agents: a learning agent, denoted by L, and an interactive agent, denoted by O. The learning agent follows a policy 

πθ (· | s), parameterized by θ ∈ Rdθ , where θ contains the trainable policy parameters. The interactive agent follows a policy ψ(·; κ) characterized by a behavioral parameter 

κ ∈ Rdκ . Different values of κ represent different behavioral characteristics of the interactive agent, such as aggressiveness or action preference. Changes in κ alter the behavior of the interactive agent and, consequently, the transition dynamics experienced by the learning agent. Remark 1 (Motivation for Finite-Depth Approximation): 

The derivatives Hold and Mold can be estimated from full sampled trajectories or computed by propagating model-based derivative information over the full horizon. However, the former can require substantial trajectory data, whereas the latter incurs full-horizon model propagation. In Section III, we introduce an approach that provides a middle ground by combining these two alternatives. Our method combines reference-environment sampling with model-based derivative propagation truncated to an adjustable propagation depth T . The resulting truncation errors in Hold , Mold , and Sold are analyzed and quantified in Section IV. □

We consider a pursuit–evasion game that captures the agent-induced environment change studied in Section II: the physical dynamics and task remain fixed, while the decision policy of an interactive agent changes. The learning agent L

jointly controls two attackers, A1 and A2, while a defender D

serves as the interactive agent O. At each stage, within a grid world, the attackers move first, producing the intermediate state ˜st. Then, the defender selects its move according to a behavior-parameterized policy ψ(· | ˜st; κ), after which the next state is generated. Thus, the game follows the transition structure in (1), with changes in κ affecting the learning agent only through the defender’s policy. The interactive agent’s behavioral parameter κ character-izes how strongly the defender relies on its belief about infor-mation hidden from it. Specifically, among the two attackers, one of them ( ϕ ∈ { 1, 2}) holds a key and seeks to reach the target row y = 0 . The identity of the key holder ϕ is known to the attackers but hidden from the defender. The defender maintains a belief bt that A1 holds the key and updates this belief by Bayes’ rule based on the observed attacker motions. Its policy is a mixture of two fixed pretrained policies, a belief-blind policy ψ0 and a belief-aware policy ψ1. We let 

ψ(· | ˜st; κ) = (1 − κ)ψ0(· | ˜st) + κψ 1(· | ˜st), κ ∈ [0 , 1] .

Thus, even at the same value of N , the proposed method requires substantially less sampling data. The proposed estimates are relatively insensitive to N over the tested range and achieve low error even with a modest number of initial-state samples. This is because computing conditional expectations through the transition kernel avoids sampling subsequent actions and transitions. As N increases, the remaining error in our method is dominated by the finite-depth approximation and the fixed boundary-value estimate, leading to a relatively flat curve whose level is mainly determined by the propagation depth. B. Adaptation Performance 

We next evaluate whether sensitivity-based initialization improves adaptation beyond directly reusing the reference policy. The large scale instance considers six transfers with 

κold ∈ { 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 1.0} and |∆κ| ∈ [0 .2, 0.6] , covering both increases and decreases in the defender’s behavioral parameter. We compare sensitivity-based initialization with 

direct transfer , initialized at θ⋆(κold ), and training from scratch . The two transfer methods use the same reference policy and identical fine-tuning settings. The only difference is their policy initialization. We estimate Hold and Mold using (15) with T = 1 and 

N = 6000 per estimate, and compute the corresponding sen-sitivity through (16). We average five independent sensitivity estimates and use the average in (17). Each method is trained using PPO in the target environment for 3×10 5 steps with ten seeds. Performance is measured by the discounted return in (2), evaluated over 2000 episodes before training and every 

2×10 4 training steps thereafter. The learning rate is 3×10 −4.Fig. 4 separates the benefit of reference policy reuse from that of the sensitivity-based initialization. Both transfer methods perform better than training from scratch within the fixed budget. More importantly, sensitivity-based initializa-tion improves zero-shot mean return over direct transfer by 

0.21 -0.66 across the six transfers. This advantage persists during fine-tuning in several cases and gradually narrows in others as both methods adapt to the target environment. These results show that sensitivity-based initialization with propagation depth T = 1 can provide useful initializations for the tested transfers. VI. C ONCLUSION
