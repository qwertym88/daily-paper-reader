# Summary 

Policy learning drives many of the most consequential and heavily-invested applications of reinforcement learning today. Yet the core optimization problem it rests on (maximiz-ing expected return) is notoriously non-convex, even under a direct policy parameterization, and the field has largely responded by avoiding it: optimizing convex surrogate approxima-tions of the return under trust-region constraints (NPG, TRPO, PPO, AWR). We show that this seemingly unstructured problem is not actually structureless. In log-density-ratio coordinates 

y := log[ π/π n], the exact per-iteration objective, computable via per-decision importance sampling (PDIS), is a difference-of-convex-constrained difference-of-convex (DC-constrained DC) program . This structure lets us move beyond surrogate approximations: it recovers CPI, NPG, TRPO, and AWR as special cases along interpretable axes, and it opens a multi-step 

axis k that couples consecutive decisions. We solve the per-iteration program with sequential convex programming (SCP) and give convergence guarantees under mild conditions, bridg-ing the difference-of-convex optimization and RL literatures. Empirically, multi-step Convex-Concave RL (CCRL) wins on diagnostic MDPs where credit must propagate across a horizon, is competitive with a tuned PPO on classic control, and on a realistic, stochastic, mid-horizon healthcare domain converges markedly faster than tuned PPO to the same near-optimal sur-vival.

## Abstract 

Policy learning drives many of the most consequential and heavily-invested applica-tions of reinforcement learning today. Yet the core optimization problem it rests on (maximizing expected return) is notoriously non-convex, even under a direct policy parameterization, and the field has largely responded by avoiding it: optimizing con-vex surrogate approximations of the return under trust-region constraints (NPG, TRPO, PPO, AWR). We show that this seemingly unstructured problem is not actually struc-tureless. In log-density-ratio coordinates y := log[ π/π n], the exact per-iteration ob-jective, computable via per-decision importance sampling (PDIS), is a difference-of-convex-constrained difference-of-convex (DC-constrained DC) program . This struc-ture lets us move beyond surrogate approximations: it recovers CPI, NPG, TRPO, and AWR as special cases along interpretable axes, and it opens a multi-step axis k

that couples consecutive decisions. We solve the per-iteration program with sequen-tial convex programming (SCP), the standard solver for difference-of-convex prob-lems, and give convergence guarantees under mild conditions, bridging the difference-of-convex optimization and RL literatures. Empirically, multi-step Convex-Concave RL (CCRL) wins on diagnostic MDPs where credit must propagate across a hori-zon (its advantage growing with the dependency length), is competitive with a tuned PPO on classic control, and on a realistic, stochastic, mid-horizon healthcare domain converges markedly faster than tuned PPO to the same near-optimal survival, with an 11 .3% higher area under the training curve. ## 1 Introduction 

Learning a good policy is the defining problem of reinforcement learning (RL) for researchers and, for practitioners, the lever behind much of the current investment in AI, where pushing model ca-pabilities increasingly reduces to more efficient policy learning. The workhorse behind many of these successes, in robotics and increasingly in large language models, is policy-gradient optimiza-tion (Schulman et al., 2015; 2017; Shao et al., 2024), so any structural insight into how it works translates directly into more efficient and reliable learning. At its core, policy-gradient methods optimize the expected return J(π) of a policy π, an objective that is non-convex even under the direct policy parameterization. Rather than confront this non-convexity in J(π), prevailing methods optimize a convex single-step surrogate, the Conservative Policy Iteration (CPI) objective (Kakade & Langford, 2002), within a trust region around the current policy; NPG (Kakade, 2002), TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015), PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), and AWR (Peng et al., 2019) all instantiate this template. This surrogate is a biased proxy for J, and optimizing it typically improves the policy one timestep at a time. On problems where several timesteps couple to determine the outcome, this single-step view falls short: the update can move the policy away from the optimum until the value estimates catch up. As a workaround, the multi-step dependence is instead carried by the value or advantage estimates that feed the update, while 1the policy-optimization objective itself remains a single-step proxy (Sutton, 1988; Schulman et al., 2016). In this work we confront this seemingly unyielding non-convex optimization directly, through a structured reformulation of the RL objective. Our idea is to optimize the exact per-iteration improvement rather than a surrogate of it. The return difference J(π) − J(πn) is estimable from on-policy trajectories by per-decision importance sam-pling (PDIS) (Precup et al., 2000), and is non-convex in π only because it contains products of im-portance ratios. Re-expressing each policy through its log-ratio to the current one, y := log( π/π n),converts these products into sums of exponentials and decomposes the objective as a difference of two convex functions , a difference-of-convex (DC) program. We call the resulting framework 

Convex-Concave Reinforcement Learning (CCRL). The formulation has three consequences. First, it makes the exact objective directly optimizable, rather than only through a surrogate. Second, it unifies existing methods: at horizon k = 1 the program reduces to the single-step surrogate optimized by CPI, NPG, TRPO, PPO, and AWR. Third, it exposes the horizon k as a free parameter: k ≥ 2 couples several consecutive decisions within a single update, so one update can credit an action for outcomes several steps later, and k = H,the full horizon, optimizes the exact, unbiased return. We solve the program by sequential convex programming (SCP), the standard method for DC problems, adapted so that each step preserves a valid policy, and we establish convergence guarantees. What the reformulation changes is the iteration. Each SCP round replaces the objective by a convex approximation that is solved to global optimality, so the objective improves monotonically by con-struction rather than through a tuned step size, and the iterates approach a stationary point at a known rate. This is bought with computation: a round solves a convex program where a policy-gradient method takes one gradient step. The problem itself remains non-convex, so a stationary point, not a global optimum, is what the iteration delivers. The algorithm we deploy simplifies this procedure. Rather than keeping the policy valid at every round, it enforces validity only once, at the end, and it caps the log-ratios so that the k-step estimates stay well behaved. Enforcing validity only at the end preserves the monotone improvement within an update but forfeits the convergence guarantees (Section 6). The cap is one of two knobs PPO does not have, alongside the truncation depth k, which was the consequential one in our experiments. We evaluate CCRL against a strongly tuned PPO baseline in four settings. On diagnostic MDPs with delayed reward (a risky corridor and increasingly deep risky chains), CCRL’s advantage grows as the delay lengthens. On classic-control tasks (CartPole, Acrobot, MountainCar, and LunarLander) it is competitive with PPO: there, a larger k recovers credit that the dense per-step rewards already supply, while adding the cost of noisier advantage estimates and higher importance-weight variance. On a realistic, stochastic, mid-horizon sepsis task, both reach the same near-optimal survival, but CCRL gets there markedly faster, with an 11 .3% higher area under the training curve.

2.1 Non-convexity of general iterative on-policy optimization 

The iterative on-policy improvement problem (3) is generally non-convex in the policy π. For in-stance, take the direct parameterization of π, where every state-action pair (s, a ) has a separate parameter π(a|s). The feasible set here is convex; the space of valid policies Π is a product of prob-ability simplices, and the f -divergence trust region Df (π ∥ πn) ≤ δ is convex in π as long as the generator f is convex (Belousov & Peters, 2017). The non-convexity lies in the optimization objec-tive Jk(π; πn). At k = 1 , J1 is linear in π and problem (3) is convex; but for k ≥ 2, the cumulative IS product Q 

> t′

ρ(St′ , A t′ ) is a product of linear functions of π across distinct (s, a ) pairs along a trajectory, and this multiplicative coupling makes Jk non-convex in π. Gradient-based optimization techniques applied to problem (3) cannot guarantee global optimality of the solution obtained. 

The optimization problem being non-convex does not mean it is completely structureless, however. 

Non-convex functions can sometimes be re-cast in forms that enable more effective optimization than generic gradient methods. One such structure is the difference-of-convex (DC) decomposition: a function is DC if it can be written as g − h with both g and h convex. DC programs are well-studied in the optimization theory literature (Tao & An, 1997), though optimizing a difference of convex functions is NP-hard in general. The structure instead gives a principled iteration: linearizing the concave part at the current iterate leaves a convex subproblem solvable to global optimality in polynomial time, and the resulting sequence decreases the objective monotonically and converges to a stationary point, which is more than generic gradient descent guarantees and less than global optimality. In the next section, we show that under a suitable change of variable, the trust-region PDIS problem (3) admits an explicit DC decomposition.

## 5 Convergence Analysis 

We now state what the algorithm guarantees. Prior policy-gradient analyses prove global k = 1 

under tabular direct parameterization (Mei et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2021; Xiao, 2022); outside it, the general treatment falls back to gradient stationarity (Agarwal et al., 2022). Our exact objective at k = H is non-convex, yet its DC structure lets us do better. We give an inner-loop guarantee (convergence of the SCP rounds within one policy iteration) and an outer-loop guarantee (contrac-tion of the policy sequence). All results assume the direct parameterization on log-density ratios; full statements and proofs are in Appendix F.

7.4 CCRL can outperform PPO on a stochastic, medium-horizon task 

Sepsis care is a canonical sequential-decision problem in clinical RL (Komorowski et al., 2018; Oberst & Sontag, 2019): a clinician treats a patient whose condition evolves stochastically, with survival revealed only at the end of the episode. Crucially, many first-line therapies act only once built up to a therapeutic level, so their benefit is realized several decisions after treatment begins. Our Therapeutic-Threshold Sepsis MDP captures this. A state pairs illness severity h with an accu-mulated dose d, and at each of H=20 steps the clinician chooses to rest , dose (advance d toward the therapeutic threshold, with no immediate benefit), or administer (a high-probability recovery once the threshold d=D−1 is reached, but ineffective and dose-resetting otherwise). Survival, a sparse terminal reward, is thus earned only by carrying the patient through D preparatory doses and then administering, so the payoff is separated from the actions that earn it by the full depth D. With 

M =5 severity levels and D=3 doses to threshold, the optimal policy survives 84 .0% of episodes (full dynamics in Appendix G.6). In this regime, credit is genuinely multi-step (each preparatory dose has a negative one-step advan-tage, so a k=1 method like PPO is initially misled away from dosing), while the moderate horizon keeps the advantage structure learnable and the one real threat, transition variance, is controllable with a tight y-clip. ## 8 Related Work 

CCRL connects to three lines of work, each discussed in the paper where it is used: the CPI-surrogate family it generalizes (CPI, NPG, TRPO, AWR; Section 3.5); multi-step credit-assignment methods that shape the value or advantage target rather than the objective (TD( λ), GAE, Retrace); and the difference-of-convex and policy-gradient-convergence literatures our analysis builds on (Section 5). Closest in spirit is POIS (Metelli et al., 2018), which likewise treats importance sampling as an 

optimization objective; CCRL differs by exposing its difference-of-convex structure and using the truncation depth k to couple consecutive decisions. A comprehensive, fully-referenced treatment is given in Appendix H. 

## 9 Conclusion 

This work studied iterative on-policy improvement as a log-sum-exp difference-of-convex (DC) pro-gram and proposed Convex-Concave RL (CCRL), which recovers CPI, NPG, TRPO, and AWR as special cases and extends them along the divergence order α and the truncation depth k. Theoreti-cally, we established an unconditional O(1 /R ) inner-loop stationarity guarantee for any k and, at the exact-improvement setting k=H with a χ2 trust region, a conditional geometric contraction of the outer policy sequence under bounded concentrability. Empirically, on the risky corridor and chain MDPs the multi-step objective recovered credit that single-step methods missed, with a margin that widened as the chain deepened. On classic control it is competitive with PPO but not consistently better, which we attribute to noisy advantage estimates and k-step PDIS variance; on a stochastic, horizon-20 clinical task it shows a convergence edge. 

Limitations and open directions. CCRL brings convex-optimization machinery to policy optimiza-tion, subject to several limitations. Each policy iteration runs several SCP rounds and is therefore more expensive than PPO’s single gradient step; the truncation depth k is an additional knob that can be delicate to tune; and the theory leans on relatively strong assumptions—bounded concentra-bility and a globally solved inner problem for the outer-loop contraction, and α > 1 (excluding the reverse-KL endpoint) for the inner-loop analysis. Promising directions include (1) variance reduc-tion for the k-step couplings, (2) estimating appropriate couplings k dynamically (per state or per iteration), (3) scaling to larger, higher-dimensional domains, and (4) sharper convergence analysis giving full-sequence bounds for the inner loop. 12 Contributions

## H Extended related work 

Iterative on-policy policy optimization. A large family of methods improves a policy by repeat-edly maximizing a per-iteration surrogate of J(π) under a proximity constraint to the current it-erate. Conservative Policy Iteration (CPI) (Kakade & Langford, 2002) established the surrogate-based template; natural policy gradient (NPG) (Kakade, 2002), TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015), and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) instantiate it with different trust-region mechanisms (exact KL, clipping), and advantage-weighted regression—AWR (Peng et al., 2019) and AWAC (Nair et al., 2020)—recasts the update as regression onto an exponentially-tilted behavior policy, π∗(a|s) ∝

πb(a|s) exp( Aπb (s, a )/η ). All of these optimize the single-step ( k = 1 ) CPI surrogate; CCRL re-covers that surrogate and instantiates CPI, NPG, TRPO, and AWR as (k, α, validity ) special cases (Section 3.5)—PPO’s clipping heuristic and AWAC’s off-policy reuse lie outside this (α, validity )

family—and extends the template along the multi-step axis k ≥ 2.35 Multi-step credit assignment. The difficulty of propagating credit across delayed rewards is classi-cally addressed by injecting multi-step information into the value target : TD( λ) and eligibility traces (Sutton, 1988) interpolate between one-step bootstrapping and Monte-Carlo returns, generalized ad-vantage estimation (GAE) (Schulman et al., 2016) applies the same exponential weighting to advan-tage estimates for variance reduction, and off-policy return operators such as Retrace( λ) (Munos et al., 2016) correct for the behavior–target mismatch with truncated importance weights. These methods shape the advantage estimates fed into a single-step update; CCRL is complementary—it places the multi-step horizon k inside the optimization objective itself (2), so the policy update, not just the critic target, couples consecutive decisions. 

Importance sampling as an optimization objective. PDIS and its variants (Precup et al., 2000; Jiang & Li, 2016) are primarily studied for off-policy evaluation . A closer line uses importance sampling as an optimization objective: POIS (Metelli et al., 2018) reuses collected trajectories by optimizing an IS-based surrogate offline, with a high-confidence penalty controlling the estimator’s variance. We likewise treat PDIS as the optimization target rather than an evaluation estimator, but expose the surrogate’s difference-of-convex structure in log-density-ratio coordinates and use the truncation depth k to trade off multi-step credit propagation against variance. 

Convergence of policy-gradient methods. Tabular policy-gradient convergence has been estab-lished at several rates. Mei et al. (2020) give O(1 /t ) global convergence for softmax PG via a non-uniform Łojasiewicz inequality, though Li et al. (2023) show the effective iteration count can be exponential in |S| /(1 − γ). Agarwal et al. (2021) give O(1 /ε ) suboptimality for natural pol-icy gradient (NPG) under direct parameterization. Cen et al. (2022) obtain linear convergence for entropy-regularized NPG, and Xiao (2022) sharpens this to linear convergence for policy mirror descent (PMD) with geometrically increasing step sizes (subsuming NPG) without entropy regu-larization. This line has since been generalized: Lan (2023) establishes linear convergence and sampling-complexity bounds for PMD across a broad class of regularized MDPs, and Alfano et al. (2023) extends linear-convergence guarantees to PMD with general (including neural) parameter-izations. All of these global-convergence results depend on the k = 1 CPI-surrogate objective being linear in π; when the objective is non-convex, the general policy-gradient treatment in the RL-theory monograph of Agarwal et al. (2022) yields only gradient-stationarity guarantees rather than global rates. Our convergence analysis (Section 5) applies to k ≥ 1 via the DC structure: an unconditional O(1 /R ) inner-loop stationarity rate for any k, and a conditional geometric contrac-tion of the outer policy sequence at the exact-improvement setting k = H (with χ2 trust regions) under concentrability—comparable in form to the tabular linear rates above but proven for the exact multi-step objective, where linearity in π no longer holds. 

Difference-of-convex and geometric programming. The change of variable that convexifies our objective—taking logarithms to turn multiplicative importance-ratio products into additive exponentials—is the defining move of geometric programming , in which posynomial programs be-come convex in log-transformed variables (Boyd et al., 2007). Applied to the k-step PDIS objective, this exposes a difference-of-convex (DC) structure. DC programming has a long history (Tao & An, 1997; Lipp & Boyd, 2016; Thi & Dinh, 2018) but has seen limited use in reinforcement learning; our contribution is to identify the DC structure in the PDIS objective and to handle the policy-simplex validity constraint—a DC equality—within our SCP procedure (Section 4). We keep our guarantees at the assumption-free level standard for DCA, leaving full-sequence rates as an extension. 36
