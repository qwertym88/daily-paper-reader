## Abstract 

Real-world interaction networks are inherently dynamic: edges form and dissolve as node behavior shifts over time. Most snapshot-based contrastive methods encode temporal dependencies implicitly in encoder weights, without an explicit model of how node representations evolve, making them brittle under distribution shifts. We propose KAIROS (Koopman-Aligned Invariant Representations for Open Dynamic Systems), a self-supervised framework that embeds a differentiable Koopman operator within a dynamic graph contrastive learning loop to linearize temporal evolution in the learned embedding space. A dual-view encoder pairs raw node features with a graph-diffused structural view and is optimized with multi-granularity contrastive objectives across temporal windows. For anomaly detection, KAIROS uses the Koopman prediction residual together with temporal inconsistency and local neighborhood deviation to separate irregular behavior from predictable graph evolution. Evaluated on nine dynamic graph benchmarks, KAIROS achieves state-of-the-art anomaly detection results on all nine datasets, with gains of up to 23.15 ROC-AUC points over prior work, while remaining competitive for unsupervised node classification. These results show that explicit dynamics modeling provides a scalable and effective inductive bias for temporal graph representation learning. 

## 1 Introduction 

Real-world relational systems such as financial transaction networks, social platforms, and citation graphs are inherently dynamic. Dynamic Graph Representation Learning (DGRL) seeks node embeddings that capture structural dependencies and temporal evolution, enabling downstream tasks such as node classification, link prediction, and anomaly detection. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are well established for static graphs, but extending them to dynamic settings remains challenging. Many dynamic-graph encoders model temporal evolution through architectural mechanisms and lack a transition operator that predicts how node embeddings evolve between snapshots. Snapshot-based models aggregate per-snapshot neighborhoods, while continuous-time models encode interactions through functional time encodings, causal anonymous walks, neighborhood/community influence aggregation, or frequency-domain temporal features [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. These designs do not provide a parameterized one-step operator over node embeddings. Without such a model, predictable evolution and anomalous deviation are difficult to separate under distribution shift, where both may appear as movement in embedding space [6, 7, 8]. This limitation is amplified in self-supervised dynamic graph learning. Strong dynamic contrastive baselines such as CLDG and CLDG++ maximize agreement between temporal views [9, 10]. This provides a useful smoothness bias but does not model how representations evolve. When evolu-tion is non-smooth, view agreement can penalize legitimate change and fail to isolate deviations. Existing dynamic graph anomaly detectors address temporal anomalies through dynamic embed-

dings, transformer-based spatiotemporal modeling, or semi-supervised pseudo-labeling [11, 12, 13]. However, they do not define anomaly scores as residuals of a learned transition operator. Koopman operator theory offers a direct way to impose such a transition model: observables of nonlinear dynamical systems evolve linearly in an infinite-dimensional lifted space [14, 15]. Neural Koopman approximations have been used for forecasting, control, and sequence modeling through reconstruction, forecasting, or operator-regression objectives [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. To our knowledge, no prior work couples a graph encoder and Koopman operator end-to-end under a contrastive objective, or uses the resulting Koopman residual as an unsupervised anomaly signal on dynamic graphs. We propose KAIROS (Koopman-Aligned Invariant Representations for Open Dynamic Systems), a self-supervised framework that embeds a differentiable Koopman operator inside a dynamic graph contrastive learning loop. A dual-view GNN encoder processes raw features and a Personalized PageRank (PPR)-diffused view [21], and optimizes an InfoNCE objective across temporal windows. A linear operator K, initialized at the identity and regularized toward orthogonality, is co-optimized to predict each node’s next-snapshot embedding. Initializing K = I makes the identity transition a special case of the learned operator, so any deviation from identity is loss-driven. The same encoder supports two downstream tasks without architectural change: (i) for unsupervised node classification, the Koopman regularizer is disabled and the frozen encoder feeds a linear probe; (ii) for unsupervised dynamic-graph anomaly detection, the Koopman prediction residual is combined with temporal inconsistency and local neighborhood deviation, each z-normalized, into a unified anomaly score. Our main contributions are: (i) We couple a differentiable Koopman operator with self-supervised dynamic graph contrastive learning, jointly optimizing the graph encoder and linear temporal op-erator end-to-end. (ii) We introduce a dual-view contrastive objective over raw and PPR-diffused features, augmented with Koopman prediction and orthogonality regularization. (iii) We define a fully unsupervised anomaly score from temporal inconsistency, Koopman prediction residual, and neighborhood deviation, without pseudo-labels or reconstruction losses. 

## 2 Related Work 

Dynamic Graph Representation Learning. Two regimes dominate DGRL. Discrete-time snap-shot models such as DySAT [1] aggregate per-snapshot neighborhoods and combine them with temporal self-attention, while continuous-time models such as TGAT [2], CAW [3], MNCI [4], and FreeDyG [5] encode interactions through functional time encodings, causal anonymous walks, neighborhood/community influence aggregation, or frequency-domain temporal features. A comple-mentary line improves the expressivity and stability of temporal message passing through long-range propagation and refined inductive biases [22, 23, 24]. None of these designs supplies an explicit model of how representations evolve between snapshots: temporal dependencies are absorbed into encoder weights, leaving distribution shift as a recurring failure mode [6, 7, 8]. The limitation is representational rather than architectural: predictable evolution and anomalous deviation become indistinguishable. KAIROS departs from this setting by coupling representation learning with a differ-entiable Koopman operator that explicitly parameterizes inter-snapshot dynamics, turning temporal predictability into an inductive bias. 

Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning on Graphs. Static graph contrastive learning is mature, with a well-mapped design space [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30] that modifies topology or features and aligns the resulting views under InfoNCE or redundancy-reduction objectives [31, 32]. Subsequent analyses have exposed structural fragilities of this framework: asymmetric encoders without augmentations match or exceed augmentation-heavy baselines [33]; perfect alignment can be harmful under topology shifts [34]; hidden architectural mechanisms often dominate loss-level behavior [35, 36]; and InfoNCE admits a spectral-clustering interpretation [37, 38]. A complementary line studies spectral, principled, or efficiency-aware augmentations [39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. When extended to dynamic graphs, however, these advances are largely stripped of their temporal premise: CLDG [9] and CLDG++ [10] treat temporally sampled subgraphs as augmented views and maximize InfoNCE agreement across time windows, optionally at multiple time spans, but impose no explicit dynamics between those windows. The implicit assumption that temporally adjacent embeddings remain similar reduces to a smoothness penalty that becomes brittle when graph evolution is non-smooth. 

Graph Anomaly Detection. Unsupervised anomaly detection on attributed graphs is dominated by context–patch disagreement methods that combine contrastive or reconstruction objectives with 2multi-scale or augmentation-aware designs [44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. These methods are powerful but inherently static: they ignore temporal evolution and repeatedly flag the same structural anomaly at every snapshot, reducing precision under non-stationary baselines. Contrastive graph anomaly detection refines this framework along three axes: structural imbalance [50], clean-view formula-tions [51], and dual-encoder augmentation [52]. Existing dynamic-graph detectors fail in different ways: NetWalk [11] relies on shallow random-walk embeddings and lacks attribute-aware expressive-ness; TADDY [12] applies self-attention over edge-centric temporal subgraphs whose complexity grows quadratically, limiting scalability on realistic graphs; SAD [13] is semi-supervised and depends on pseudo-labels derived from partial ground truth, making it unsuitable for the fully unsupervised setting; and spatiotemporal anomaly networks [53, 54] target multivariate time series rather than evolving topologies. 

Koopman Operator Theory in Machine Learning. Koopman operator theory provides a linear rep-resentation of nonlinear dynamics in an infinite-dimensional space [14, 15], and its finite-dimensional approximations now support a broad class of learning methods. Foundational work established neural realizations of Koopman embeddings [16, 17, 55, 56, 57] and rigorous statistical guaran-tees [58, 59, 60]. Application-driven extensions span operator forecasting [18], robust control [19], action recognition [61], sequence disentanglement [20], structured operator parameterizations [62], and recent neural-ODE and probabilistic forecasting hybrids [63, 64, 65, 66]. On graphs, however, Koopman theory remains underexplored. The closest related work is Shi et al. [67], which links GNN message passing to dynamic mode decomposition for supervised dynamics modeling, without a contrastive or anomaly-detection component. KAIROS instead uses a differentiable Koopman operator inside self-supervised dynamic graph contrastive learning and uses its prediction residual as an unsupervised anomaly signal. 

## 3 Background and Preliminaries 

We consider dynamic graphs represented as temporal snapshots {G 1, . . . , GT } over a fixed node universe V, with node features X and timestamp function T . Continuous-time graphs are converted to snapshots by partitioning the temporal span into T equal-width windows. KAIROS uses two views of each snapshot: the raw feature view X and a PPR-diffused structural view Xppr .The goal is to learn a self-supervised encoder fθ that supports two downstream tasks: unsupervised dynamic node classification via a frozen linear probe, and unsupervised dynamic graph anomaly detection via a scalar node-level anomaly score. KAIROS models temporal evolution in the learned embedding space with a linear Koopman operator K, whose prediction residual provides one anomaly signal. Details of this section is in Appendix!A 

## 4 KAIROS 

KAIROS is a self-supervised framework for learning node representations from dynamic graphs and addressing both Problems 1 and 2 with a shared encoder. The framework consists of four coupled components: (i) temporal snapshot construction and inductive view sampling (Section 4.1); (ii) a dual-encoder architecture with independent GNN backbones for raw and PPR-diffused views, equipped with SimCLR v2 projection heads (Section 4.2); (iii) a multi-granularity contrastive objective coupling local, global, and cross-view signals across time (Section 4.3); (iv) a Koopman temporal operator K

that linearizes embedding-space dynamics, regularizes training, and produces a per-node anomaly residual at inference (Section 4.4). At inference, three complementary anomaly signals are z-normalized and summed into a unified score (Section 4.5). An overview is shown in Figure 1.

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced KAIROS, a self-supervised framework that couples dynamic graph contrastive learn-ing with a differentiable Koopman operator. By learning a linear transition in embedding space, KAIROS separates predictable temporal evolution from irregular deviation and derives anomaly scores from Koopman prediction residuals. Results across nine temporal graph benchmarks suggest that explicit dynamics modeling is an effective inductive bias for temporal graph representation learning, particularly in fully unsupervised anomaly detection. 

Limitations. KAIROS operates on discrete-time snapshots and therefore depends on the tempo-ral windowing scheme. Highly irregular or event-driven graphs may violate the linear Koopman assumption, reducing the effectiveness of the Koopman regularizer. The framework also follows prior work in evaluating anomaly detection with synthetic anomaly injection, so performance on naturally occurring anomalies may vary across domains.

15 A Details of Background and Preliminaries 

We formalize the input data, structural representations, temporal evolution, and learning objectives used in this work. Notation is introduced locally. 

A.1 Dynamic Graph Modeling Definition 1 (Discrete-Time Dynamic Graph) . A discrete-time dynamic graph (DTDG) with T

temporal snapshots is an ordered sequence G1, G2, . . . , GT , where each Gt = ( Vt, Et, Xt) consists of a node set Vt, an edge set Et ⊆ V t × V t, and a node feature matrix Xt ∈ R|V t|× F . Here, F

denotes the feature dimension. For a fixed node universe V, we write N = |V| .

In KAIROS, all snapshots are represented over a fixed global node universe V with N = |V| ; Vt

denotes the active nodes observed in snapshot t, while inactive nodes are retained in the canonicalized snapshot with no incident temporal edges except self-loops. 

Definition 2 (Continuous-Time Dynamic Graph) . A continuous-time dynamic graph (CTDG) is a tuple G = ( V, E, X, T ), where X ∈ RN ×F is the node feature matrix and T : V ∪ E → R+ assigns a real-valued timestamp to each node and edge. The total temporal span is ∆t = max( T ) − min( T ).

Both formats are handled uniformly by partitioning ∆t into T equal-width windows, converting any CTDG into an ordered snapshot sequence G1, . . . , GT . Each snapshot retains the temporal edges whose timestamps fall within its window while sharing the fixed node universe V.

A.4 Studied Problems 

KAIROS addresses two complementary tasks using the same learned representation. 16 Problem 1 (Unsupervised Dynamic Node Classification) . Given a dynamic graph G with input modalities (X, A, S, T ) and no label supervision during training , learn an encoder fθ such that the node representation zi ∈ Rd, obtained from the frozen encoder, supports node classification through a post-hoc linear classifier trained on a small labeled subset. 

Problem 2 (Unsupervised Dynamic Graph Anomaly Detection) . Given the same dynamic graph G

with no anomaly labels during training, assign each node vi ∈ V an anomaly score si ∈ R such that larger values correspond to unusual temporal, structural, or neighborhood behavior relative to the learned graph dynamics. 

Both problems use the same encoder architecture fθ but different training objectives. For Problem 1, 

λkoop is set to zero because strict linear temporal predictability can over-constrain the embedding space and suppress class-discriminative directions. For Problem 2, λkoop is selected per dataset on the validation set.

## C Baseline Descriptions 

For node classification, we compare against fourteen methods across four categories. 18 Semi-Supervised Methods. LP [74] propagates labels along the graph transition matrix, converging to a harmonic labeling that is globally smooth with respect to the topology while remaining consistent with labeled seed nodes. 

GCN [75] encodes node features and graph topology through stacked layers of localized spectral convolutions. 

GAT [76] employs masked self-attention to assign learnable importance weights to neighbors, removing the need for a fixed aggregation kernel. 

GraphSAGE [77] learns inductive node embeddings by sampling and aggregating features from local neighborhoods, enabling generalization to unseen nodes. 

Unsupervised Dynamic Graph Methods. CAW [3] encodes temporal network motifs by extracting causal anonymous walks and using set-based anonymization to capture structural correlations over time. 

TGAT [2] extends self-attention to continuous-time temporal graphs via functional time encoding, jointly modeling node features and evolving topology. 

DySAT [1] computes dynamic node representations through a two-level self-attention architecture that attends over structural neighborhoods and historical snapshots. 

MNCI [4] generates time-conditioned node embeddings by combining neighborhood and community-level influence through a specialized aggregation function. 

Unsupervised Static Contrastive Methods. DGI [25] trains a GCN encoder by maximizing mutual information between local patch representa-tions and a global graph summary. 

GRACE [27] produces two corrupted graph views by independently dropping edges and masking node features, then maximizes cross-view node-level agreement. 

MVGRL [26] contrasts node and graph representations across views generated from the input adjacency and its Personalized PageRank diffusion. 

CCA-SSG [29] applies augmentation-generated view pairs under a redundancy-reduction objective inspired by canonical correlation analysis. 

Dynamic Contrastive Baselines. CLDG [9] extracts temporal snapshot views via a configurable timespan sampling layer supporting both high-overlap ( 75% ) and low-overlap ( 25% ) strategies, treating each sampled window as an aug-mented temporal view of the graph. An InfoNCE objective trains the encoder to produce temporally consistent node embeddings, followed by a single full-graph pass for downstream evaluation. 

CLDG++ [10] extends CLDG with a structural diffusion view derived from a Personalized PageRank weighted graph, and defines a multi-scale contrastive objective that enforces consistency at three granularities: local-local, local-global, and global-global across time spans. CLDG++ is the primary baseline for KAIROS, as it shares the same input modalities ( X, A, S, T ) and evaluation protocol. 

Anomaly Detection Baselines. For anomaly detection (Table 3), we additionally compare against seven dedicated methods spanning static, dynamic, and semi-supervised paradigms; CLDG and CLDG++ are also evaluated on this task using the anomaly scoring protocol described in Section 5.3. 

AEGIS [47] performs inductive anomaly detection on attributed networks via an adversarial graph differentiation framework, training a generator to synthesize potential anomalies and a discriminator to learn decision boundaries that separate abnormal nodes from normal ones. 

CoLA [44] detects anomalies on attributed networks via contrastive self-supervised learning, measur-ing the agreement between each node’s representation and its local neighborhood subgraph to surface structural and attribute inconsistencies. 

ANEMONE [45] identifies graph anomalies through multi-scale contrastive learning, simultaneously evaluating patch-level and context-level agreements to capture both local and global irregularity patterns. 19 GRADATE [46] generates multiple graph views via edge-based augmentation and applies a multi-scale contrastive network with node-subgraph, node-node, and subgraph-subgraph contrasts to capture anomalous signals across complementary structural granularities. 

NetWalk [11] learns flexible deep embeddings for dynamic networks by processing network walks through a deep autoencoder for clique embedding, incrementally updating vertex representations to detect evolving anomalies in a streaming fashion. 

TADDY [12] captures spatial-temporal patterns across discrete graph snapshots using a Transformer architecture with centrality-based spatial-temporal node encodings to model dynamic structural dependencies for anomaly detection. 

SAD [13] formulates anomaly detection on dynamic graphs in a semi-supervised manner, encoding both labeled and unlabeled samples through a temporal graph network augmented with a time-equipped memory bank and a pseudo-label contrastive learning module.

The checkpoint with the lowest total training loss (Eq. 8) is retained for evaluation. Anchor batch size B=256 nodes, sampled from the intersection of source-node sets across all V views. Neighbor sampling is adaptive: graphs with N ≤8,000 nodes use full-neighbor expansion ( MultiLayerFullNeighborSampler ); larger graphs use per-layer fanout [20 , 20] (NeighborSampler ). Evaluation protocol. Node classification. Encoder weights are frozen; one forward pass on the canonicalized full static graph ( to_simple → to_bidirected → add_self_loop ). Node labels are split per class: within each class, indices are randomly shuffled (seed 24) and divided 10% train, 10% validation, 80% test. All experiments run under five random seeds

## N Dataset Details 

Data format and node features. All nine datasets are stored as space-separated edge-list files with columns: source node index, destination node index, timestamp. Node labels are stored in a separate file (node index, class label). No dataset provides raw node attributes; each node receives a 24 128-dimensional sinusoidal positional encoding keyed to its integer node index (Eq. 17), following the CLDG evaluation protocol [9]. 

Snapshot preprocessing. Each temporal subgraph is canonicalized identically during training and during anomaly evaluation: duplicate edges are removed, the graph is symmetrized, and self-loops are added. 

Dataset summary. Table 1 (main paper) gives graph-level statistics ( |V| , |E| , class count). Table 13 below details the per-dataset anomaly injection parameters. The primary six datasets (DBLP, Bit-coinotc, BITotc, BITalpha, TAX51, Reddit) replicate the CLDG/CLDG++ benchmark [9, 10]; the extended three (MOOC, Arxiv, Elliptic) broaden coverage to larger graphs, a 40-class citation setting, and a blockchain transaction network, and are not part of the CLDG or CLDG++ benchmark. Anomaly injection protocol. Structural anomalies: n cliques of m=15 nodes, with all intra-clique edges assigned a timestamp drawn uniformly from [min T , max T ] of the respective dataset. Attribute anomalies: n·m nodes each have their 128-d sinusoidal feature replaced by that of the most Euclidean-distant node found among k=50 randomly sampled candidates. The two sets are node-disjoint, yielding exactly 2nm anomaly nodes per dataset: nm structural and nm attribute.
