Despite recent advances in humanoid locomotion, controllers optimized for command tracking and robustness tend to produce mechanical gaits, whereas controllers tied to human motion data often fail to generalize to commands outside the data distribution. This work introduces a learning framework that balances these competing objectives to synthesize real-time steerable, robust, and biomimetic locomotion policies from human data. Using an in-house curated locomotion dataset covering diverse speeds and directions, we first learn a natural locomotion prior policy through a teacher–student distillation process. Specifically, we train a full-body reference-conditioned policy with Reinforcement Learning (RL), then distill it into a lightweight prior policy conditioned solely on proprioception and a planar torso-velocity steering command. Next, we fine-tune the prior policy with multi-task RL to expand command coverage and robustness beyond the data distribution, pairing a goal-conditioned task that tracks arbitrary commands with a reference-guided task that tracks the human data as an explicit style regularizer. We validate our framework on three humanoid robots: the Boston Dynamics Atlas R1, Atlas D1, and Unitree G1. Experimental results demonstrate robust performance across real-world scenarios, including direct user-controlled locomotion in indoor and outdoor environments, and integration as the locomotion layer within hierarchical control stacks. Benchmarks against Tabula Rasa RL policies trained without human data and ablation studies confirm that our framework yields a lightweight, deployable policy that reconstructs coordinated whole-body behavior from a steering command, retaining the human gait characteristics while remaining robust and fully steerable. 

INTRODUCTION 

Enabling robots to work alongside people in shared environments has long motivated the field of humanoid robotics. Within this broader goal, humanoid locomotion has been a central benchmark because walking is one of the most visible and socially salient forms of embodied behavior. This pursuit has shaped generations of robotic research, from the pioneering, cautious footsteps of Honda ASIMO in the early 2000s [1] to the robust and energetic gait of Boston Dynamics PETMAN in the 2010s [2]. Beyond aesthetic appeal, human-mimetic locomotion can provide familiar kinematic and social cues, such as step timing, body motion, and velocity transitions, that people can interpret through prior social and sensorimotor experience from human-human interaction [3, 4]. As a result, such motions can make robot behavior easier to anticipate, interpret, and coordinate with, thereby improving legibility, trust, and usability in human-robot collaborative environments [5, 6]. However, achieving human-like locomotion remains an open chal-lenge, particularly when natural full-body motion must be steered by high-level commands in real-time and transferred reliably to phys-ical hardware. This requirement exposes a fundamental trade-off, wherein controllers optimized primarily for command tracking and dis-turbance recovery often produce mechanical, unnatural gaits, whereas controllers tightly constrained to recorded human motion data can lose responsiveness when commands require transitions, speeds, or direc-tion combinations that are underrepresented in the reference motion dataset used for development. A deployable controller must satisfy three requirements simultaneously: (1) it must preserve the stylistic quality of human locomotion and natural gait transitions across speeds arXiv:2610.10489v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026 Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 2

and directions; (2) it must respond accurately and stably to a broad range of user-specified velocity commands; and (3) it must execute with sufficiently low latency and modest memory and compute re-quirements for onboard deployment. To address this challenge, we introduce HuMBLE (Hu man Motion-driven Behavior Learning for 

Embodied Locomotion). HuMBLE is a learning-based framework that distills human-motion priors into a compact command-conditioned locomotion policy and subsequently expands command coverage while preserving stylistic consistency. At runtime, the resulting policy maps proprioceptive observations and SE(2) velocity commands directly to joint-level actions, without requiring reference motions, latent motion sampling, or computationally intensive generative inference. This broader pursuit of stable, responsive, and natural humanoid locomotion has led to a long line of work based on first-principles approaches to control and motion generation. Historically, these ap-proaches have included heuristic motion generation [7, 8], high-level motion features [9], and biomechanically derived control objectives [10]. These principles have shaped modern control frameworks, includ-ing model-based control frameworks [11–13] and reward-engineered Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches [14, 15]. Such methods have demonstrated stable, responsive locomotion on humanoids, particularly for command tracking, balance, and disturbance recovery. However, en-coding the full variability of human gait using hand-designed objectives remains difficult, given that human-like walking depends on highly coupled full-body coordination, subtle nuances in contact timing, arm and leg swing trajectories, posture, and speed- and direction-dependent gait transitions. As a result, controllers optimized primarily through analytic models or hand-crafted objectives may achieve strong tracking and recovery while failing to reproduce the stylistic variability and coordination patterns present in human locomotion. An alternative approach uses human motion data directly as a source of supervision for producing naturalistic behavior. Human motion data is relatively easy to collect at scale and has long been central to char-acter animation, from motion graphs and interactive motion synthesis [16–18] to modern kinematic methods such as Motion Matching [19– 21]. Recent work has increasingly shifted toward deep generative models, including Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) [22], Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [23], diffusion models [24], and flow-matching models [25]. In this setting, motion data is treated as samples from an underlying reference distribution rather than as target trajecto-ries to be tracked. This paradigm shifts the objective from reproducing specific sequences to aligning the distribution of synthesized behaviors with that of the reference dataset. VAEs, in particular, have been widely used to embed high-dimensional motion into structured latent spaces that can be explored by higher-level policies [26–30]. Related ideas have also been adapted to legged robots, either by coupling VAE-based motion generators with RL motion trackers [31] or by conditioning policies on learned gait embeddings [32]. Despite their interpretability and controllability, VAE-based approaches can struggle to reproduce the kinematic structure and contact consistency of the demonstrated motions, resulting in artifacts such as foot slippage and unnatural joint configurations that undermine physical plausibility of the motions. Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) provides an alternative ap-proach for directly training physically grounded controllers using mo-tion data. Instead of first synthesizing kinematic motions and then tracking them with a separate controller, AIL methods train policies using a discriminator reward that evaluates how closely the executed behavior matches the demonstrations, optimizing until the two are in-distinguishable. The Adversarial Motion Prior ( AMP ) [33] framework is a representative example wherein an RL policy is optimized using both task rewards for command tracking and a discriminator-based style reward derived from motion data. This formulation has been applied to achieve stylized and steerable locomotion on quadrupedal and humanoid robots while avoiding an explicit kinematic motion-generation stage [34–36]. Extensions such as ASE [37] and CALM 

[38] further introduce skill embeddings and hierarchical control to mitigate mode collapse and preserve behavioral diversity. However, ad-versarial objectives introduce a min-max training problem that can be sensitive to reward scaling, discriminator design, and hyperparameter selection. Additionally, the learned style reward offers only indirect influence over the resulting behavior, which complicates fine-grained user control over emergent behaviors. More recently, diffusion and flow-matching models have emerged as more expressive alternatives for modeling multimodal motion distri-butions, with conditioning mechanisms based on partial states [39, 40], pose or velocity commands [41], or text prompts [42]. A growing body of work has begun extending these architectures to physical con-trol by mapping directly from robot states and commands to actions [40, 43, 44]. Although these approaches leverage expressive generative architectures to preserve motion diversity and eliminate separate track-ing layers to streamline deployment, the underlying iterative sampling or integration procedures remain difficult to reconcile with the strict latency, computational, and power constraints of real-time onboard control. The approaches discussed above show that motion data can provide rich priors for natural locomotion. However, adversarial or generative formulations introduce training instabilities, indirect behavioral con-trol, or runtime inference overheads that are prohibitive for low-latency onboard humanoid control. Instead, we cast human-motion-driven locomotion learning as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Pro-cess (POMDP), wherein full-body reference states are available as 

privileged information during training but are not provided to the final deployment policy. Rather than explicitly tracking a full-body reference trajectory at runtime, the deployment policy learns to reconstruct coor-dinated full-body behavior from proprioceptive observations and user SE(2) velocity commands, mapping these inputs directly to joint-level actions. Generally, teacher–student architectures have proven effective in addressing POMDP formulations in legged robotics, particularly for rough-terrain locomotion where terrain geometry is only partially observable [45, 46] or unobservable on hardware [47]. More closely aligned with our work, similar principles were used to learn diverse human-like behaviors conditioned on high-level user commands from human motion data in physically simulated characters [48]. Inspired by this approach, we distill full-body motion priors from human motion data into a single, compact, command-conditioned policy, avoiding both training instabilities and computationally intensive generative modules at deployment. A primary technical challenge with relying solely on this teacher– student distillation is that training the policy strictly within the support of the reference dataset restricts its command coverage. At deployment time, steering commands that are absent or poorly represented in the original dataset can lead to out-of-distribution failures. To address this limitation, we adopt an approach similar to the pre-training and post-training paradigm that has recently emerged in foundation mod-els [49, 50]. Building on this core concept, applying RL fine-tuning as a post-training step has increasingly gained attention across the robotics community as a way to expose policies to broader distribu-tions over tasks, commands, or environments. For instance, Parkour in the Wild [51] uses this strategy to distill multi-skill terrain-aware locomotion into a single policy and then fine-tunes it on broader terrain distributions. InterPrior [52] applies a similar principle to whole-body loco-manipulation, distilling diverse skills into a policy conditioned on sparse, multimodal goals and fine-tuning it for generalization across large human-object interaction spaces. Extending this post-distillation fine-tuning paradigm, HuMBLE resolves the style–command coverage trade-off through a structured multi-task RL formulation. Rather than fine-tuning the distilled policy solely for goal-conditioned command track-ing, HuMBLE couples short-horizon reference-guided imitation, which anchors the policy to the human motion manifold, with long-horizon goal-conditioned command tracking, which improves responsiveness and robustness beyond the dataset’s support. A goal-conditioned state-initialization scheme further stabilizes this process by seeding rollouts from dynamically plausible, command-relevant states. The key idea behind HuMBLE is to separate style acquisition from command-space expansion. The proposed framework implements this idea through a two-stage training process based on a newly curated human motion dataset encompassing diverse headings, velocities, and gait modes, including small stepping, canonical walking, sprinting, sidestepping, and turning. In the first stage, a natural locomotion prior is distilled from a full-body reference-conditioned teacher policy into a student policy conditioned only on planar torso-velocity commands (SE(2) velocity commands) and the robot’s proprioceptive state. In the second stage, this prior is fine-tuned using the multi-task formula-tion described above to expand command coverage while preserving fidelity to the motion data. Together, these components yield a compact command-conditioned policy that produces naturalistic, diverse, and steerable locomotion without requiring full-body reference motions at runtime. We evaluate how HuMBLE balances the trade-off between human-like motion style, command responsiveness, robustness during real-world deployment through experiments on Boston Dynamics Atlas R1, Atlas D1, and Unitree G1 humanoids across both laboratory and outdoor locomotion scenarios as illustrated in Figure 1. In our quanti-tative evaluation, policy performance is analyzed using metrics aligned with the design criteria: fidelity to the human motion data, command-tracking accuracy over an expansive SE(2) velocity-command space, response latency, disturbance rejection, and sim-to-real transfer con-sistency. Furthermore, we isolate the efficacy of our key architectural choices through simulation-based ablation studies. These evaluations demonstrate that the learned policy preserves natural, full-body move-ment patterns by maintaining consistency with the original motion data while remaining highly responsive to user steering commands within a single, compact multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture. Crucially, the resulting policy exhibits robust generalization to a wide variety of steering inputs and command transitions that are absent or only sparsely represented in the reference motion dataset. Our com-prehensive evaluation confirms that HuMBLE bridges the gap between stylized motion and agile control, achieving robustness and command responsiveness comparable to a Tabula Rasa RL baseline optimized solely for command-tracking performance, while dramatically improv-ing full-body behavioral naturalness. Alongside our core results, we release the curated human locomo-tion dataset used in this study, which includes approximately three hours of motion capture data spanning diverse speeds, directions, and gait modalities. This dataset is provided to ensure research repro-ducibility and foster broader progress in humanoid locomotion. 

RESULTS 

This section presents the qualitative and quantitative evaluations of 

HuMBLE and summarizes the empirical findings. The evaluation is con-ducted across three humanoid platforms: the Boston Dynamics Atlas R1, Atlas D1, and Unitree G1. We first qualitatively assess the stylistic quality of the final deployment policy trained with HuMBLE , focusing on visible features such as arm swing, heel strike, heel-toe rolling, knee extension, and upper-lower body coordination. To evaluate the preser-vation of human locomotion style quantitatively, we perform a data fidelity analysis that measures the reconstruction accuracy of full-body behaviors relative to the target reference dataset, using this metric as a proxy for stylistic consistency. Subsequently, we assess the policy’s command responsiveness, command coverage, and perturbation ro-bustness through systematic, grid-search-style evaluation sweeps. We then analyze sim-to-real transfer consistency by comparing command-tracking errors in simulation against physical hardware performance across all three humanoid platforms. Beyond these core benchmark evaluations, we showcase the versatility of the learned policy across di-verse real-world deployment scenarios, including user joystick steering and integration within a hierarchical multi-policy framework, demon-strated both in the laboratory and outdoor environments. Finally, we present simulation-based ablation studies to analyze the impact of key design choices within our framework. All policies under evaluation were trained using the Isaac Lab 

[55] physics simulator. While it served as our primary sim-based quantitative evaluation environment as well, we cross-validated re-sponsiveness, command coverage and robustness evaluation using a simulation pipeline built on MuJoCo [56], which provides a high-fidelity, hardware-validated proxy that captures physical deployment constraints. 

> Qualitative Gait Characteristics

We first highlight the stylistic characteristics exhibited by the policy trained with HuMBLE during forward walking. On the Atlas R1 robot, we contrast these characteristics against two baselines: the robot’s default controller provided by Boston Dynamics, which uses footstep planning tracked by a Model Predictive Controller (MPC), and a Tabula Rasa RL policy trained without human motion priors. Figure 2 illus-trates stroboscopic snapshots of the Atlas R1 robot executing forward walk in response to a constant forward velocity steering command un-der three different controllers: the HuMBLE policy, the Tabula Rasa RL policy, and the MPC. Compared to the MPC, the HuMBLE policy exhibits visible biomimetic gait features associated with the human-motion prior, in-cluding synchronized arm swing, knee extension near stance, hip motion used to regulate torso orientation, a heel-strike-like contact transition, and heel-to-toe rolling of the stance foot. In contrast, the MPC produces stiff arm motion with the limbs remaining nearly fixed relative to the torso. While the MPC’s gait appears more strictly peri-odic, the HuMBLE policy captures the natural fluidity and whole-body coordination characteristic of human movement. Relative to the Tabula Rasa policy, the HuMBLE policy shows stronger spatiotemporal coordination between the upper and lower body, the clear presence of knee-locking, and a prominent heel-to-toe rolling during ground contact, leading to smoother momentum transfer and quieter steps. Similar gait features are observed for policies trained with HuMBLE on the Atlas D1 and Unitree G1 robots. Notably, on Atlas D1, despite its non-human foot morphology, the policy retains a heel-to-toe rolling consistent with the human-motion prior. Beyond the constant-velocity comparison presented in Figure 2, the 

HuMBLE policy also seamlessly adapts its gait style across command speeds with smooth transitions as illustrated in the video. At lower speeds, the policy adopts a flatter, shuffling gait, which transitions toward a more pronounced heel-strike and toe-off pattern as the com-manded speed increases. As evidenced by the Tabula Rasa RL and MPC baselines, such human-like characteristics do not necessarily emerge even in highly capable locomotion controllers designed for utility and robustness. > Fidelity to the Motion Dataset

Beyond visual inspection, we quantitatively assess the stylistic char-acteristics of the policy trained by HuMBLE . Because stylistic quality is inherently subjective and difficult to measure directly, we use mo-tion fidelity to the retargeted human motion data as a quantitative proxy. Since the final HuMBLE policy is not conditioned on full-body reference data, it must reconstruct full-body behavior from proprio-ceptive observations and SE(2) velocity commands alone. Thus, its deviation from the reference trajectories measures how well the policy preserves the motion patterns encoded in the dataset. We also compare the deployment policy against a policy conditioned on the full-body reference, which we refer to as the teacher policy in the next section. The teacher policy is trained using a motion-mimic framework [57] to track the reference motions while producing dynamically feasible behavior, thereby providing a meaningful upper bound on deployment performance. > Command Coverage, Responsiveness, and Robustness

In addition to preserving stylistic consistency with the human data, the 

HuMBLE policy must accurately track a broad range of steering com-mands with high responsiveness, while simultaneously maintaining robustness to external perturbations. To quantitatively evaluate these properties, we conducted a series of systematic, grid-search-style eval-uation sweeps in simulation. To provide a benchmark, we compare the 

HuMBLE policy against a Tabula Rasa RL policy trained exclusively for velocity command tracking and robustness, without the stylistic guidance or constraints of the human motion data. While this baseline may not represent a globally optimal control design, as performance can vary with alternative RL formulations, it serves as a representative reference for a hardware-deployable locomotion policy with robust and reliable velocity command tracking under the identical observation, ac-tion, and domain randomization specifications as HuMBLE . A detailed implementation setup for our Tabula Rasa RL policy is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Section S6). Figure 4 summarizes this evaluation for Atlas R1, with correspond-ing results for the other robots detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Figure S2 for Atlas D1 and Figure S3 for Unitree G1). In the first set of experiments, we perform a step-response analysis, inspired by classical control evaluation protocols, to characterize the robot’s dy-namic response across a broad command space. Evaluating a wide velocity envelope is motivated by the challenge of training a single policy to perform effectively over a diverse spectrum of speeds while maintaining high-resolution control. We evaluate the step response for each command by applying a step input to the policy. Steady state is reached when the robot’s torso velocity has settled after the step input; in practice, we take this as 3.0 s after the step input. Rise time is mea-sured as the duration from step initiation until 90% of the steady-state torso velocity is first reached. In Figure 4A, we report the relative command-tracking error, de-fined as the steady-state tracking error normalized by the command magnitude. The HuMBLE policy demonstrates errors comparable to those of the Tabula Rasa RL baseline across most of the evaluated SE(2) command space. The most significant errors are observed in command regions that are underrepresented or missing in the motion dataset, such as fast diagonal walking. For near-zero velocity com-mands, the HuMBLE policy performs favorably compared to the Tabula Rasa RL baseline. This outcome likely results from the inclusion of slow-shuffling references in the motion dataset, which provide a motion prior for small but non-zero locomotion commands that are challenging to discover through task-reward optimization alone. Regarding responsiveness, as illustrated in Figure 4B, both policies exhibit longer rise times at higher command magnitudes. The Tabula Rasa RL baseline typically achieves large commands more rapidly, while the HuMBLE policy yields smoother and more gradual responses in the high-speed regime. This pattern aligns with the acceleration profiles inherent to the human locomotion data and highlights the trade-off between naturalistic transitions and aggressive command tracking. In the final set of experiments, we evaluate robustness by perturbing the robot’s torso with planar force pushes of varying directions and magnitudes, and report whether the robot can withstand each distur-bance without falling. A fall is defined as the robot’s torso tilting excessively or the torso height falling below a threshold ( 30 ◦ along the pitch or roll axes, and 40% of the nominal standing torso height respectively, for Atlas R1). This evaluation is conducted across three scenarios: standing still, walking forward, and sidestepping. Forces are applied as continuous 0.2 s pulses once the policy reaches steady-state velocity, while the target command remains constant throughout each test case. As illustrated in Figure 4C, the HuMBLE policy withstands push magnitudes of up to 1400 N along major principal directions on At-las R1. Notably, the push robustness of the HuMBLE policy exhibits a directional dependence closely aligned with the gait kinematics. For example, during forward walking, our policy withstands stronger pushes along the longitudinal direction compared to the lateral direc-tion, primarily due to the sagittal leg swing and the effective support polygon established during forward locomotion. Application of Learned Locomotion Behaviors 

We demonstrate the utility of the HuMBLE policy across three repre-sentative scenarios: direct operator control via a joystick to command SE(2) velocity targets, autonomous navigation with obstacle and haz-ard avoidance, and a box parkour task where HuMBLE is integrated alongside separately learned climbing behaviors. These demonstra-tions are intended to show that the learned policy can serve as a low-level locomotion module driven by either direct user joystick inputs or higher-level controllers. During joystick-controlled deployments on physical hardware, the policy is stable and responsive to a broad range of commands, both in controlled lab environments and real-world settings. Notably, the pol-icy exhibits smooth emergent transitions between distinct gait patterns: slow shuffling steps for low-velocity commands, standard-pace walk-ing with nominal strides for mid-range commands, and high-velocity sprinting characterized by large strides and distinct flight phases. As the policy uses a lightweight MLP architecture, it can run suf-ficiently fast onboard for real-time control across all robot platforms considered. For the Unitree G1, we measured the average policy in-ference time to be 1.1 ms on the robot’s NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin’s CPU using ONNX Runtime 3[59]. We demonstrate the policy’s modularity and applicability to down-stream tasks by integrating it into hierarchical control stacks where a higher-level controller provides SE(2) steering commands to the 

HuMBLE policy, as illustrated in Figure 1E. In the first demonstra-tion, HuMBLE is deployed as the locomotion layer within a perceptive waypoint navigation stack, which plans around obstacles or hazardous terrain to safely reach waypoints within a warehouse environment. In the second, a high-level box parkour policy is trained to orchestrate three sub-policies: the HuMBLE policy, a box-climb-up policy, and a box-climb-down policy. Given a goal waypoint, which may be on the ground or on top of a box, the high-level policy sequences and generates steering commands for the sub-policies to move the robot to the waypoint. These demonstrations are intended to illustrate how the HuMBLE policy can be driven by higher-level controllers. While the detailed design and evaluation of the higher-level controllers them-selves are outside the scope of this work, an implementation summary of the high-level box parkour policy is outlined in the Supplemen-tary Materials (Section S7) to illustrate how the hierarchical control framework can be structured.

> Role of Teacher–Student Distillation

The objective of Stage I is to learn a prior policy that reconstructs full-body locomotion from proprioceptive observations and SE(2) steering commands in in-distribution settings. Here, in-distribution refers to rollouts in which the steering commands and reference trajectories are drawn directly from the motion dataset, meaning the commanded velocities perfectly match the intentions of the motion data. This setting poses two coupled challenges: the policy must realize kine-matic reference motions on a physical system subject to dynamics and physical constraints, while also inferring full-body coordination from SE(2) commands rather than full-body reference states. 

HuMBLE addresses these challenges through teacher–student dis-tillation. To evaluate this design choice, we compare our approach against a single-stage RL baseline that directly trains a command-conditioned policy, πbaseline (odeploy ), without a separate teacher. This baseline uses the deployment observation space and is optimized with the same tracking reward, rteacher , forcing it to reconstruct the refer-ence trajectories from proprioception and steering commands alone. For a controlled comparison, πbaseline uses the same asymmetric critic architecture and hyperparameters as the teacher-training phase. We evaluate the data-tracking fidelity of the HuMBLE prior pol-icy and this single-stage baseline on the simulated Unitree G1 robot, using the same metric as in the data-fidelity analysis. The results are summarized in Figure 5A. For several canonical motion segments extracted from the reference motion dataset, including Fast Forward Walk , Fast Backward Walk ,and Turn in Place , the single-stage baseline reproduces the reference motion but with consistently higher error than the HuMBLE prior. Across these three motions, the baseline increases the tracking error by 16.36% for linear velocity, 54.85% for angular velocity, and 70.87% for joint positions relative to the HuMBLE prior policy. The gap becomes substantially larger for Sidestep motion, a coordination-heavy behavior that requires lateral balance and precise full-body timing. In this case, the baseline fails to reconstruct the reference behavior: after taking a few initial steps, the robot settles into a near-standing posture rather than responding to the steering command. This failure leads to increases in errors of 240.87%, 80.54%, and 186.34% for linear velocity, angular velocity, and joint positions, respectively. These results indicate that single-stage partial-observation imitation can recover some locomotion modes, but is less reliable at preserving the full diversity of behaviors in the motion dataset. Teacher–student distillation improves this process by separating the two sources of difficulty: the teacher first learns a dynamically feasible realization of the full-body references in simulation, and the student then distills this behavior into the deployment observation space. This staged formulation yields a more reliable command-conditioned prior for the subsequent fine-tuning stage. 

> Efficacy of the Multi-Task RL

Stage II of HuMBLE uses a multi-task RL framework to balance two competing objectives: improving command responsiveness and cov-erage while preserving the locomotion style learned in Stage I. The long-horizon goal-conditioned task improves responsiveness and ro-bustness across a broad range of SE(2) steering commands, including those that are sparse or absent in the motion dataset. In parallel, the short-horizon reference-guided imitation task regularizes the policy to-ward the human-motion prior, preventing the fine-tuning process from sacrificing natural gait patterns to satisfy aggressive velocity targets. In the nominal HuMBLE training configuration, we allocate the par-allel simulation environments equally between these two task groups, using a 50:50 split to balance command generalization and stylistic consistency. To analyze how the two task objectives trade off command respon-siveness against motion fidelity, we sweep the allocation ratio between reference-guided and goal-conditioned environments. Starting from the same Stage I prior, we fine-tune policies with five environment splits: 0:100, 25:75, 50:50, 75:25, and 100:0, where the first value denotes the percentage of reference-guided environments and the second denotes the percentage of goal-conditioned environments. We then evaluate each policy for both reference-tracking fidelity and command tracking. The evaluation is performed across four canonical motion modes: forward walk, backward walk, sidestep, and turn in place. For reference-tracking fidelity, the policy is driven by the time-varying SE(2) commands annotated from the corresponding reference motion, and we report the DTW-aligned full-body tracking error relative to that reference trajectory. Specifically, we pair each motion modes with the same benchmark segments used in our previous experiments: 

Fast Forward Walk , Fast Backward Walk , Sidestep , and Turn in Place ,respectively. Meanwhile, for velocity command tracking, the policy is driven by a constant command associated with each mode: (1.35, 0, 0 )

for forward walk, (−1.35, 0, 0 ) for backward walk, (0, 1.35, 0 ) for sidestep, and (0, 0, 1.7 ) for turn in place. In this regime, we report the mean velocity-tracking error. Figure 5B shows how motion fidelity and command-tracking per-formance vary with the allocation ratio between reference-guided and goal-conditioned environments. Although the trends differ somewhat across motion modes, the results reveal a consistent trade-off: increas-ing the fraction of reference-guided environments improves fidelity to the motion data, whereas increasing the fraction of goal-conditioned en-vironments improves velocity command tracking. The resulting curves therefore characterize the Pareto trade-off induced by the multi-task fine-tuning objective. A detailed inspection of the extreme ratios further highlights this fundamental balance. The 0:100 policy, trained exclusively on goal-conditioned environments, rapidly loses stylistic consistency with the motion data. During forward and backward walking, it exhibits wiggly torso movement and unnatural arm swings that are inconsistent with the reference motions. Conversely, the 100:0 policy, trained solely on reference-guided environments, fails to execute the lateral command for sidestepping and falls. Because lateral steering commands are sparse in the dataset, this failure underlines the vulnerability of reference-guided policies under out-of-distribution conditions. Together, these extreme cases validate our multi-task RL design in balancing command generalization with motion quality. While simultaneously optimizing both objectives remains challeng-ing, the proposed multi-task formulation provides a controllable mech-anism for balancing stylistic consistency and command responsiveness. In Stage II, the environment allocation ratio serves as an interpretable hyperparameter that can be adjusted to match the desired deployment behavior. The nominal 50:50 allocation provides a practical middle ground, preserving motion fidelity while improving command tracking for general-purpose steerable locomotion. 

> Impact of RL Fine-Tuning

The Stage I prior policy can respond to the in-distribution steering command derived from a motion dataset, but the dataset may not cover the full command space necessary for real-world deployment under arbitrary user commands. Stage II of HuMBLE aims to address this by improving the command coverage of the final policy for robust execution under arbitrary steering commands. In this final ablation study, we evaluate the command coverage of the final deployment policy after Stage II RL fine-tuning and di-rectly compare its performance with that of the Stage I prior policy. Following the step-response evaluation methodology established in our primary command coverage analysis, we apply a series of target SE(2) velocity step inputs to both policies on a simulated Unitree G1 robot and measure the steady-state command tracking error. Figure 5C illustrates the comparative results of this evaluation. The Stage I prior policy maintains a reasonable level of command-tracking proficiency in regions where the underlying motion-data distribution is dense. However, its performance degrades rapidly in regions where the reference data is sparse or absent. Specifically, across several high-velocity command regimes, the prior policy fails to respond robustly to the steering command, resulting in dynamic instability and frequent falls. Most notably, this degradation becomes particularly pronounced during lateral motion paired with yawing—the sparsest region in our dataset, as visualized in Figure 7—which inevitably leads to falls. Con-versely, the fine-tuned deployment policy exhibits consistently lower tracking errors and vastly expanded command coverage across the entire operational space. Notably, across all test cases, the deployment policy experiences no falls and reliably generates locomotion behavior associated with the target velocity commands without collapsing into unnatural gaits. These results demonstrate that teacher–student distillation alone is insufficient to achieve the high level of responsiveness and broad command-space coverage required for real-world deployment. Instead, executing the multi-task RL fine-tuning phase in Stage II is critical to ensuring that the humanoid locomotion policy functions robustly across general-purpose locomotion tasks. 

DISCUSSION 

The goal of this work is to develop a humanoid locomotion controller that simultaneously achieves two distinct criteria: first, preserving the stylistic quality and natural transitions of human movement; second, maintaining responsiveness to user steering commands across a wide operational envelope. The HuMBLE framework targets these objectives by combining a newly curated human motion dataset with a two-stage learning pipeline. Our motion dataset captures a highly diverse reper-toire of human locomotion, providing the data density necessary to span wide variations in steering speeds and omnidirectional headings. Employing this dataset as a foundation, our two-stage training pipeline decouples style acquisition from command-space expansion. Initially, it focuses on internalizing and reconstructing the complex full-body Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 12 

behaviors solely from proprioception and the velocity command. Sub-sequently, the framework expands the command coverage beyond the original dataset’s support. Notably, it achieves this while maintain-ing the stylistic quality established in the prior policy. Because style is preserved by an explicit reference-tracking reward rather than a learned or latent style objective, the balance between the style and command-tracking objectives remains directly interpretable. Extensive empirical evaluations across three distinct humanoid plat-forms validate that HuMBLE successfully bridges the gap between stylistic quality and responsive command tracking across a wide spec-trum of steering inputs. The final deployment policies consistently reproduce distinct biological features such as synchronized arm swing-ing, knee-locking, and heel-strike transitions, which remain difficult to elicit through reward engineering alone. Furthermore, our quantita-tive analysis and ablation study demonstrate that HuMBLE effectively mitigates the trade-off between stylistic fidelity to motion data and high responsiveness across a broad command space, providing a reli-able option for direct command control in general-purpose locomotion tasks. HuMBLE embeds these capabilities into a single compact MLP network with minimal memory overhead and low computational com-plexity. This lightweight architecture streamlines the deployment of natural, robust behaviors directly onto physical robotic hardware across diverse real-world scenarios. In summary, the key contributions of HuMBLE span from the com-pilation and release of a large-scale human motion dataset curated for learning naturalistic locomotion behaviors, through a structured two-stage learning framework designed to decouple and optimize style preservation alongside command expansion, to a rigorous evaluation methodology devised to benchmark the data fidelity, command cover-age, responsiveness, and robustness of data-driven humanoid policies, as demonstrated via stable, steerable locomotion across diverse physi-cal robotic platforms. Despite these contributions, several limitations remain, which con-versely present promising opportunities for future research. First, our results are restricted to standard humanoid locomotion on flat terrain environments. Although an immediate extension to mild terrain rough-ness is feasible through more extensive domain randomization, scaling to more general locomotion scenarios, such as staircase climbing [60] or executing whole-body movements like climbing [61] or vaulting over obstacles [53, 62] presents an open challenge. These advanced locomotion modalities require a new, dedicated set of human motion references that capture these agile behaviors. They also necessitate training a policy conditioned on terrain observations, which requires the integration of exteroceptive sensory inputs at deployment. Integrating these diverse behavioral and sensory modalities to explore the bound-aries of our two-stage learning framework represents a compelling direction for future work. Second, HuMBLE requires a command annotation process to train a steerable policy conditioned on velocity commands. In this work, we addressed this via post-processing after dataset collection, estimating the performer’s movement intentions and annotating each motion frame with the estimated SE(2) velocity command. While this approach is relatively straightforward for SE(2) commands, which can be directly computed from the reference trajectory itself, it may not scale easily to more complicated steering modes. For instance, incorporating high-level semantic annotations, such as natural language command inputs [42, 48, 63], may require manual labeling or more advanced captioning techniques. Third, in our experiments, we deliberately chose a simple MLP with a modest number of learnable parameters to minimize computational and memory overhead at deployment. Our results demonstrate that this compact architecture is sufficient to internalize and reconstruct full-body behaviors from velocity command inputs, capturing the un-derlying distribution of a motion dataset containing N = 528, 214 

frames, which is equivalent to approximately three hours of human locomotion data. However, we leave a rigorous scalability analysis of our framework using this simple architecture to future work. De-termining how the number of parameters affects policy performance, identifying the capacity limits of the chosen architecture, and mapping the exact boundary conditions under which the policy fails to capture the data distribution all remain open questions. We are particularly interested in evaluating the capacity of the HuMBLE framework, when constrained to a simple network architecture, to capture more diverse human motion behaviors beyond locomotion tasks. Specifically, we intend to explore more complex scenarios, such as whole-body loco-manipulation or humanoid teleoperation, leveraging natural skill sets learned from larger-scale human motion datasets across diverse be-havioral modalities. Whether a simple MLP can accommodate these extensions, and whether our two-stage learning pipeline can effectively capture data distributions of this complexity to develop steerable poli-cies with extended command modalities, will serve as central research questions in our future work. Beyond locomotion, we view HuMBLE as a general recipe for turn-ing human motion data into command-driven policies. The framework makes few assumptions specific to locomotion and it can be applied to broader applications whenever the following ingredients are available: a motion dataset, a command signal that can be annotated onto each frame, and a reward that measures tracking of that command. In prac-tice, this means that once the comparatively simpler task of training a motion imitation policy has been addressed for a given robot, the setup can be extended to a command-conditioned policy using the proposed recipe. Other under-constrained command modalities are a natural next step, such as teleoperation from sparse hand and base inputs, where the policy must reconstruct full-body behavior, much as HuMBLE does for SE(2) locomotion commands. 

MATERIALS AND METHODS 

Figure 6 summarizes the HuMBLE training pipeline, which leverages retargeted human locomotion references to train a hardware-deployable, command-conditioned locomotion policy. The pipeline is structured into two sequential training stages and comprises three policy instances. In Stage I, a full-body reference-conditioned teacher policy, πteacher , is trained to track kinematic references from the dataset. Teacher–student distillation is then used to train a prior policy, πprior , that observes only the deployment inputs: proprioception and SE(2) velocity commands. In Stage II, the prior policy is fine-tuned into the final deployment policy, πdeploy , using a multi-task RL formulation split into two con-current task groups: a short-horizon reference-guided imitation task and a long-horizon goal-conditioned command-tracking task. The resulting policy maps deployment observations onto joint-position tar-gets, which are tracked by joint-level PD controllers. In the following sections, we describe the motion dataset and command annotations, the deployment policy architecture, the technical details of each training stage, and the hardware deployment procedure. 

> Dataset Collection and Command Annotation

We collect high-quality human motion references using a Vicon motion capture system [64]. Motion data is captured at a frame rate of 120 Hz 

utilizing a marker set and a character rig calibrated to the actor’s spe-cific anthropometry and range of motion. The raw marker trajectories are post-processed by correcting missing marker data via interpolation and skeletal kinematic constraints, followed by filtering to eliminate high-frequency noise. Finally, the character rig is mapped to the refined marker data and exported in the Biovision Hierarchy (BVH) format, providing a standardized skeletal animation foundation. Fig. 6. Overview of the HuMBLE framework. Our approach trains the final control policy πdeploy through a two-stage process. Stage I em-ploys a teacher–student distillation pipeline to extract a natural locomotion prior πprior . Specifically, a teacher policy πteacher trained to track full-body reference motions is distilled into the student policy πprior , which maps only proprioceptive observations and a SE(2) command to joint-level actions. In Stage II, the prior policy is fine-tuned via multi-task RL to enhance responsiveness to arbitrary user steering commands while preserving the stylistic qualities of the reference motion data, ultimately forming the final deployment policy πdeploy .Subsequently, we convert the BVH animations into whole-body robot motion trajectories tailored for the target robotic system through a two-stage motion retargeting process. In the first stage, the BVH anima-tions are mapped to a kinematic proxy skeleton featuring a human-like joint topology with spherical joints, but with link proportions matched to those of the target robot. Alignment frames are defined on source skeleton segments at the head, torso, upper arms, lower arms, hands, pelvis, knees, and feet. We formulate this retargeting as a spacetime optimization problem that minimizes tracking errors and constraint violations between these source frames and their counterparts on the proxy skeleton by solving for the proxy joint poses across all timesteps simultaneously [65]. Additionally, we temporally warp the trajectory by scaling its time duration by a factor equal to the square root of the spatial scaling factor, preserving dynamic consistency under a nominal gravitational acceleration of 9.81 m/s 2.The second stage applies kinematic retargeting to map the resulting proxy motions onto the target robot’s morphology. To do so, we mini-mize constraint violations and regularization costs across all timesteps. Similar to the first stage, alignment frames are placed on the target robot to specify spacetime constraints that guide retargeting. To regu-larize under-constrained joints and links, small penalties are imposed to minimize joint velocity and maintain a neutral posture. Finally, collision primitives are specified on the robot and the environment, with non-penetration constraints imposed to prevent self-collisions and ground interpenetration. The resulting reference motions encompass diverse locomotion behaviors ranging from slow-paced walking to high-speed sprinting with varied headings. Stage I: Training the Prior Policy 

Stage I focuses on training the prior policy, πprior , which internalizes and reconstructs the human motion patterns present in the dataset. Be-cause the final deployment policy, πdeploy , is obtained by fine-tuning 

πprior (as detailed in Stage II), both πdeploy and πprior have identi-cal neural network architectures and share the same observation (i.e.,

Therefore, πprior must be capa-ble of reconstructing full-body motions consistent with the reference dataset from only proprioception and SE(2) velocity commands. To address this partial observability, we employ a teacher–student distilla-tion approach. A teacher policy, πteacher , is trained using an RL-based motion imitation framework [57], with access to privileged full-body reference states from the motion dataset, to generate actions that drive the robot to match the reference trajectory as closely as possible. Sub-sequently, the prior policy, πprior , is trained via imitation learning to match the actions of πteacher . The resulting πprior learns to map the non-privileged deployment observations to actions that produce reference-consistent motions for states in-distribution of the dataset.

This training setup enables a single-teacher policy to reliably replicate the diverse locomotion behaviors in our dataset. A value of zero indicates that the steering command lies within the dataset support (in-distribution), while a value of one corresponds to out-of-distribution commands. Throughout Stage I teacher training, this value remains strictly zero, as all steering commands are derived directly from the reference motion data. However, this indicator is dynamically modulated during the subsequent multi-task fine-tuning phase in Stage II. > Prior Policy Training via Teacher–Student Distillation

Once the teacher policy is trained to reliably produce reference motions conditioned on the full-body reference input, we train the prior policy, 

πprior , using teacher–student distillation [45, 47, 48]. This process is a variant of the DAgger algorithm [54], where the student policy (in our framework, the prior policy) generates rollout data that are subsequently labeled with the actions the teacher policy would have taken at the same state. We therefore collect rollouts using πprior (odeploy ), and for each state encountered during these rollouts, πteacher (oteacher ) acts as an expert to provide the ground-truth action labels. We then optimize the prior policy in a supervised learning fashion, minimizing the difference between the prior policy’s actions and the teacher’s labels:

This constraint forces the prior policy to internalize the motion priors and recovery strategies orig-inally guided by the teacher’s privileged reference observation. By distilling the teacher’s expertise, the prior policy learns to exhibit natu-ral, high-fidelity locomotion solely from proprioceptive feedback and SE(2) velocity commands. 

Stage II: Fine-tuning via Multi-Task RL 

The prior policy trained in Stage I maintains high fidelity to the motion dataset. However, it struggles with out-of-distribution velocity com-mands that are underrepresented in the motion dataset. For example, diagonal strafing combined with a fast turning rate is within the space of commands reachable via a joystick but uncommon in human locomo-tion. To address this, Stage II focuses on expanding command coverage Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 16 

and enhancing the policy’s robustness by fine-tuning the prior policy to track velocity commands drawn from a distribution that matches the operational range of interest. However, naively fine-tuning the policy with a reward that incen-tivizes tracking arbitrary velocity commands typically leads to style collapse, in which the policy compromises the natural movement pat-terns learned in Stage I to better track those commands. To mitigate this more effectively, we formulate Stage II as a multi-task RL problem, sharing conceptual similarities with concurrent work [61]. We take advantage of parallel training environments by partitioning the envi-ronments into two concurrent groups. The first subset of environments, 

Eref , is dedicated to a short-horizon reference-guided imitation task .Here, the policy continues to track the reference dataset, acting as a regularizer to preserve stylistic consistency acquired in the previous stage. The remaining environments, Egoal , focus on a long-horizon goal-conditioned task . In this task, the policy is optimized for robust tracking of randomly sampled velocity commands, independently of any motion-reference tracking objective. Formally, the fine-tuning reward, rfine-tune , is structured as 

rfine-tune =

(

rref if env ∈ Eref 

rgoal if env ∈ Egoal . (7) 

We design this multi-task RL setup to carefully balance stylistic consistency against command coverage, responsiveness, and robust-ness. Conceptually, the reference-guided environment acts as style-regularizing experience replay, preventing the policy from diverging into unnatural gaits while the goal-conditioned environment expands the policy’s operational envelope beyond the reference motion dataset’s distribution. In the following sections, we describe the specific design choices and reward formulations for these two environments in detail. 

> Short-horizon Reference-Guided Imitation Task

The reference-guided environments follow a setup similar to that of the teacher policy, using the same reward function rteacher presented in Equation (4) and the same initialization scheme (RSI) upon termination. The primary distinction is that the policy observes only the deployment observation space, odeploy , and has no access to the full-body reference, 

oref .In this partially observable setting, the policy must reconstruct the reference motion solely from the command and proprioception. Although the policy was pre-trained via distillation to perform this reconstruction, minor tracking errors are inevitable: because the pol-icy lacks direct observation of the reference, it cannot as effectively produce corrective actions once it deviates too far from the reference trajectory. For long episodes, these errors compound, causing the pol-icy to drift from the reference and eventually reaching states where the reference-tracking reward is no longer informative. Therefore, we deliberately set a short episode length for the reference-guided task to focus the reinforcement signal on local style preservation and prevent the optimization from being derailed by long-term cumulative drift. 

> Long-horizon Goal-Conditioned Task

The goal-conditioned environments train the agent to track randomly sampled commands. Unlike the reference-guided task, these environ-ments do not use a reference motion trajectory to compute reward. Instead, the reward function is defined as: 

rgoal = rcmd + rreg + rsurvival , (8) 

where the command tracking term, rcmd , incentivizes the agent to match the SE(2) velocity of the robot torso to the sampled command. Both the reference-guided imitation and goal-conditioned tasks share a common set of reward components, specifically the regularization rewards, rreg , and the survival rewards, rsurvival , alongside a common set of termination criteria such as terminating upon excessively large contact forces. These elements are identical to those utilized during teacher training. For a comprehensive breakdown of the reward for-mulations and termination criteria, we refer readers to Section S4 and Table S4 of the Supplementary Materials, respectively. We set a longer episode length for the goal-conditioned environ-ments, allowing commands to be resampled within an episode to expose the policy to these transitions. In addition to the standard termination criteria, an episode is terminated if the robot falls into an unrecoverable state and cannot continue tracking the command. For initializing goal-conditioned rollouts, we introduce a scheme named Goal-Conditioned Reference-State Initialization (GC-RSI), which is detailed in the subse-quent section. 

> Goal-Conditioned Reference State Initialization

In GC-RSI, the target velocity command is used to retrieve a motion frame from the dataset with a similar annotated command. To facilitate efficient retrieval, we precompute a reference-state lookup table by binning reference states according to their annotated commands. Given a query command, we sample a reference frame from its corresponding bin and randomly pick a candidate state from a temporal window centered on that frame. Finally, to encourage broader exploration, we apply a small perturbation to this state to produce the final initial state. This command-conditioned initialization mechanism serves two critical purposes. First, it anchors fine-tuning near states that remain within the learned behavioral prior’s support. Since πprior has already learned to control motions drawn from the dataset distribution, initial-izing from a reference state whose annotated command is close to the sampled command allows the policy to rely on its existing prior at the beginning of each episode. This prevents the policy from immediately drifting into out-of-distribution states or developing an isolated behav-ior mode unrelated to the reference motions. Instead, GC-RSI provides a gradual pathway for expanding the policy’s command coverage while preserving the stylistic and dynamical structure acquired during prior training. Second, GC-RSI prevents severe mismatches between the commanded velocity and the initial state. This is particularly crucial for high-magnitude commands; forcing the robot to accelerate aggres-sively from a mismatched pose can cause the episode to terminate prematurely before meaningful learning can occur. 

> Training Details for Multi-Task Fine-Tuning

We once again employ PPO [67] for the RL-based fine-tuning stage. However, initializing PPO directly from the teacher–student distilled prior policy, πprior , introduces a cold-start problem: randomly initial-izing the PPO critic network can rapidly erode the stylistic nuances learned during prior training as the actor is updated using noisy, un-reliable value estimates from an untrained critic. To address this, we initialize the fine-tuning process by loading both the policy network of 

πprior and the critic network from the teacher training stage, Vteacher .Because Vteacher was trained to approximate the value function asso-ciated with the teacher policy πteacher , rather than πprior , it must be adapted before actual multi-task fine-tuning. We therefore perform an initial value-function warm-start phase of 5000 iterations on the reference-guided task ( Vteacher → Vprior ), training only the critic while keeping the policy fixed. Once this adaptation phase is complete, we transition to full multi-task RL to jointly train the deployment policy and critic networks ( πprior → πdeploy and Vprior → Vdeploy ). Because the reward functions differ between the goal-conditioned and reference-guided tasks, the critic must predict task-appropriate returns. To achieve this, we leverage the data support indicator obser-vation osup ∈ { 0, 1 } introduced in Equation (5), which is provided exclusively to the critic network as a context signal to distinguish Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 17 

the active task domain. While this indicator is kept strictly at zero throughout Stage I teacher training, during Stage II fine-tuning, it is specified by the environment type: it remains zero in short-horizon reference-guided environments and is set to one in long-horizon goal-conditioned environments. This contextual modulation allows the critic network to effectively learn a dual value function conditioned on the task support, yielding more accurate on-policy value estimations. Moreover, we separately normalize the critic’s estimated advantages for the reference-guided and goal-conditioned environments, an approach similarly employed in RobotKeyframing [69] to address potential scale differences between the two reward functions. Finally, to further safeguard the internalized stylistic characteristics against large policy updates, we lower the policy entropy coefficient, and utilize a small learning rate compared to the previous stage. 

Hardware Deployment 

Following the two-stage training process, the policy is deployed to the robot hardware zero-shot, serving as a steerable locomotion controller that guides the robot to exhibit human-like behaviors in response to real-time joystick commands. For deployment with a joystick, the steering inputs are processed to bound their rate of change, as we found this resulted in a smoother user driving experience at some expense of the policy’s responsiveness. The linear velocity observation for the policy relies on the plat-form’s onboard state estimator. The Boston Dynamics Atlas robots are equipped with a robust state estimator that provides reliable linear-velocity feedback in both indoor and outdoor environments. However, deploying the G1 robot in unstructured outdoor environments can in-troduce significant noise and drift into the velocity estimation. To mitigate potential system failure in these scenarios, we trained an alter-native policy variant that excludes linear velocity observations. This linear-velocity-free policy ensures stable operation, albeit at the cost of reduced velocity-tracking precision relative to the target command.

> Section S2. Motion Dataset Details
> Dataset Composition and Choreography

We collected an in-house motion dataset with choreography designed to cover the broad envelope of SE(2) torso velocity space that humans experience during natural locomotion on flat ground. Data was collected at five different speed categories: slow walking, normal walking, fast walking, jogging, and sprinting. Within each of these speed categories, various dance cards were specified and reused, ensuring consistent coverage of SE(2) trajectories across speeds. These dance cards were selected to capture an overlapping set of turning radii and angles, as well as distinct human heading-control strategies, such as heading-aligned walking and locked heading, where an actor maintains a constant body orientation. The primary structured tracking scripts include: • Discrete directional turns: Changes in heading executed across an eight-pointed star configuration ( 0◦, ±45 ◦, ±90 ◦, ±135 ◦, and 180 ◦). • Continuous spline tracking: Spiral trajectories designed to sweep through a continuous range of curvature radii during constant-velocity locomotion. • Linear maneuvers: Straight-line initialization, abrupt terminations, directional reversals, and lateral strafing. • Zero-velocity pivots: Turning in place to execute heading changes at zero linear velocity, initiating and terminating at the same eight cardinal orientations. Throughout the development of the HuMBLE framework, additional data were collected iteratively to address observed gaps in the policy’s capabilities due to data sparsity. For example, if sidestepping performance was sub-optimal, additional sidestepping data were collected to provide more examples. In addition to these structured takes, unstructured data were collected to capture natural transitions between speeds, turns, stopping, and starting that are not inherently present in the structured scripts. These transitions were elicited by generating a randomized sequence of prompt commands (e.g. start, stop, turn, walk fast, slow, etc.) that were vocalized to the actor at randomized intervals. This protocol ensured high data entropy and prevented bias from the actor’s subconscious movement preferences during transition. A variety of these takes were gathered under different constraints, including restricted headings, speed limits, and directional boundaries (such as straight-line locomotion versus unconstrained turning). While not exhaustive, this dataset provided broad coverage of trajectories representative of standard joystick-based steering. The captured behaviors were partially constrained by the dimensions of the motion capture volume. To maximize spatial efficiency within these boundaries, choreography was optimized by separating left and right turns into distinct takes to enable larger turning radii, and by clearly marking the volume perimeter so the actor could fully utilize the available capture area. Beyond standard locomotion, additional data were captured to cover tasks such as stepping onto boxes of various sizes, box manipulation, reaching for goals while constrained to a small footprint, push recoveries, and exaggerated walk style variations. Although these motions are not utilized for HuMBLE , we plan to release the dataset to the public to support research on more diverse behaviors. 

> Post-Process

All trajectories are mirrored across the sagittal plane to enforce bilateral symmetry, providing isotropic coverage of left and right movements while simultaneously doubling the effective size of the dataset. Additionally, due to the inherent sensor noise and tracking artifacts occasionally present in optical motion capture systems, foot bones can exhibit slight misalignments, making the precise timing of ground contact events ambiguous. To address this issue, the ground plane and the corresponding sole positions are estimated based on the kinematic configuration and foot height relative to the coordinate origin during periods when the actor is empirically determined to be stationary. These estimated contact-aligned bone segments are appended directly to the dataset’s BVH files. 

> Temporal Adjustment

The average height of the motion capture actor was 178 cm . While Boston Dynamics Atlas robots roughly match these dimensions, many modern commercial humanoid platforms, including Unitree G1, are designed at approximately a 0.8 × geometric scale relative to the actor. To facilitate immediate deployment on smaller humanoids, a pre-scaled variant of the dataset is provided alongside the raw sequences. Under this scaling, all linear metrics (including positions, offsets, and segment dimensions) are uniformly scaled such that 1 m in the original data maps to 0.8 m in the scaled variant. However, spatial scaling alone can compromise the physical realism of the motions, as it implicitly alters the effective gravitational acceleration governing the system dynamics. Naively retargeting this spatially scaled data degrades tracking fidelity during the subsequent policy training phase in physical simulation; this drawback is particularly pronounced in motions featuring frequent flight phases such as sprinting. As a straightforward countermeasure, we temporally warp the motion trajectories by scaling the original frame rate by a factor of 1/ √0.8 . This temporal scaling preserves the nominal value of gravitational acceleration g across the scaled kinematics. To illustrate this rationale, consider a simplified free-fall scenario where the vertical trajectory is governed by the equation of motion, z = 12 gt 2. When spatial scaling is applied, the modified vertical trajectory becomes (0.8 )z = 12 gt ′2 where t′ is the scaled motion duration. Substituting the original expression yields (0.8 )gt 2 = gt ′2,which results in the required temporal warp, t′ = √0.8 t.Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 22

> Domain Randomization

To facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and foster robust recovery behaviors, we employ domain randomization. This includes injecting Gaussian noise into the actor’s proprioceptive observations, applying random impulsive perturbations to the torso, and varying environmental parameters such as surface friction. Furthermore, we randomize the robot’s physical properties, such as link masses, center-of-mass (CoM) offsets, joint friction coefficients, and joint-zero position offsets. The domain randomizations used can differ for various robots, for example, high stiction in the parallel linkage mechanism on the ankle of Atlas R1 resulted in the use of the joint friction randomization. Beyond bridging the reality gap, these randomizations expose the agent to a broader distribution of states during training. This prevents the policy from overfitting to the narrow state-space of the reference trajectories, thereby enhancing its capability to recover from significant deviations and environmental uncertainties. A comprehensive list of the domain randomization parameters and their respective ranges used across our experiments is summarized in Table S5. We note that during initial hardware deployment of the final policy on the Unitree G1 robot, the emergent ankle behaviors exhibited excessive stiffness. While these behaviors were not problematic in simulation, they proved brittle in real-world environments; specifically, the lack of ankle compliance led to frequent stumbling when the foot failed to strike the ground at a nominal landing angle. To mitigate this, we introduced additional domain randomization in terrain roughness. By forcing the agent to traverse uneven surfaces, we successfully increased the diversity of the roll-out samples, exposing the policy to a much wider range of ankle angles and impact conditions. Terrain roughness randomization is implemented by uniformly sampling random height offsets for each grid point at a resolution of 0.1 m . The continuous terrain mesh is subsequently generated by interpolating these grid points. The specific ranges for these terrain offsets used in our G1 policy train are detailed in Table S5. To prevent domain randomization from compromising the stylistic fidelity of the learned behaviors, training begins on a flat surface, allowing the teacher policy to prioritize the acquisition of high-fidelity stylistic nuances from the motion dataset without the interference of environmental noise. Then we introduce terrain randomization only after the midpoint of the teacher policy’s training phase, maintaining it through the remainder of the optimization process. Additionally, to streamline the policy training, we provide the terrain height observation oterrain to the critic network as privileged information. This observation samples terrain heights from a 17 × 11 grid centered at the robot with 0.1 m spacing between sample points. The resulting heightmap is then flattened into a vector before being provided to the critic. 

> Section S6. Tabula Rasa Policy

The Tabula Rasa RL policy used for benchmarking shares the same observation and action spaces as the final deployable HuMBLE policy. It maps proprioceptive observations and SE(2) velocity commands directly to joint actions, generating locomotion behaviors that track the target velocity. Unlike our proposed method, these baseline policies are trained without prior human motion data, relying on emergent locomotion behaviors shaped purely by hand-crafted reward functions. In this section, we provide a high-level overview of the training and implementation details for the Tabula Rasa RL policy as implemented on the Unitree G1 robot. Similar to HuMBLE training setup, the G1 Tabula Rasa training also employs an Asymmetric Actor-Critic architecture, where the critic network is provided with additional privileged observation to accelerate the training. These privileged observations include the robot’s base height, the positions and velocities of the feet relative to the base frame, and the feet wrenches resulting from joint torques. Additionally, we introduce a binary command activation indicator, which is set to zero when the velocity commands are zero, and set to one otherwise. This indicator helps train the policy to stand still rather than step in place when the velocity command is zero. At a high-level, the reward function of Tabula Rasa policy is structured as rtabula rasa = rcmd + rreg + rsurvival where the components represent the command tracking reward, regularization reward and survival reward respectively. Among these terms, survival term is defined as a constant positive value, similar to the HuMBLE RL training rewards, and the command tracking reward is designed to be identical to that of the goal-conditioned task during HuMBLE fine-tuning stage. Both the magnitude of the survival term and the tracking subterm weights are adjusted to align with the landscape of the overall Tabula Rasa reward function. The regularization reward of Tabula Rasa , by contrast, is considerably more complex than its HuMBLE counterpart, as it is solely responsible for shaping the behavior of the policy. These regularization terms include penalties on torso vertical, roll, and pitch movements, as well as deviations from an upright posture. At the joint level, motion is regularized by penalizing deviations from a nominal configuration, alongside soft constraints on joint positions to prevent action-induced joint limit violations. To encourage smooth stepping patterns while preventing stomping or stance-foot slippage, penalties are imposed on foot acceleration and jerk, coupled with a foot-slide penalty. Furthermore, the yaw of the feet relative to the torso frame is restricted to prevent excessive twisting, which is complemented by a term encouraging the feet to remain flat relative to the ground. Joint torques and joint accelerations are also penalized to prevent excessive actuator stress, mitigate hardware damage, and reduce energy consumption. Lastly, to encourage a natural leg swing, two additional sub-terms incentivize longer swing foot air time and a foot clearance profile targeting a maximum swing height of 0.15 m .Because the Tabula Rasa policy does not utilize a motion dataset, we omit the assistive wrench curriculum, which depends on tracking deviations from a reference trajectory. Instead, we implement a curriculum that progressively expands the velocity command sampling range. This adaptive curriculum broadens the command space when an environment exhibits high tracking fidelity to previously sampled velocity commands, and contracts the range otherwise. For the same reason, episode initialization is designed to reset the robot to its nominal joint configuration with randomized joint position deviations sampled from U (−0.2, 0.2 ) rad and joint velocity deviations from U (−0.2, 0.2 ) rad/s .The domain randomization settings for the Tabula Rasa policy training are identical to those maintained across the entire HuMBLE training pipeline. The termination conditions match the goal-conditioned task during HuMBLE fine-tuning stage, while the PPO hyperparameters are set to the same values used during the HuMBLE teacher policy training. 

> Section S7. Autonomous Box Parkour

In this section, we present the implementation details of the autonomous box parkour pipeline, showcased as an extended use case of the 

HuMBLE policy. The objective of this pipeline is to enable the robot to navigate box parkour environments, including unseen configurations, toward a defined SE(2) goal pose while producing natural motions. The task requires the robot to perform three behaviors: walking, climbing up, and Research Article RAI Institute & Boston Dynamics 25 

climbing down. To achieve this, we adopt a perceptive multi-task hierarchical RL framework utilizing a mixture-of-experts technique. In this setup, a high-level policy orchestrates separately learned motor behaviors to guide the robot through the environment toward its goal. The perceptive high-level policy outputs, at 10 Hz , SE(2) velocity commands (vlong , vlat , ωturn ) and a set of gating weights over three frozen, pretrained low-level expert policies: a HuMBLE policy, a box climb-up policy, and a box climb-down policy. The experts each run at 50 Hz and output joint position targets. The SE(2) velocity command is low-pass filtered to prevent abrupt changes and is provided to each expert in its required representation: the HuMBLE policy receives the velocity command directly, whereas the climb-up and climb-down experts receive an SE(2) pose waypoint obtained by integrating the velocity command. Each expert’s outputs are then combined as a weighted average, using gating weights normalized to sum to one, to yield the final action. Note that only the high-level policy is perceptive, while the low-level experts are non-perceptive. The Unitree G1 robot successfully performs a variety of long-horizon navigation tasks in environments containing one and two boxes, deployed zero-shot on hardware. The robot transitions smoothly between walking, climbing up, and climbing down, chaining these behaviors autonomously, with the high-level policy selecting and blending experts on the fly. 

> Low-level Policies Training Details

The HuMBLE policy is trained as described in the main manuscript. The box climb-up and climb-down policies (collectively, the box-climbing policies) are trained via reference guided imitation with PPO. While both the box-climbing policies and the HuMBLE teacher policy take inspiration from ZEST , their implementations differ as follows. First, in the box-climbing policies the command is defined as an SE(2) pose waypoint rather than an SE(2) velocity. At every timestep, the reference motions and their mirrored counterparts define a waypoint at a 0.5 s look-ahead horizon, representing the desired future pose expressed in the robot’s torso heading frame. Gaussian observation noise is added to the waypoint commands to account for inaccuracies in the high-level planner. Second, the full-body reference states are not provided as inputs to the box-climbing policies. This omission allows these policies to be directly deployed without distillation, and it also enables them to generalize to unseen conditions. Third, compared to the HuMBLE teacher policy, the critic observations include different privileged information such as tracking scores and a task phase variable that increments from zero to one along the reference motion trajectory. Otherwise, the reward functions, training curriculum, domain randomization schemes, and PPO hyperparameters used in the box-climbing policies closely follow the HuMBLE teacher training, with only minor differences. 

> High-level Policy Training Details

The high-level policy is trained using PPO in two stages. The first stage focuses exclusively on primitive tasks, walking, climb-up and climb-down, with each task sampled with equal probability. The second stage additionally introduces three compound tasks: walking to climb-up, climb-up to climb-down, and climb-down to walking. These compound tasks are sampled with half the probability of the primitive tasks. The policy observes proprioceptive information, the SE(2) goal pose command, and a local height scan. The critic receives additional privileged information, including the torso height and—specifically for box-climbing motions—the torso height error relative to the reference motion. All tasks incorporate regularization and goal tracking rewards, whereas the box-climbing tasks additionally include rewards based on reference torso height that encourage stylistic fidelity to the reference motions.
