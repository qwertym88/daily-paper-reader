Abstract — Humanoid robots still struggle to plan contact-rich whole-body manipulation from egocentric RGB and pro-prioception. Workhorse learns such manipulation from robot-free human demonstrations. A visual planner predicts five-link targets: the poses of the torso, both wrists, and both feet. A reinforcement-learning whole-body tracker follows them on the robot. Both policies train separately on the same recorded human poses, without retargeting. We augment the training data of each policy to imitate the errors that the other makes at deployment. On a real Unitree G1, Workhorse sorts boxes with its hands and a kick, catches a thrown box, and topples and climbs a suitcase. During box sorting, we show recoveries after a person pushes the robot or takes the box away. In a simulated copy of the demonstration room, the system completes box sorting in 77 % of episodes, and in 64 % under 40 N ·s pushes. With both policies retrained from the same demonstrations, a simulated second humanoid completes box sorting in 83 % of episodes without pushes. 

I. INTRODUCTION We want humanoid robots to interact with the world using whole-body motions, much like humans do. Humans can squat to lift a box, brace a leg against an object to push or topple it, and climb onto objects to reach higher places. Although humanoid robots can execute such whole-body motions using learned motion trackers [1, 2, 3], these methods require a whole-body reference trajectory to be specified. For manipulation tasks, however, no such reference is available, so the robot must plan its own whole-body motion to perform the task. Doing so from only egocentric RGB observations and proprioception is challenging, and becomes even more difficult when the robot must move dynamically, respond to changes in the scene, and recover from unexpected perturbations. The robot can learn to plan whole-body motions for ma-nipulation tasks from demonstrations. Teleoperation captures

However, in most tasks studied with either approach, the feet are primarily used for locomotion rather than as active tools for manipulation. We present Workhorse (Fig. 1), which learns whole-body loco-manipulation from robot-free human demonstrations. A visual planner and a whole-body tracker (Fig. 2) commu-nicate through a shared interface consisting of the poses of the torso, both wrists, and both feet. During training, the poses come directly from human demonstrations, without requiring retargeting to a robot. We demonstrate Workhorse on a real Unitree G1 across three whole-body loco-manipulation tasks: box sorting with a kick, box catching, and suitcase pushing, toppling, and climbing. Each task has its own planner and whole-body tracker. For box sorting, we demonstrate recovery from per-turbations such as a person pushing the robot or removing the box. RELATED WORK This section reviews whole-body controllers, recording devices, and policies learned from human demonstrations, and each part ends with what Workhorse does differently. 

A. Humanoid Whole-Body Controllers 

Motion trackers reproduce a whole-body reference mo-tion [1, 2, 3]. Some tolerate external disturbances [9] or noisy reference motions [10]. Other controllers accept a sparse or latent command [11, 12, 13, 14]. The command of all these controllers is usually a retargeted motion or a teleoperator’s input, which most of them treat as ground truth. A planner’s command drifts until the planner sends a new plan, and then it jumps. We train the whole-body tracker on such commands (§IV-C.2). 

B. Human Data Collection Devices 

Marker-based optical capture [15] is precise but expensive. The pose estimate of inertial sensors worn on the body drifts [16]. UMI [17] and DexUMI [18] put a device with a camera in the demonstrator’s hands, and each device recovers its own pose by odometry. A whole-body version needs five such devices, including one on each foot. Each device has its own drift and its own clock, and odometry from a camera on a foot is poorly conditioned. An arm exoskeleton with a back-mounted LiDAR measures the arms and the torso [19], but it constrains the demonstrator. Our rig is lightweight and quick to set up. It reports the poses of the torso, the wrists, and the feet together, in one gravity-aligned frame. 

C. Humanoid Policies from Human Demonstrations 

Teleoperation [4, 5] records the demonstration on the robot itself, so the data needs no conversion. Its interface can limit how reactive and how dynamic the demonstration is. Robot-free rigs record the poses of a few key body parts and RGB video [6, 7, 8, 20, 21]. To our knowledge, the feet only walk in the tasks reported with these rigs. Some of these systems turn the human motion into joint references for a whole-body controller by inverse kinematics or retargeting [6, 7]. Other systems retarget human motion capture or video and train a whole-body controller on it [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Some of them use the feet on objects, for example to kick a box or a door [23, 24]. Retargeting is specific to one robot, and it changes the demonstrated motion. In Workhorse, the five recorded link poses are the training target of both policies, with no retargeting (§IV-A). HUMAN DATA COLLECTION Both policies learn directly from the human demonstra-tions, so their quality limits how well the behavior transfers to the robot. This section describes the rig (§III-A), the recording protocol (§III-B), and the synchronization and processing (§III-C). The protocol splits each session into repetitions, and it perturbs only the object, never the demon-strator. 

A. Rig 

The rig tracks the hands and the feet, which make the contacts, and the chest camera, which records what the demonstrator sees. Five wearable trackers (HTC Vive) sit on the chest camera mount, the backs of the hands, and the tops of the shoes (Fig. 3(a)). Four Lighthouse base stations locate them at 60 Hz in a 5 m × 5 m area, in one gravity-aligned world frame. One DJI Osmo Action 4 on the chest records 129° near-fisheye video at 30 frames/s with a rolling shutter. It saves the video to its own SD card. The robot carries the same camera, which streams over USB at deployment. The planner therefore receives images with the same lens distortion and rolling shutter in training and at deployment. We do not remove the lens distortion from the images. 

B. Recording Protocol 

Each session is one continuous recording in which the demonstrator repeats the task. A T-pose before each repeti-tion marks where to split the session. As in HuMI [6], we solve inverse kinematics on the robot model in real time during recording. A live preview shows the result and flags a hand that is out of reach, and the demonstrator then corrects the pose (Fig. 3(b)). The camera is on the chest, so the demonstrator turns the torso to look for an object. Across sessions, we vary the lighting and the object placement. We also perturb the object during recording to widen the data coverage. We never perturb the demonstrator, so every recorded pose is intended motion. One demonstrator recorded all the data: 2.2 h for box sorting, 1.2 h for the suitcase task, and 13 min for box catching. 

C. Synchronization and Processing 

The camera and the wearable trackers record two streams on different clocks. To align them, the demonstrator claps three times at the start of each session. We compute a clap score from the distance between the two wearable trackers on the hands and from their velocity. We match its three peaks to the three clap onsets in the camera audio, which gives the clock offset (Fig. 3(c)). The match minimizes the difference between the clap intervals of the two streams. After synchronization, the two streams disagree by 5.5 ms. We resample the wearable-tracker poses from 60 Hz to 50 Hz, the rate of the whole-body tracker. We interpolate the samples that a wearable tracker misses when it briefly loses sight of the base stations. Images stay at 30 frames/s, and we select them by timestamp. After processing, each session holds the five wearable-tracker poses at 50 Hz in one world frame and the video on the same clock. IV. HUMANOID SKILL TRANSFER This section turns the human demonstrations into two policies and runs them on the robot (Fig. 2). Both policies share one interface, the recorded poses of five links (§IV-A). The visual planner trains on images edited to show the robot and on histories with added drift (§IV-B). The whole-body tracker trains on commands that drift and jump as they do at deployment (§IV-C). At deployment, timestamps align the two policies, which run at different rates and with different delays (§IV-D).

The interface is the poses of five links: the torso, the two wrists, and the two feet. It contains no joint angles, and it is the recorded human motion itself. If the whole-body tracker follows it exactly, the robot reproduces the demonstrated motion, and its camera follows the demonstrator’s camera trajectory.

The estimator is a Kalman filter that fuses the contact kinematics of the legs with the measured acceleration. The planner replans at 5 Hz, so t − t0 stays short, but the estimate still drifts. We train the whole-body tracker on this drift (§IV-C.2).

1) Observation, Output, and Architecture: The planner reads an image history and the five-link history, which both end at t0, the capture time of the newest image. The image history has four images, 0.24 s apart. The five-link history has nine samples of the five link poses, 0.12 s apart, in the torso frame of t0. Each pose, in the history and in the chunk, is a position and a 6D rotation, the first two columns of the rotation matrix. Poses in the torso frame do not show how the torso tilts or how high it is. Each sample of the five-link history therefore also includes the gravity direction in its own torso frame, which we call projected gravity. Inference takes 10 Euler steps.

3) Planner Augmentation: At each history time, the drift augmentation moves the five links as one rigid body, by a rigid motion in the world frame. Along the history, this motion follows a random walk: it is the identity at t0 and accumulates random steps toward older times. The steps are larger when the torso moves more over the history. The motion also tilts the torso, so projected gravity and the torso height follow it. Each wrist and each foot also follows a smaller random walk of its own. The chunk stays clean (Fig. 6(a)). The camera is fixed to the torso, so the rigid motion also moves the camera. The camera motion is the camera pose after the drift, expressed in the camera frame before the drift. We warp each image of the history by the camera motion at its own time (Fig. 5(c)). To first order, the interaction matrix [33] maps the camera motion to a 2D shift of each pixel. This shift also depends on the metric depth of the pixel, which comes from §IV-B.2. Each pixel of the warped image copies the original image at the pixel’s position minus its shift. The background of the images therefore moves consistently with the five-link history. Each training sample also receives one random change of brightness, contrast, and lighting gradient, shared by its four images (Fig. 5(b)). We also mask samples and links of the five-link history at random, so the planner has to use the images. 

C. Whole-Body Tracker 

The whole-body tracker turns the five-link command into joint setpoints. It has to reach the commanded wrist poses and keep the robot balanced when the command drifts or a person pushes the robot. It is a reinforcement-learning policy that builds on the motion tracker of BeyondMimic [1]. The five-link command holds six samples of the chunk, 0.04 s apart, from the current time t to t+0 .2 s . 1) Reward: Five links do not determine the rest of the body, so the reward needs a whole-body pose. Before train-ing, an inverse-kinematics (IK) solve places the robot on the recorded five-link targets, with every stance foot flat on the floor. This solve is plain IK and not retargeting: it does not scale the motion, and its solution need not be consistent over time. The joints can even flip between two time steps. We accept this because the IK pose serves only the style reward, the start state of each episode, and the image edit of §IV-B.2. The reward is a sum of Gaussian kernels with unit weight (Table I), minus penalties on the rate of the joint setpoints and on joint-limit violations. The style reward follows Be-yondMimic: it scores all 14 links of the IK pose and keeps the motion natural. The task reward scores the torso and the wrists in the world frame, so the wrists reach the commanded poses. It scores the feet in the torso frame, so the robot can step where it needs to for balance. A clearance kernel rewards 10 cm of ankle height whenever the recorded foot swings. 

2) Command Augmentation: At deployment, the com-mand comes from a chunk, moved into the current torso frame with the onboard state estimate (§IV-A.2). Until the planner replans, the command drifts with the errors of the state estimate, the planner, and the tracker. The new chunk comes from a new image, so the command jumps back at the replan. Training reproduces this drift and jump on the recorded trajectory (Fig. 6(b)). In training, a replan occurs at a random interval of 0.2 s to 1 s. Between two replans, one rigid drift of the five-link command grows linearly from zero. It reaches at most 10 cm of horizontal shift and 10° of yaw about the recorded torso. Each sample of the command carries the drift extrapolated to its own time. We compute the reward against the drifted command, because the tracker cannot separate drift from intended motion. The planner corrects the resulting error at its next replan.

D. Deployment 

The planner replans at 5 Hz, and the tracker runs at 50 Hz. The camera and the state estimator deliver their values with different delays, and the inference adds its own. Following UMI [17], we measure each sensor delay once and stamp every value with the time that it describes (Fig. 7). Action k

of a chunk, counted from zero, has the timestamp t0 + k∆t.When a chunk arrives, its first actions are already in the past, and the tracker skips them. At every control step t,the tracker interpolates the chunk at the six sample times of its command. It uses linear interpolation in position and spherical linear interpolation in rotation. It then moves the result into the torso frame of t (§IV-A.2). The planner infers consecutive chunks independently, so they can disagree where they overlap. We move the overlap-ping actions of the previous chunk into the torso frame of the new t0. Following real-time chunking [36], we inpaint them into the new chunk during the flow integration, so the new chunk continues the previous one. With the timestamps and the inpainted overlap, the planner can run slower than the tracker and deliver its chunks late. Together, the shared interface, the image edit, the two augmentations, and the timestamps let two separately trained policies run as one system. V. A. Whole-Body Capability 

On the real Unitree G1, the whole-body tracker runs onboard and the planner runs on an external laptop. The robot uses no external tracking, and the planner reads the five-link history from the robot’s own proprioception. The real-robot experiments demonstrate capability, and we report success rates only in simulation (§V-B). The three tasks share the interface, the training procedure, and the deployment software. Each task has its own planner and tracker, both trained on that task’s data. 

Box sorting. The robot walks to a box, squats, grasps it with both hands, and carries it to the shelf (Fig. 1(a)). It places two boxes on the upper shelf this way, straightens them, and kicks the third box into the bottom shelf. One episode handles the three boxes one after another without a reset. A person pushes and kicks the robot during the task (Fig. 8). In each kick and push shown, the whole-body tracker keeps the robot on its feet, and the planner continues the task. When a person takes the box away, the planner sees this in the images, and the robot reaches again or walks to the nearest box. The demonstrations contain perturbed objects but no push on the demonstrator (§III-B), so none shows a recovery from a push. 

Box catching. The robot catches a thrown box in the air with both hands and sets it down (Fig. 1(b)). The thrower sets the timing and the trajectory, so the robot has to react to both. The flight shown lasts about 0.56 s. 

Suitcase pushing, toppling, and climbing. The robot pushes a 14 kg rolling suitcase to the shelf with its hands. It topples the suitcase flat with its hands and one leg, which it presses against the base. It steps up 0.39 m onto the suitcase, takes a box from the top shelf, and jumps down (Fig. 1(c)).

Protocol. We compare the full system with five variants on box sorting (Table II). All variants share the training data, the optimizer, and the schedule, and only the named component changes. Each system runs 100 episodes without pushes and 100 with pushes. Apart from the pushes, only the poses of the three boxes and the robot’s start pose change between episodes. Appearance, lighting, friction, and mass stay fixed. A push is an impulse of 40 N ·s on the pelvis in a random direction: 40 N for 1 s, every 5 s to 10 s. The full system succeeds in 77 % of episodes without pushes and in 64 % with pushes. With 100 episodes, the 95 % binomial interval of a rate near 77 % is about ±8 points. 

Joint command. This variant gives the whole-body tracker a joint command in place of the five-link command. An on-line inverse-kinematics solver computes the 29 joint positions from the five-link targets, as BifrostUMI [7] does with its retargeting module. Success drops to 54 % without pushes and to 42 % with pushes. With pushes, the robot falls in 4 % of episodes, against 15 % for the full system. A joint command is always reachable, which may explain the fewer falls. The online solver is imperfect and can hit a singularity, which may explain the lower success. 

Link-local action frame. This variant expresses the target of each link relative to the current pose of the same link. Success drops to 5 % without pushes and to 4 % with pushes, and the robot falls in most episodes. In the shared torso frame of the full system, the five poses show the body shape directly. The planner then likely learns the targets more easily and sends fewer out-of-distribution chunks to the whole-body tracker. 

Without projected gravity. This variant removes projected gravity and the torso height from the five-link history (§IV-B.1). Success drops to 1 %, and the robot falls in almost every episode. The planner then has to estimate the torso’s tilt and height from the images. 

Without planner augmentation. This variant removes all planner augmentation of §IV-B.3, so it does not separate the effects of its parts. Success drops to 20 % without pushes and to 0 % with pushes, and the robot falls in most episodes. The drift augmentation likely teaches the planner to recover when the whole-body tracker does not follow a chunk exactly. 

Without command augmentation. This variant removes the command augmentation of the whole-body tracker (§IV-C.2). Without pushes, success is 76 %, against 77 % for the full system. With pushes, success drops from 64 % to 52 %, and falls rise from 15 % to 23 %. The command augmentation therefore appears to matter mainly with pushes. A push leaves the chunk far from the robot’s state. A whole-body tracker trained only on clean commands then receives a command outside its training distribution. 

C. Cross-Embodiment 

In simulation, we retrain both policies for the Unitree H2 from the same demonstrations, with no new recording (§IV-A.1, Fig. 9(b)). The H2 planner trains on images rendered in the reconstructed room, while the simulated H2 replays the demonstrations. The H2 is a full-size humanoid, closer to the demonstrator in size and proportion than the G1 (Fig. 4). Without pushes, the H2 completes box sorting in 83 % of 100 episodes, and the G1 in 77 %. The difference is smaller than the 95 % interval of a difference between two such rates, about ±11 points. 

D. Summary 

On the real G1, Workhorse performs the three tasks and recovers from the perturbations of Fig. 8. In simulation, no variant succeeds more often than the full system, and the joint-command variant falls less often with pushes. With the link-local action frame, or without projected gravity or planner augmentation, the robot falls in most episodes. The command augmentation appears to matter mainly with pushes. Without the augmentations, errors that the policies did not see in training likely grow over the long task and with pushes. The same demonstrations also train both policies for a second humanoid in simulation. VI. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Scope of the results. Each task has its own planner and its own whole-body tracker. One demonstrator recorded all the data in one room. All tests take place in that room or in the reconstructed room. The real-robot experiments demonstrate capability. Success rates come from simulation only. Under 40 N ·s pushes, the full system falls in 15 % of the simulated episodes. 

Real-world deployment on the H2. The H2 result is in simulation (§V-C). Running the retrained policies on the real H2 is the next step. 

A richer interface. The five links do not describe the fingers or the head. Future work could add the state of a gripper or a dexterous hand to the interface. It could also add a neck that carries the camera, so the robot can choose where it looks. 

Recording outside one room. The Lighthouse base stations limit data collection to one room. A pose estimate without base stations would let us record in other places and test the robot there.
