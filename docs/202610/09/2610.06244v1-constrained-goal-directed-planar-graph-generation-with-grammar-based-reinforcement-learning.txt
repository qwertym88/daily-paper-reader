Abstract 

Planar graphs are central to applications across science and engineering, yet ex-isting generators provide limited support for goal-directed generation under hard structural and geometric feasibility constraints. We propose a dataset-free method for generating planar graph embeddings by combining parametric graph grammars with safe reinforcement learning to optimize generic task-specific objectives while satisfying constraints during construction. We formulate the generation process as a constrained Markov decision process, where the graph grammar defines the state and action spaces. We further introduce an action projection that maps sampled actions toward state-dependent safe sets, improving constraint satisfaction during training. In contrast to classical graph generators and deep generative models, which typically offer limited goal-directed control or rely on weak constraint satis-faction, our method constructs feasible planar graph embeddings directly during generation. We also introduce a benchmark suite for constrained and goal-directed planar graph generation, together with classical and deep generative baselines. Across all benchmark tasks, our method consistently outperforms baselines while satisfying the formulated constraints. 

1 Introduction 

Goal-directed planar graph generation is a central research topic in computer science, with applications spanning urban planning and road-network design [1, 2, 3], engineering design, including architecture, structural optimization, origami design [4, 5, 6, 7, 8] as well as computational mesh-generation [9]. In many of these applications, graphs are not merely abstract combinatorial representations of relational data, but require explicit planar embeddings whose structure must be optimized for task-specific objectives under hard structural and geometric constraints. Despite this need, methods for systematically generating goal-directed embedded planar graphs while enforcing hard constraints remain limited in performance or tailored to domain-specific settings [10]. Classical graph generators [11, 12, 13] and specialized planar graph generators [14, 15] provide tractable random graph models, including samplers for well-defined planar graph families. However, they are not generally designed for goal-directed multi-objective optimization nor do they construct

Deep generative models [10, 16, 17] can learn graph distributions and support property-conditioned generation [18], but they require training data which induces distributional biases and typically handle hard structural or geometric feasibility through soft conditioning, rejection sampling or post-processing. In contrast, autoregressive models, reinforcement learning, and hybrids of both have been used for sequential graph construction under hard validity constraints, especially in molecular design [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Yet many of these methods rely on discrete topological actions or domain-specific validity rules with relatively small graph instances, leaving generic and larger scale goal-directed generation of embedded planar graphs under hard constraints an open challenge. Here, we propose a generic dataset-free method for goal-directed embedded planar graph generation across varying graph sizes. The method combines parametric graph grammars with safe reinforcement learning (Figure 1). We formulate the generation process as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) whose state and action spaces are induced by the grammar. The grammar defines graph rewriting rules with continuous parameters, while an agent learns to apply these rules sequentially to optimize user-specified multi-objective rewards. Since graph size is controlled directly through sequential rule applications and termination criteria, our method can generate graphs with an exact prescribed number of nodes, a requirement that is difficult to enforce in many distributional graph generators. To improve feasibility during learning, we introduce a differentiable action projection that maps sampled actions toward state-dependent safe sets. The method constructs the planar embedding subject to hard constraints during generation, enabling controllable synthesis of planar graph embeddings for settings where multiple optimization objectives jointly matter. We additionally propose a new benchmark suite for constrained goal-directed embedded planar graph generation, that covers a wide variety of graph properties involving geometric, topological and spectral graph metrics. 

2 Preliminaries 

In this section we introduce parametric graph grammars, as well as their relation to Markov Decision Processes (MDP), to provide the basis for the definition of our method. 

2.1 Graph Grammars 

Graph grammars provide a rule-based formalism for the generation of structured graphs via sequential graph rewriting [24]. We adopt this formalism to model graph construction in which graph states, admissible rule application sites, and parametric rules are explicitly defined. In the following, we formalize the notion of graph states, introduce a parametric graph grammar, and define the graph transition operator that induces state-to-state evolution. 

Graph states. At each discrete step t ∈ N in the rewriting process, we define a graph in its respective state as Gt = ( Vt, E dt , E bt , F t), where Vt is the node set, Edt is a set of directed edges forming a directed acyclic graph (DAG), Ebt is a set of undirected boundary edges encoding the planar embedding, Ft ∈ R|Vt|× m assigns m-dimensional feature vectors to nodes, including planar coordinates. All graphs considered in this work are finite, connected and planar. Summary. Under the formulation proposed in this section, the graph generation process using the graph grammar is equivalent to a CMDP subject to hard, instantaneous constraints, where states are represented as graphs and actions are defined as graph rewriting rule applications. We leverage this formulation in Section 3 to develop a safe RL algorithm that preserves feasibility throughout generation. 

3 Planar Graph Generation 

Building on the CMDP formulation of the parametric graph grammar in Section 2, we formalize planar graph generation as a constrained sequential decision-making problem. The grammar defines admissible matches and available graph transformations, while a learned policy coordinates the choice of specific match, rule selection and assignments of parameter values to generate graphs optimized for user-specified objectives. Therefore, RL with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [27] is employed to learn a policy that maps the current graph state to a distribution over grammar-based actions, enabling informed decisions over long trajectories τ . To efficiently account for constraints during learning and inference, we introduce a differentiable action projection mechanism that maps actions onto a learned safe set, enabling stable and sample-efficient policy optimization. 

3.1 General Setup 

The environment state at step t is the graph state Gt. The state encodes the full graph topology, the generative frontier, the planar embedding, and node features. The initial state G0 is defined by two boundary nodes connected by an edge of length 1 (see Figure 1). Based on the preliminaries, the environment dynamics are deterministic for admissible actions and all stochasticity arises from the policy. We adopt an actor-critic architecture in which both the actor and the critic operate on a separate graph state encodings using a geometry-aware Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based feature extractor that maps Gt to node- and graph-level feature representations. An overview of the architecture is shown in Figure 2 and explained in detail, including training, in Appendix B. 

3.2 Constraint Taxonomy 

The graph generation process is subject to instantaneous feasibility constraints, which we explicitly categorize and relate to state-dependent safe action sets. We define the set of constraints as 

C(Gt) = {cℓ(Gt), c α(Gt), c deg (Gt), c pl (Gt)} (6) where the first constraint cℓ(Gt) = 0 if ℓ(e) ∈ [ℓmin , ℓ max ] ∀e ∈ Edt ∪ Ebt , i.e. all edge lengths in the graph are within bounds, otherwise 1. The second constraint cα(Gt) = 0 if all αi(v) > α min ∀v ∈ Vt

representing minimum bounds on all sector angles αi between consecutive incident edges around all nodes v of Gt, otherwise 1. The third constraint cdeg (Gt) = 0 if deg( v) ≤ deg max ∀v ∈ Vt,otherwise 1, representing maximum degree bounds on nodes. The last constraint cpl (Gt) checks whether the graph is drawn as a valid planar embedding, i.e. that no edge intersections occur, returns 

0 if so, otherwise 1. Hence, a graph transition T is only admissible if c = 0 ∀ c ∈ C (T (Gt; at)) .We further decompose the constraint set as C = CT ∪ C G into topological and geometric constraints, respectively. Topological constraints CT enforce the structural admissibility of an action and cannot be resolved by changing the values of continuous parameters θt after a rule pt and a match gt are determined. Such violations are intrinsic to the graph topology generated by a rewriting rule and therefore irreversible in our set-up. Geometric constraints CG, in contrast, correspond to violations 4that can, in principle, be resolved by finding the appropriate values of θt given a rule pt and a match 

gt. These violations occur due to boundary edge-length violations in Ebt or edge intersections. In practice, violations of CT occur infrequently (see Appendix C.3) and thus can be simply handled by rejection sampling over the discrete components of the policy. In contrast, violations of CG occur at a high rate, particularly edge intersections, and naive rejection sampling would lead to severe sampling inefficiency. This motivates the introduction of an explicit action projection mechanism that maps nominal parametrization of rules directly to a safe set A(Gt) → A safe (Gt), see Section 3.4.

4 Related Works 

Classical graph models such as Erd˝ os-Rényi or Barabási-Albert graphs [11, 12], as well as specialized planar graph generators [14, 15], provide useful baselines and, in some cases, planarity guarantees at the abstract graph level. However, they are not designed for goal-directed multi-objective optimization or for constructing task-optimized planar embeddings under hard geometric constraints. Deep graph generative models can learn graph distributions with high distributional fidelity [10, 16, 17], and conditional or guided variants can bias samples toward desired properties [36, 37]. Models such as DiGress and GDSS with Prodigy guidance generate abstract graph structures and can be adapted to multi-objective and single-objective property-conditioned generation, respectively [38, 39, 40]. However, enforcing exact graph size, planarity, and hard structural constraints through conditioning remains difficult, with validity often relying on rejection sampling, post-processing, or external guidance [18]. TreeDiff [18] extends pretrained graph diffusion models with verifier-guided tree search at inference time but adapting it to embedded planar graphs requires task-specific modifications to the diffusion backbone, intermediate-state verifier, and search procedure. Geometry-aware models such as NeVAE and GeoDiff primarily target molecular domains and rely on chemistry-specific validity assumptions [41, 42]. MuDiff more directly couples discrete structure and continuous coordi-nates [43], but feasibility is still learned implicitly from data rather than enforced as a construction constraint. Reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for goal-directed graph construction through sequential decisions [44, 45]. It has been widely used in molecular graph generation, where policies add atoms and bonds under chemistry-specific validity [2, 19, 20, 21]. Grammar- and rule-based RL has been applied to molecular optimization, where policies select productions from molecular grammars to preserve chemical validity [23, 46], to structural design, where grammar actions preserve domain-specific structural constraints [5, 8]. Outside generative settings, many graph-based RL meth-ods focus on tasks such as adversarial attacks, knowledge-graph completion, or relational reasoning rather than generic graph generation [45]. It has also been applied to computation-graph optimization, where RL selects rules for neural-network subgraph rewriting [47]. These works demonstrate the value of structured action spaces. However, they mainly address discrete and structural domain-specific construction. Our distinguishing setting is the goal-directed generation of planar embedded graphs using hybrid discrete-continuous action spaces: the policy jointly selects a graph match, a rewriting rule, and safe continuous geometric parameters, thereby coupling discrete topological graph rewriting with continuous embedding optimization under state-dependent topological and geometric constraints. 

5 Experiments 

We evaluate our method in two stages. First, we introduce four multi-objective benchmarks to assess the method’s performance under varying geometric, topological and spectral objective as well as different constraint tightness. Second, we ablate the proposed action projection on a single-objective graph generation task and perform a scalability study. All results are averaged over 5 seeds with 1-sigma standard deviations reported for the rewards achieved by our method. Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU, while the environment ran on an AMD 16-Core CPU; runtimes ranged from 10 minutes to 2 hours depending on benchmark complexity. 75.1 Benchmarks 

To the best of our knowledge, no standardized benchmarks have been established for constrained, goal-directed generation of planar embedded graphs. Accordingly, the proposed benchmark suite comprises four heterogeneous tasks combining geometric, topological, and spectral metrics for constrained multi-objective optimization and varying graph sizes |G|, where all metrics used are defined in detail in Appendix D. Table 1 reports benchmark results and compares our method against the three best baselines out of all 10 reported in Appendix G. This includes the best results from the 

House of Graphs (HOG) dataset [48], which contains more than 4,000 planar graphs with graph size up to 250. Since HOG graphs are not embedded, we compute embeddings using a force-directed spring layout [49]. Further, we compare against three diffusion-based generators; DiGress, GDSS, and MuDiff [38, 39, 43] on the same dataset and report the strongest contender for each benchmark. These models were selected as they allow for conditional generation, in the case of DiGress and MuDiff, while GDSS can be guided towards graph size using Prodigy [40]. The classical generator baselines used are described in Appendix E. For all classical baselines, we apply ranking-based selection to retrieve graphs satisfying the most constraints and, among them, maximize the reward. For all deep generative baselines, we evaluate from 1000 generated candidates. After retaining the largest connected component and filtering out non-planar graphs, candidates are ranked first by proximity to the target graph size and then by benchmark reward. The remaining geometric and structural constraint statistics are evaluated and reported, but they are not used for candidate selection. This alternative protocol for deep generative models is motivated by the fact that we empirically found diffusion models to rarely satisfy the constraint set, and prioritizing constraint satisfaction would substantially reduce the reward performance of these models. We consequently exclude the constraint satisfaction criterion from the ranking procedure for diffusion baselines to enable a fairer comparison of reward metrics. More details on the evaluation protocol are available in Appendix F. For benchmark 1, 2, 4, we enforce a tight constraint set C2 with ℓ ∈ [0 .75 , 2.5] , αmin = 20 ◦, and 

deg max = 8 . For benchmark 3, we use the relaxed constraint set C1 with ℓ ∈ [0 .1, 3.5] , αmin = 1 ◦

and deg max = 15 to allow for fairer comparison against unconstrained baselines.

5.2 Ablation and Scalability Study 

We further evaluate action projection and scalability on a single squareness objective rsquare over varying graph sizes. Figure 3 shows that CMA-ES-supervised action projection improves terminal reward by steering the policy away from infeasible continuous actions, allowing the agent to focus on reward optimization. This also reduces constraint violations by a factor of 15 compared to the projection-free variant; detailed statistics are provided in Appendix C.3. Figure 4 evaluates the reward of graph sizes up to 150 nodes under two constraint regimes. As expected, tighter constraints and larger graph sizes reduce achieved reward. However, the method still generates feasible square-like graphs at graph sizes substantially larger than common graph diffusion models operating on planar graph datasets, which typically use at most 64 nodes [38, 50]. 

6 Limitations & Future Work 

While our experiments generate graphs with up to 150 nodes (Figure 4), scalability remains limited by the growing combinatorial grammar design space for longer decision horizons. This limitation should be viewed relative to deep generative baselines, which often lose validity or efficiency on larger graphs under strict structural constraints such as planarity, as shown in Appendix G. Tackling this scalability ceiling is left for future work, where a principled approach could be the adoption of hierarchical reinforcement learning [51] to reduce the effective decision horizon. 7 Conclusions 

We introduced a grammar-based reinforcement learning method and benchmark suite that, to our knowledge, is the first to combine goal-directed generation of embedded planar graphs with hard structural and geometric constraint enforcement. By formulating graph rewriting as a constrained Markov decision process, the method constructs topology and geometry jointly while enforcing feasi-bility through grammar-induced admissibility and learned action projection. Across four benchmarks, our method generates graphs with exact prescribed node counts, satisfies all constraints, and achieves the best overall multi-objective reward. Although a few baselines are competitive in the benchmarks, they do not generalize across other multi-objective tasks; by contrast, our method maintains strong rewards while satisfying the full constraint set in all benchmarks, indicating stronger generalization across heterogeneous benchmarks. On the other hand, diffusion-based generators struggle strongly to satisfy constraints while maximizing the reward. Beyond the metrics and constraints used here, the framework is designed to accommodate arbitrary user-specified objectives and constraints through the reward and constraint definitions.

14 A Grammar Rules 

This appendix specifies the seven graph rewriting rules defined in the grammar G. Each rule p ∈ P 

acts on a single boundary node ¯vbt determined by the match gt and is applied via the graph transition operator T to the graph state Gt. A rule may introduce new embedded nodes and edges connecting those to ¯vbt , parameterized geometrically by a rule-specific continuous vector θt ∈ Θ( pt) that encodes normalized sector angles and edge lengths (cf. Appendix A.3). All rules update both the directed edge set Edt and the node set Vt. Prior to each rule application, the boundary edges Ebt in Gt

are removed as part of T , and reintroduced afterwards according to the connecting procedure (cf. Appendix A.2). This connecting procedure identifies how the leaf nodes in V bt in the current graph state are connected using boundary edges, such that the resulting graph is planar and geometrically closed. These boundary edges then populate the edge set Ebt . The rule semantics and notations are introduced in Appendix A.1. A schematic overview of all rules in P is shown in Figure 5 and Table 2. 

A.1 Rule semantics and notation in Figure 5 and Table 2. 

Each rule p(·) ∈ P . With reference to Section 2.1, a rule p(·) : L → R rewrites a local neighborhood (left-hand side L) in the graph state Gt around a boundary node ¯vbt identified by the match gt with a new subgraph R. In the visualizations of all rules in Figure 5, the top row depicts the graph state Gt

prior to a rule application with the highlighted subgraph L (i.e. the match ¯vbt ), which can be rewritten with a rule and the graph transition operator T . Additionally, we depict optional incoming directed edges to ¯vbt using dashed edges. This represents edges that may, or may not be present at the rule application site, implying that any number of existing incoming directed edges may be valid at a selected match ¯vbt , as long as the degree constraint cdeg (see Section 3.2) is satisfied. The bottom row in Figure 5 depicts the graph state Gt+1 where the right-hand side R of the rules has been applied. The newly added edges are depicted as outward directed edges from ¯vbt in red with new nodes with a red outline. Several rules allow to merge an edge to an existing node in the graph Gt (i.e. rules 2,3,4,6,7). This merging mechanism is enabled by the connecting procedure (cf. Appendix A.2). Figure 5 highlights the merged nodes in R as dashed-outlined nodes. A.2 Connecting Procedure cf. Figure 6 

The connecting procedure is a deterministic geometric routine that serves two purposes: (i) it identifies merge-able nodes that can be connected to from a match ¯vbt by rules with merging semantics, while ensuring topological planarity (cf. Figure 6(a)), (ii) it reconstructs the graph’s boundary edges after each rule application so that the updated graph remains planar and geometrically closed (cf. Figure 6(b)). The procedure operates exclusively on the current embedded graph state and depends only on the node positions and the directed edge configuration Edt . In case (i), the procedure is initialized at the match ¯vbt and geometrically traverses the connected edges in Edt , starting from ¯vbt , in either clock-wise or counter-clock-wise direction, depending on the selected rule. The traversal terminates upon finding a boundary node, which is then labeled as the node to merge to. This is indicated in the figure using the dashed-curved arrow (traversal process) and the dashed-outlined node (merge-able node) in Figure 6(a). In case (ii), the procedure is initialized at any boundary node and follows the same traversal principle until another boundary node is found. Then, a boundary edge connects both boundary nodes, depicted as the undirected edge between the highlighted nodes in Figure 6(b). Applying this process to all boundary nodes guarantees that the resulting boundary edge set Ebt forms a non-self-intersecting polygonal chain that encloses the graph.

A.4 Implementation 

The complete grammar and graph rewriting logic is implemented in JAX [53], enabling compiled and efficient implementation of the transition operator. Low-level geometric operations, in particular planarity checks based on pairwise edge-intersection tests, are formulated in a vectorized manner and evaluated in parallel. The grammar execution runs on the CPU, as the control flow of the grammar is predominantly sequential with sparse data structures that benefit more from high multi-core CPU throughput than from GPU parallelism.

B.3 Critic Network 

The critic predicts a scalar state value from the graph-level embedding ˆgt ∈ RZ and discards node-level representations. Note that the critics feature extractor does not share the feature-extractor parameters with the policies feature extractor. Each uses an architecturally identical but independently trained extractor, to disentangle learning signals for actor and critic. To provide an explicit graph-size signal, a learned embedding e(log(1 + |Vt|)) ∈ RZ is added to form ˜gt = ˆ gt + e(log(1 + |Vt|)) . The state value V (Gt) is then predicted from ˜gt ∈ RZ using a two-layer MLP.

We detail (i) the action projection architecture, (ii) the supervised training procedure, and (iii) the generation of supervision signals for action projection training. We additionally show that the policy space is preserved up to a reparameterization using the action projection in Appendix C.1.3 

C.1 Action Projection Architecture 

At time step t, the actor produces a nominal hybrid action at = ( gt, p t, ξ t), where gt denotes the selected boundary node (match), pt ∈ P the selected grammar rule, and ξt a nominal continuous parameter vector. The action projection is then conditioned on the local node-level embedding at the selected match and on the discrete rule choice using a learned embedding e(pt) ∈ REp . The sampled node-level embedding ˆH¯vbt ∈ RZ is extracted using the equivalent feature extractor (cf. Appendix B.1 and Section 3.3) as for the actor–critic networks, but trained separately in terms of trainable parameters ψ.

E Graph Generators 

We evaluate graph algorithms using synthetic datasets generated from well-established random graph models, focusing on classical, planar, and diffusion-based generators. Additionally, we include the House of Graphs (HOG) [48] dataset in the benchmarks, which includes, to the best of our knowledge, the most comprehensive connected planar graph dataset, albeit drawn without an embedding. Across all generators, benchmark-favorable settings and method-specific adaptations described throughout this chapter are applied to align each approach with the benchmark objectives. While computational budgets are kept as comparable as possible, diffusion-based models still require substantially higher training time. For all methods that do not provide an embedding by default, we use the force-directed spring layout to pre-process the graph to be embedded [49] using the N ETWORK X library [63]. This library is also used in the following described graph generators. 

E.1 Classical Random Graph Models Erd˝ os–Rényi (ER) Graphs. We use the G(n, p ) model, where each edge between the n

nodes is independently included with probability p = 2mn(n−1) . This model serves as a uniform random baseline and produces graphs with a binomial degree distribution, implemented using 

networkx.erdos_renyi_graph [11]. For benchmarking, we have generated 10,000 graphs per configuration, with edge probabilities ranging from 0.04 to 0.10. Connectivity is enforced, and disconnected graphs are filtered out. Higher edge densities substantially reduce the proportion of planar graphs, reflecting the well-known trade-off between connectivity and planarity in sparse regimes. Specifically, this method does not produce any planar graph for the second and third benchmark, see Appendix G. Despite extensive exploration of this parameter space, Erd˝ os–Rényi graphs never achieve the best performance in our evaluations. This behavior is expected because the model is constraint-free and not goal-directed. 25 Exponential Random Graph Models (ERGM). Graphs are sampled from an exponential distribution over structural features using a Metropolis–Hastings MCMC sampler: 

P (G) ∝ exp ( βedge · #edges + βtriangle · #triangles + βstar · #2-stars ) . (41) We use the implementation from [64] performing 1000 MCMC steps after burn-in. Parameters are selected from βedge ∈ {− 2.5, −2.0, −1.5} and βtriangle ∈ { 0.0, 0.1} with fixed βstar = 0 .0. Compared to other models, ERGMs involve a large hyperparameter space and are more difficult to tune, as certain parameter settings lead to disconnected graphs, while others produce dense, non-planar structures. As ERGMs do not enforce planarity, many sampled graphs violate planarity. 

E.2 Planar Graph Generators Plantri. We generate triangulated planar graphs using the P LANTRI software [14], which guarantees planarity and non-redundancy via combinatorial generation and isomorphism rejection. Due to expensive I/O and limited scaling beyond 15–20 nodes, we evaluate Plantri only on the first 2,000 sampled graphs. 

Boltzmann Sampler. Uniform planar graphs are sampled using a Boltzmann sampler based on ana-lytic combinatorics [15], implemented via the planar_graph_sampler library [65]. This approach generates unbiased planar graphs with expected quadratic time complexity. Due to the relatively high computational cost of this approach, we evaluate 50 graphs per benchmark, as larger sample sizes are a lot more expensive compared to our method. 

Delaunay Triangulations. A set of |G| − b > 0 points are sampled uniformly from circular and square domains, while an additional b boundary points placed along the domain boundary 

equidistantly . The resulting point set is triangulated according to the Delaunay criterion using 

scipy.spatial.Delaunay . For circular target metrics, boundary points are sampled along a circle to enforce a larger isoperimetric ratio, while an analogous construction is used for the squareness metric. 

Lattice Graphs. Planar grid graphs are generated as regular 2D lattices using 

networkx.grid_2d_graph . For a target graph size, we select an approximately square grid with 4-neighbor connectivity; in the circular setting, rows are wrapped into rings. These graphs provide deterministic spatial baselines with highly regular structure and no triangles, although the final node count may differ slightly from the target size. 

E.3 Deep Generative Graph Models 

We further include representative modern Deep Generative Models (DGM) for graph generation as baselines, including DiGress, GDSS and MuDiff [38, 39, 43]. These models are distribution-learning approaches that generate graphs by sampling from learned probabilistic processes. While DiGress and MuDiff provide a conditional sampling mechanisms towards target properties, they do not enforce hard constraints during generation. The same applies to GDSS, which is a purely unconditional model, but can be enhanced with PRODIGY [40] guidance towards single-objective target properties. Due to this, all models are evaluated by selecting candidates according to the procedure described in Section F to adhere to as many constraints as possible for a fair comparison. 

DiGress. DiGress is a discrete diffusion model for categorical graph generation with optional conditional guidance [38]. The model iteratively denoises graph structures in discrete space and enables conditioning through learned property predictors. In our evaluation, DiGress is first trained once in an unconditional manner on the full HOG dataset to learn a general distribution over connected graphs. Subsequently, for each benchmark, we train a dedicated property regressor that estimates the respective benchmark-specific objective (e.g., number of triangles and isoperimetric ratio in Benchmark G.4) which is trained using a per-metric standardized mean-squared error loss, so that metrics on different scales contribute comparably to the objective. This regressor is then used within the conditional generation mechanism of DiGress to guide sampling toward graphs that optimize the target objective. All architectural settings follow the original DiGress implementation. Minor adjustments to the number of diffusion steps and training epochs were applied to ensure stable convergence on the HOG dataset. F Ranking-Based Graph Selection for Baseline Evaluations 

We consider a set of candidate graphs ˜Gi produced by a given method. Candidates are first filtered for planarity (this can still contain graphs with edge intersections). Only planar graphs are retained for further processing through the benchmark pipeline, resulting in the set of graphs Gi.For each retained graph, we compute benchmark metrics M(Gi) and the final reward ri(see Sec-tion 3.6). The additional quantities used for ranking are split according to the generator class and are described separately for classical and diffusion-based methods below. 

Classical Generators For classical generators, we randomly sample candidates according to the generator specifications and report metrics, and constraint satisfaction. For each generator, we retain only the graphs that satisfy most constraints. If multiple graphs satisfy equally many constraints, we report the graph with highest reward among them. As visible in the benchmarks (cf. Appendix G), classical generators rarely satisfy all constraints. 

Deep Generative Models For deep generative models, we sample approximately M ≈ 1000 

candidate graphs per generator. Since generated samples may contain small disconnected components, we retain only the largest connected component Gi with size |Gi| for subsequent evaluation, ensuring that otherwise valid planar structures are not discarded. Graph-size proximity is prioritized to ensure comparability with the target benchmark configuration: since DGMs do not explicitly control the number of nodes during generation, samples can vary substantially in size, and differences in graph size can strongly affect both evaluation metrics and reward.
