Abstract 

We study mixed-integer quadratic optimization (MIQO) problems with indicator variables. We propose a unified framework, based on decision diagrams, that serves both to solve the associated optimization problems and to construct ideal conic quadratic extended formulations of the closure of the convex hull of the underlying mixed-integer set. The construction applies to arbitrary quadratics and to any combinatorial constraints admitting a tractable dynamic programming representation. The resulting diagrams and the ensuing convex hull descriptions are of polynomial size when the quadratic is low-rank, or when the support graph of the Hessian or of its inverse is a tree, recovering and generalizing several results from the literature. For structured sparse and inverse-sparse quadratics, we show that approximate decision diagrams have size linear in the dimension while yielding solutions with arbitrarily low optimality gap. Computational experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

The indicator constraints x ◦ (1 − z) = 0 are usually linearized as −M z ≤ x ≤ M z, provided a valid value of M can be inferred from constraints (1b) or set XF ,Z .Problems of the form (1) arise naturally in finance, statistics, and control. In cardinality-constrained portfolio optimization, factor models decompose covariance into diagonal specific risk and a low-rank common-factor term (Bienstock 1996); in slowly varying regression and online mon-itoring, local temporal interactions produce sparse block-banded quadratics (Bertsimas et al. 2024, Gomez et al. 2024); and in hybrid model predictive control, eliminating the state variables typically produces a dense quadratic with a sparse or block-banded inverse (Lee et al. 2024). Solution approaches for mixed-integer optimization (MIO) problems often rely on effective con-2vex relaxations. A natural convex relaxation of (1), obtained by replacing the indicator constraints with big-M constraints and constraints z ∈ Z with z ∈ conv (Z), where “conv (Z)” denotes the convex hull of Z, is typically weak. As a result, branch-and-bound algorithms based on this nat-ural relaxation require substantial enumeration before being able to prove optimality. A stronger relaxation, which is the main focus of this paper, is given by 

min  

> x0,x,z

d⊤x + c⊤z + x0 (2a) s.t. Ax + Gz ≤ b (2b) 

(x0, x, z) ∈ cl conv (XF ,Z ) (2c) where “cl” denotes the closure of a set. Relaxation (2) dominates the natural relaxation in terms of strength, and results in an exact reformulation of the problem if constraints (1b) are removed. Unfortunately, since optimization over set XF ,Z is NP-hard in general even if Z = {0, 1}n (Natarajan 1995), computing the convex hull of XF ,Z is a daunting task: explicit descriptions often require complicated nonlinear functions defined by pieces, an exponential number of constraints, or an exponential number of additional variables. Nonetheless, if additional assumptions are imposed on set XF ,Z , then optimization over this set may become polynomial-time solvable, and compact conic quadratic descriptions of cl conv (XF ,Z ) are sometimes possible. Indeed, several such results exist in the literature, each relying on different convexification techniques. Convexifications of XF ,Z are available if either the dimension n or the rank of the quadratic form—given by the number of columns m of F —is fixed. On the one hand, set XF ,Z can be represented as a disjunction of |Z| ≤ 2n convex sets, each corresponding to a possible value of the binary variables. Disjunctive programming techniques (Balas 1979, Ceria and Soares 1999) can thus be used to produce representations of cl conv (XF ,Z ) with O(|Z|) additional variables and constraints. The widely used perspective reformulation (Frangioni and Gentile 2006, Aktürk et al. 2009, Günlük and Linderoth 2010) can be interpreted as a special case of disjunctive programming convexifications with n = 1 . Frangioni et al. (2020) computationally tested convexifications of low-dimensional sub-blocks; the approach appears to be effective only for blocks of dimension at most two, as the burden of handling the additional variables and constraints otherwise outweighs the benefits of the stronger relaxations. Exact convex hulls for the case n = 2 have also been derived 3in lifted spaces containing the matrix variable X = xx ⊤, often incorporating nonnegativity or box constraints on x (Anstreicher and Burer 2021, Han et al. 2023, De Rosa and Khajavirad 2024); related lifted inequalities for general MIQO are studied by Dong and Linderoth (2013). On the other hand, descriptions of cl conv (XF ,Z ) for m = 1 were first obtained by Atamturk and Gomez (2025); Shafiee and Kılınç-Karzan (2024) and Wei et al. (2022) extended these results to more general constraints. Moreover, Han and Gómez (2024) show that disjunctive programming techniques can also be applied to settings with m ≥ 2, deriving compact formulations of cl conv (XF ,Z ) of size polynomial in n but exponential in m.If both n and m are large, disjunctive programming techniques cannot be applied effectively, and obtaining compact convexifications requires exploiting the structure of matrix F . Liu et al. (2023) and Lee et al. (2024) consider the full-rank case, and derive compact extended formulations for cl conv (XF ) when matrix F F ⊤ is tridiagonal or its inverse is tridiagonal, respectively. Both papers rely on dynamic programming to solve optimization problems over XF : the first then uses lifting (Richard and Tawarmalani 2010) to derive the convex hull description, whereas the second exploits an underlying polyhedrality of the set cl conv (XF ) (Wei et al. 2024). Moreover, Gomez et al. (2024) use decision diagrams and the results of Wei et al. (2024) to either describe or approximate cl conv (XF ) when matrix F F ⊤ is full rank and banded. For other matrix classes, Atamtürk and Gómez (2018), Liu et al. (2025) derive strong formulations for quadratics with Stieltjes matrices. 

Contributions and outline We develop a unified, structure-adaptive decision-diagram frame-work for optimizing over XF ,Z and constructing ideal conic quadratic extended formulations of 

cl conv( XF ,Z ). Decision diagrams are compact graphical representations of discrete sets derived from dynamic programs (Bergman et al. 2016a, Castro et al. 2022); our construction extends their recent use for banded MIQO (Gomez et al. 2024) to arbitrary matrices F and combinatorial sets Z.Its states are defined through Gram–Schmidt projections of the rows of F , and exploitable structure emerges automatically through the merging of equivalent states. The framework yields polynomial-size exact diagrams for low-dimensional, low-rank, tree, and inverse-tree structures, removes prior full-rank requirements, incorporates structured constraints on z, and can exploit multiple structures simultaneously. Moreover, for sparse or inverse-sparse matrices we construct ϵ-exact diagrams by merging nearly identical states and prove that, under polynomial volume growth and uniformly 4bounded boundary size, they have size linear in n for fixed ϵ > 0.The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In §2 we summarize the notation used in the paper. In §3 we review preliminaries on decision diagrams for discrete optimization and on the Gram-Schmidt process. In §4 we introduce the proposed decision diagrams for MIQO, in §5 we discuss their construction and implementation, and in §6 we prove that they yield exact convexifications of 

XF ,Z . In §7 we show that the resulting diagrams and convexifications are of polynomial size for low-rank, tree and inverse tree structures, and in §8 we construct approximations resulting in arbitrarily low errors for sparse and inverse-sparse structures. In §9 we illustrate how the approximation theory can inform variable ordering, and in §10 we present computational results with real data. All proofs of propositions, theorems, lemmas, and corollaries in §4 onwards are provided in the online companion (§EC.5).

The transition function establishes how the system transitions between states. Akey consideration when representing discrete feasible spaces is whether a partial solution can be extended to obtain a complete feasible solution and in how many ways this can be done.

# 5 Pseudocode and implementation details 

In this section, we provide pseudocode for the construction of the decision diagrams introduced in §4.3, and discuss the associated implementation details. The algorithm builds the state transition graph layer by layer: it applies the transition function (11) to every node of the current layer, and then merges the nodes of the next layer that represent equivalent states. The pseudocode assumes that standard finite-precision libraries are used to carry out the matrix operations, and explicitly encodes the operations whose outcome depends on a numerical tolerance ϵ ≥ 0. Nonetheless, as we discuss at the end of the section, if F ∈ Qn×m, then the decision diagram can also be constructed using exact rational arithmetic. 

Data structures The algorithm maintains a set of nodes and a set of arcs. A node is a tuple 

(i, ρ, H), where i ∈ Z+ is a unique identifier of the node, ρ is a state of the dynamic programming representation of Z (see §3.1), and H ∈ R(n+1 −ℓ)×m is the state matrix of the node, with ℓ denoting the layer of the node. An arc is a tuple (i, j, ℓ, ¯z, u), where i and j are the identifiers of the tail and head nodes, ℓ ∈ [n] is the layer from which the arc emanates, ¯z ∈ { 0, 1} is the value assigned to 19 variable zℓ, and u ∈ Rm is the associated transition vector. In particular, the value assignments ν

and transition vectors u of Definition 7 are encoded directly in the arcs. In the unconstrained case 

Z = {0, 1}n, the component ρ is constant and can simply be omitted. 

Elementary operation The pseudocode relies on the following elementary operation. 

• merge (L, A, ϵ ): given a list of nodes L and a list of arcs A, while two nodes (i1, ρ 1, H1),

(i2, ρ 2, H2) ∈ L are identified to satisfy ρ1 = ρ2 and ∥H1 − H2∥2,∞ ≤ ϵ: remove node i2 from L,and redirect every arc of A with head i2 to head i1.The wording “are identified to satisfy” in the routine merge is intentionally vague, as it depends on the implementation. A first implementation, which we call maximal merging, compares all pairs of nodes in the layer, requiring a quadratic number of state comparisons. In §8 we describe a second implementation of merging called neighborhood merging, which is based on exploiting sparsity of matrix F F ⊤ or its inverse, and is useful for proving approximation guarantees. However that implementation may too be impractical, as it requires a full a priori understanding of F F ⊤

and its application may lead to unnecessarily large decision diagrams. In practice, we implement 

merge using a single-pass hash-merging procedure described in the online companion (§EC.2.1, Algorithm EC.1); we refer to it as hash merging.

# 7 Decision diagrams of polynomial size 

The decision diagrams proposed in §4 can be prohibitively large in general, involving O(2 n) nodes, even if Z = {0, 1}n. As a result, the convexifications in §6 may require an exponential number of variables and constraints. # 8 Approximation 

In the previous section, we showed that the constructed decision diagrams have polynomial size in three settings. In this section, we consider more general sparsity patterns of Q and Q−1, for which exact decision diagrams may be exponentially large, and construct decision diagrams of reduced size by merging nodes whose state matrices are close but not necessarily identical, as discussed in §5. Recall from §5 that ϵ-exact diagrams are the diagrams produced by Algorithm 2 with tolerance 

ϵ > 0, and that their size depends on the implementation of the routine merge ; the goal of this section is to exhibit implementations for which the resulting diagrams are provably small while still guaranteeing high-quality solutions. Throughout the section, we assume that Z = {0, 1}n and that 29 Q = F F ⊤ ≻ 0. Moreover, to reduce clutter, throughout this section we omit the comma in double subscripts, writing for example QV W instead of QV,W .Recall from §2 the definitions of η-neighborhoods, their size maximum ∆G,η , distances dG(i, j )as well as the definition of the decay rate σ which is closely associated with the condition number. The volume growth function characterizes the rate at which ∆G,η increases with η.

Definition 13 (Polynomial volume growth) . A graph G is said to have a polynomial volume growth with parameters γ, δ > 0 if ∆G,η ≤ δ η γ for all η ≥ 1. ■

Many well-studied graph families satisfy this property. For instance, banded graphs with band-width k exhibit linear volume growth, i.e., ∆G,η ≤ 2kη + 1 . More generally, any d-dimensional grid graph (and its subgraphs) satisfies ∆G,η = O(ηd). In contrast, trees with O(n) leaves may violate this assumption. Notable examples include complete binary trees, for which ∆G,η = O(2 η), and star graphs, where ∆G, 1 = n. Our next definition quantifies how similar two partial solutions are, as seen from a given node of the graph. 

Definition 14 (η-similarity) . We say that v1, v2 ∈ { 0, 1}ℓ−1 are η-similar in G with respect to a node i ∈ [n] if (v1)j = ( v2)j for every j ∈ [ℓ − 1] satisfying dG(i, j ) ≤ η. ■

The results of this section state that partial solutions that are η-similar with respect to all undecided nodes lead to nearly identical states. In this section we will consider decision diagrams that merge nodes that are η-similar (a process we refer to as neighborhood merging) and establish that if η is properly chosen, the decision diagrams are ϵ-exact. Moreover, the number of distinct states of such ϵ-exact decision diagram is thus governed by the number of nodes near the boundary between decided and undecided variables, which motivates the following definition.

# 9 Computational study on variable ordering 

The size of the decision diagrams usually depends on the order in which variables are processed (an exception being the low-rank setting in §7.1). The polynomial-time proofs in §7.2 and §7.3 for trees 33 and inverse trees, respectively, assume that the variables are processed starting from the leaves of the trees and finishing at the root. However, in practice and when using ϵ-exact diagrams, this order might not achieve the best performance. Indeed, as we illustrate computationally in this section, better orders can be obtained by controlling the sizes of the η-boundaries throughout the tree.

The approximation is due to two distinct effects, both of which may only prevent admissible merges. First, the quantization operates entrywise: two states satisfying ∥H1 − H2∥2,∞ ≤ ϵ may differ by more than ¯ϵ in some entry, and thus receive different keys. Second, even states that are entrywise within ¯ϵ of each other may fall in adjacent cells of the quantization grid and receive different keys. EC.2.2 Further Implementation Improvements 

We discuss three additional improvements to Algorithm 2 that can enhance its practical perfor-mance. 

Full row rank. If F has full row rank—equivalently, if Q = F F ⊤ ≻ 0—then no first row u ever vanishes. In this case, the condition ∥u∥2 > ϵ in line 9 can be replaced with the condition ¯z = 1 

alone. Doing so removes the risk of falsely detecting linear dependence, that is, of incorrectly treating a nearly dependent row as the zero row when F is ill-conditioned. 

Re-normalization. Instead of using directly the state matrix, a canonical representative of each state obtained by normalizing each row can be used instead. This canonical representation potentially increases the number of nodes that can be merged and improves the numerical behavior of the method. Indeed, rescaling a row of a state matrix by a nonzero constant affects neither the transition vectors nor the arcs generated: the projection I − uu ⊤/∥u∥22 in (11) and the normalized transition vectors of Definition 7 are invariant to rescalings of u, and each row of the state matrix evolves independently of the scale of the other rows. Nodes whose state matrices coincide after normalization 54 thus generate identical sub-diagrams and identical arc information, and merging them is valid even when their unnormalized states differ. Moreover, since row norms shrink after each projection, renormalizing at every iteration prevents the loss of precision associated with rows of vanishing norm. To implement this change, it suffices to normalize every row of F at the start of the algorithm, renormalize rows of G after operation G ← R(I − uau⊤

) in line 11 (after checking for zero rows), and delete the normalization step in line 10. The drawback of this operation, however, is that additional normalizations are performed, increasing the cost of each operation in line 11. 

Exploiting sparsity. Consider for example a tridiagonal matrix F F ⊤ as discussed in Example 3, where F preserves the tridiagonal structure. It is easy to show that, since the support of rows F{i}

and F{i+j} do not overlap for j ≥ 2, the Gram-Schmidt operation in line 11 only affects row ℓ + 1 of the state matrix. As a consequence, at level ℓ there is no need to store rows ℓ + j for j ≥ 2. The net effect of this change is to reduce memory requirements, reduce the cost of matrix multiplications in line 11, and decrease the cost of computing and comparing keys in Algorithm EC.1. The same arguments can be used for the case when F F ⊤ is banded (discarding rows corresponding to ℓ + j

where j is greater than the bandwidth) and, as we show in §7.2, similar arguments apply in even more general cases. Additionally, if matrix F is sparse and structured (e.g., banded), each state matrix will also be sparse. In this case, using libraries for sparse matrix multiplications can significantly reduce the number of operations needed.

EC.4.2.2 Parameter Tuning 

We also tested smoothing parameters ¯α ∈ { 0.8, 0.95 }, and the results are as expected: the per-formance of decision diagram methods for ¯α = 0 .8 lies between the performance reported for 

¯α ∈ { 0.7, 0.9}, and the setting ¯α = 0 .95 performed even better than the settings reported here. We did not include these results to avoid biasing the results towards instances in which decision diagram methods are better than NAT , in order to have a more balanced discussion. We tested the setting DD : τ with τ ∈ { 2, 3, 5}. The gaps in these cases were equal or close to 100%, so we do not report these results. We also experimented with decision diagrams using the hash-merging procedure of §EC.2.1 and precision ϵ = 10 −2 (instead of 10 −3 as reported in the paper). The resulting diagrams are substantially more compact (involving roughly one-tenth as many arcs), but in some instances the solutions obtained from the diagrams were suboptimal due to the coarser aggregation. Finally, we experimented with solving formulations DD : τ with branch-and-bound, but the results were not promising. Just processing the root node of the branch-and-bound tree takes, on average, three times as long as solving the SOCP relaxation (the times reported in Table EC.2), due to presolve and other features specific to solving MIOs. Just solving the root node already consumes a significant portion of the 600-second budget, and only a few hundred, or even only a few dozen, branch-and-bound nodes are then explored, which is insufficient to further close the relaxation gap or find good feasible solutions.

Zero-padding convention A hat over a matrix indicates that a vector or matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by subsets of [n] is zero-padded in the remaining coordinates, so that sums and differences of quantities indexed by different subsets are well defined. For instance, given 

T ⊆ [n] and F ∈ Rn×m, the matrix ˆFT ∈ Rn×m coincides with FT in the rows indexed by T and is zero elsewhere; in particular, ˆFT = Diag (z)F , where z ∈ { 0, 1}n is the indicator vector of T .Similarly, given S, T ⊆ [n] and M ∈ Rn×n, the matrix ˆMS,T ∈ Rn×n coincides with MS,T in the entries indexed by S × T and is zero elsewhere. Inverses are always taken before zero-padding: for example, ( ˆMT,T )−1 denotes the zero-padding of (MT,T )−1, because the padded matrix itself is singular whenever T̸ = [ n].
