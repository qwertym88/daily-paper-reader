Reusable-rocket powered landing under strong aerodynamics couples variable mass, free final time, and bounded aerodynamic controls through nonlinear velocity-frame dynamics. This paper develops a condensed proportional–integral projected-gradient (PIPG) sequential-convex method whose principal contribution is an exact reduced-space inner architecture. Because the problem contains only six terminal hard equalities and no state path constraints, 217 nodal-state variables and 210 trapezoidal dynamics equalities are eliminated from the 31-node convex subproblem, leaving 101 primal variables and six terminal equalities. Row-orthogonal preconditioning, fixed-size matrix–vector products, and nodewise circular-epigraph projections then yield a customized PIPG kernel. Physical consistency of the angle-dependent axial force is maintained by gradually releasing drag sensitivity between the reference squared angle and an epigraph variable 𝐴 , together with a convex tightness term. A pointwise Hamiltonian argument shows that the fully released limiting subproblem admits a tight optimum satisfying 𝐴 = 𝛼 2 + 𝛽 2. Deterministic annealing, two-stage inner accuracy, and a rejected-on-failure threefold extrapolation are secondary outer-loop accelerators. For a fair benchmark, condensed PIPG and full-state ECOS use identical SCP continuation, extrapolation, stationarity thresholds, and nonlinear acceptance. Their nominal outer counts are 13 and 15, their propellant consumptions differ by only 0.089 kg, and their median latencies are 2.243 ms and 79.114 ms. Both validate all 100 common ±5% initial-state perturbations; the mean outer counts are 15.05 and 13.90, while the median latencies are 2.425 ms and 71.611 ms. Across 15–51 nodes, the median ranges are 1.098–5.844 ms and 28.062–133.253 ms. These results identify exact condensation and customized PIPG as the primary source of low latency under comparable outer SCP work, while lossless relaxation and safeguarded continuation support robust application to the stronger aerodynamic model. ## I. Introduction 

Following landing-burn ignition, a reusable launch vehicle must dissipate a large amount of kinetic energy while correcting its landing point and aligning the velocity direction with the terminal approach. In a vacuum or a weakly aerodynamic regime, translational powered-descent dynamics can often be expressed in a form that is amenable to lossless convexification. During an atmospheric retropropulsive descent, however, the interaction between the freestream and the thrust-oriented vehicle produces axial force, lift, and side force that may be comparable with the commanded thrust. The resulting state–control coupling is pronounced, particularly when the aerodynamic coefficients depend on the angle of attack and sideslip angle. A practical onboard optimizer must therefore accommodate variable mass, free final time, bounded controls, and stringent terminal conditions without exceeding a short guidance-update interval. Convex-optimization-based powered-descent guidance has progressed from lossless convexification to experimental flight demonstration [1–3]. When aerodynamic forces, free time, or compound constraints preclude an exact convex reformulation, successive or sequential convexification replaces the original nonlinear optimal-control problem by a sequence of locally valid convex subproblems [4–7]. Interior-point algorithms offer accurate and robust solutions of medium-scale conic programs; ECOS, CVXGEN, and customized primal–dual methods are representative embedded implementations [8–10]. First-order primal–dual schemes, including proportional–integral projected gradient (PIPG) and extrapolated PIPG, replace repeated Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) factorizations with matrix–vector products and low-dimensional projections [11–14]. The present study is built directly upon two recent contributions by the authors. The first combined exact terminal condensation, strong-convexity regularization, and a closed-form radial proximal operator for a fixed-mass Cartesian powered-descent convex kernel. It reduced a 30-node problem to 90 controls and six terminal multipliers and attained microsecond-class latency [15]. The second extended this structural design to variable mass, free final time, and quadratic aerodynamic drag, producing an exactly condensed sequential-convex framework with a fixed-array xPIPG implementation and submillisecond end-to-end latency [16]. The former established the low-dimensional terminal-equality and structured first-order core; the latter established the nonlinear SCP wrapper around that core. The main thread of the present work remains condensed PIPG: the reduced terminal architecture is generalized to strongly coupled velocity-frame aerodynamics, a circular total-angle epigraph, and a more demanding nodal projection, while intermediate states remain absent from the first-order hot loop. Gradual drag-sensitivity release and conditional 3losslessness protect physical consistency around this core; deterministic annealing and safeguarded extrapolation shorten the outer fixed-point tail. The latter mechanisms are supporting SCP accelerators rather than substitutes for the principal condensed-PIPG contribution. Most powered-landing formulations use a local Cartesian frame with position and velocity vectors as states. This representation avoids angular singularities at low speed and yields a simple dynamics-chain structure. A velocity-frame formulation is nevertheless attractive when axial force, lift, and side force are modeled directly as functions of angle of attack and sideslip: it exposes the separate contributions of these forces to speed, flight-path angle, and heading. Li [17] developed such a sequentially convex model with constant thrust magnitude and squared-angle auxiliary variables. That study also reported a central difficulty: inserting the angle-dependent axial-force coefficient directly into the convex subproblem may produce oscillatory controls and destroy outer convergence. Freezing the coefficient at the reference trajectory improves numerical stability but makes its derivative with respect to the current aerodynamic controls identically zero. Consequently, the optimizer can only accept the drag inherited from the preceding reference and cannot allocate angle of attack within a subproblem to increase aerodynamic deceleration. The difficulty is more subtle than the truncation error of a first-order Taylor expansion. The convex epigraph 

𝐴 ≥ 𝛼 2 + 𝛽 2 is one-sided. If the axial coefficient is written directly as 𝐶 𝑥 = 𝐶 𝑥 0 + 𝐶 𝑥 1 𝐴 , the optimizer may change 𝐴 

without changing the physical angles 𝛼 and 𝛽 . For the negative 𝐶 𝑥 1 used in the present model, this mechanism creates artificial axial deceleration that is absent from the original dynamics. A convex subproblem may then have a small algebraic residual while its trajectory fails when propagated through the physical nonlinear equations. Restoring useful drag sensitivity must therefore be accompanied by a mechanism that keeps the squared-angle epigraph tight. This paper studies a deliberately structured onboard subproblem. Hard constraints are imposed on the terminal state and on the controls, while state-path limits on dynamic pressure, heat rate, load factor, and glide corridors are excluded. This scope does not diminish the engineering importance of those constraints. It isolates the class of problems in which the intermediate states are uniquely determined by the initial state, controls, thrust, and final time, allowing exact elimination of the entire dynamics chain. Transferring condensation from Cartesian coordinates to the present problem is not a change of variables alone: the velocity-frame dynamics, circular squared-angle epigraph, analytic strong-aerodynamics Jacobians, and terminal low-speed singularity must all be treated explicitly. The principal contributions are as follows: 1) The principal algorithmic contribution is a customized condensed-PIPG kernel. Exact elimination and a backward terminal-adjoint recursion form a 6 × 101 terminal map; a six-row Cholesky preconditioner makes its spectral norm analytically equal to one; and blockwise proximal maps plus fixed-cost nodal projections remove all intermediate states and factorizations from the inner hot loop. 2) The reduced kernel is extended to a velocity-frame model with variable mass, free final time, optimized constant thrust, signed strong aerodynamic forces, and a circular total-angle epigraph. A pointwise Hamiltonian analysis 4establishes conditional losslessness of the fully released squared-angle epigraph and separates this structural result from global optimality of the nonlinear problem. 3) As supporting mechanisms, gradual drag-sensitivity release suppresses artificial axial force, while deterministic proximal–tightness annealing, two-stage PIPG accuracy, and rejected-on-failure threefold extrapolation shorten the post-feasibility fixed-point tail without relaxing nonlinear task-level acceptance. 4) Nominal, 100-sample common-seed Monte Carlo, and 15–51-node grid studies compare single-precision condensed PIPG with double-precision full-state ECOS. Both implementations use identical continuation, annealing, extrapolation, outer stationarity thresholds, and nonlinear acceptance, so their SCP counts are comparable and the benchmark isolates the inner architecture more clearly. The ordering above deliberately distinguishes the condensed-PIPG core from the model extension and its supporting outer-loop mechanisms. ## VI. Fixed-Array Implementation and Online Workflow 

A. Computational Structure 

The C implementation fixes 𝑁 = 31 at compile time and uses contiguous static arrays. No memory is allocated inside either the SCP or PIPG hot loop. The principal structural optimizations are: 1) Atmospheric quantities, trigonometric functions, dynamics, and analytic Jacobians are evaluated once per node and shared by the two adjacent intervals. 2) Each seven-dimensional trapezoidal interval matrix is factorized once and reused for the state, two endpoint controls, thrust, time, and affine-defect right-hand sides. 3) Six-row backward terminal adjoints replace forward propagation of all previously introduced control columns. 4) Every interval map is retained after condensation and reused for forward state recovery, avoiding repeated linearization or factorization. 5) Equation (55) removes the power iteration formerly used to estimate 𝐻 2.6) The drag-release factor is updated once per outer iteration, and 𝑅 𝑝 𝐷 is inserted directly into the linear cost without adding inner matrix products. 7) The product 𝐻 𝒛 𝑗 is reused by the dual update and the downsampled stopping test. 8) Buffer variables use the exact diagonal proximal map. The circular epigraph projection computes a square root only on infeasible branches and performs exactly two scalar Newton updates. 9) A safeguarded extrapolation evaluates one original-dynamics defect but does not propagate each candidate. The implicit nonlinear propagator is invoked only for a final fixed-point candidate. 22 10) A deterministic annealing counter advances continuously after first feasibility, preventing small temporary proxy defects from repeatedly stalling the proximal and tightness homotopies. The full-state ECOS baseline retains every nodal state and trapezoidal equation. To prevent outer-loop choices from biasing the comparison, it uses the same continuous dynamics, control set, drag-release schedule, proximal–tightness annealing, safeguarded extrapolation, finite-accuracy outer stationarity test, and nonlinear final propagator as condensed PIPG. ECOS differs at the convex-subproblem level: it uses a double-precision full-state conic formulation and conventional 10 −8-level interior-point tolerances, whereas PIPG uses the single-precision condensed representation and two-stage inner tolerances. 

B. Outer Update Sequence 

At each outer iteration, 𝜆 𝑝 𝐷 is updated by Eq. (26) and the condensed convex subproblem is reconstructed. Before the local-feasible stage, the recovered ordinary surrogate step becomes the next reference. After first feasibility, the proximal and tightness weights follow Eqs. (69) and (30), and one candidate is formed by Eq. (70). If Eq. (71) passes, the candidate replaces the ordinary step; otherwise the ordinary step is used. There is no dynamic trust-region radius, multiple-candidate line search, or nonlinear propagation of rejected candidates. Original-dynamics propagation is called only after all fixed-point conditions in Eq. (72) have been met, and the method terminates only when this final propagation passes. The resulting algorithm is most accurately described as a proximal-stabilized SCP method with a task-screened extrapolation. It does not inherit the global-convergence guarantees of trust-region successive convexification, and its safe-extrapolation gate should not be interpreted as an actual-to-predicted reduction test.

## VIII. Discussion 

A. Structural Origin of Millisecond-Class Latency 

The velocity-frame model is intrinsically more expensive than its Cartesian counterpart. The 𝑉 , 𝛾 , and 𝜓 equations are strongly coupled and contain exponential density, trigonometric functions, reciprocal speed, reciprocal heading cosine, and aerodynamic controls. With one total-angle epigraph per node, the 31-node condensed program nevertheless contains only 101 primal variables, and each PIPG update requires 31 axisymmetric three-dimensional projections. The principal structural source of the nominal latency below 3 ms is the combination of exact dynamics condensation and a customized PIPG kernel. At the modeling level, hard state constraints are concentrated at the terminal point, 31 permitting complete elimination of the state chain. At the algebraic and numerical levels, a six-row backward adjoint constructs the terminal map, terminal-buffer scaling and row-orthogonal preconditioning equalize all nonzero singular values, and fixed-size arrays realize the resulting matrix–vector products and block projections. Deterministic annealing, two-stage inner accuracy, and safeguarded extrapolation then provide secondary savings by reducing the number of reconstructed subproblems and the cumulative hot-loop work. Analytic Jacobians, interval-factorization reuse, and downsampled stopping checks further reduce arithmetic and memory traffic without changing the mathematical formulation. These mechanisms yield a nominal single-precision solve in 13 SCP iterations and 2450 PIPG updates with a median of 2.243 ms. Row preconditioning limits the inner iterations caused by equality-scale disparity; condensation reduces the work per update; gradual drag release prevents the outer failure caused by incorrect sensitivity; and safeguarded extrapolation directly removes repeated linearization, condensation, and recovery calls. The microsecond-class Cartesian kernel in Ref. [15] excludes the aerodynamic SCP outer loop and assumes fixed mass. The additional latency here is therefore associated with repeated nonlinear reconstruction and joint optimization of variable mass, thrust, final time, and aerodynamic controls, not with a breakdown of the condensation concept. 

B. Accuracy Budget for Receding-Horizon Guidance 

The method separates task-level physical accuracy from outer fixed-point accuracy. The former is defined by original nonlinear propagation, terminal position and velocity, dry mass, thrust, and the total-angle limit; it determines whether a trajectory is admissible as a command candidate. The latter measures changes in fuel, controls, thrust, and final time between consecutive SCP references and indicates whether the solution has left the outer transient. The final nonlinear terminal threshold remains 10 −3, while the surrogate terminal threshold that triggers final propagation is 10 −4.Safeguarded extrapolation changes the path taken by the references but does not relax either exit threshold. An operational receding-horizon implementation would normally shift the trajectory from the preceding guidance cycle rather than restart from the same linear interpolation. If {𝒙 𝑘 , 𝒖 𝑘 } is available, the elapsed portion can be discarded and the terminal state and control extended to form the next reference. Such a warm start should reduce both the initial terminal defect and the fixed-point change, but it introduces dependence on guidance frequency and neighboring navigation states. The present cold-start timing is intentionally more reproducible and is best interpreted as an empirical cost for an independent replan, not as the minimum attainable closed-loop latency. At 100 Hz, one update interval is 10 ms. The nominal PIPG median and P95 consume 22.4 and 23.2% of that interval, respectively. The Monte Carlo P95 is 3.739 ms, leaving approximately 63% of the empirical budget for navigation, control allocation, and system scheduling. These figures do not replace a target-processor worst-case execution-time analysis. A flight implementation should use separate soft and hard deadlines: the soft deadline triggers validation of the best available candidate, whereas expiration of the hard deadline retains the preceding validated trajectory and 32 activates a backup guidance mode rather than issuing an unqualified new command. The grid study also informs the online discretization choice. Fifteen and 21 nodes reduce the median to 1.098 and 1.535 ms but offer limited temporal resolution of the aerodynamic and control-boundary arcs. Forty-one and 51 nodes increase the medians to 3.079 and 5.844 ms while remaining below the illustrative 10 ms soft budget. The 31-node case provides stable terminal time and propellant with a 2.273 ms median in the independent grid campaign and is a reasonable balance for the present dynamics. A model with faster attitude dynamics or a steeper aerodynamic database would require a new grid study. 

C. Interpretation of the ECOS Comparison 

ECOS is used as a full-state interior-point benchmark. To avoid crediting an outer-loop imbalance to condensation, both implementations share the physical dynamics, control bounds, fuel objective, drag-release schedule, proximal– tightness annealing, safeguarded extrapolation, outer stationarity thresholds, and terminal propagator. ECOS retains the full trapezoidal state chain and applies typical 10 −8-level conic tolerances; PIPG uses a single-precision condensed representation and two-stage inner tolerances. The nominal outer counts of 13 and 15 and the Monte Carlo means of 15.05 and 13.90 explicitly verify comparable SCP work. The nominal propellant difference of 0.089 kg, a relative difference of approximately 8 .8 × 10 −6, shows that the latency advantage is not explained by an evidently inferior PIPG objective. ECOS retains 217 nodal-state variables and 210 dynamics equalities, while condensed PIPG removes them before the inner solve. In the Monte Carlo study, PIPG performs about 14.8 times as many inner iterations as ECOS but requires only 1 /29 .5 of its median end-to-end time. A PIPG update consists mainly of products by 𝐻 and 𝐻 T, diagonal proximal operations, and 𝑁 three-dimensional projections; an ECOS iteration includes cone scaling and predictor–corrector factorization of a sparse full-state KKT system. ECOS retains important advantages. It supplies mature conic feasibility and duality-gap diagnostics for each convex subproblem and accommodates additional sparse state-path constraints naturally. Condensed PIPG is most effective when the hard equality dimension is small and the control set remains nodewise projectable. If heat rate, dynamic pressure, terrain, or load constraints dominate a mission, full-state sparse, partially condensed, and multiple-shooting formulations should be compared anew rather than extrapolating the present time ratio. 

D. Applicability of Complete Condensation 

Complete condensation relies on few hard terminal equalities and few hard state-path constraints. Eliminating the states in the presence of dense nodal heat, dynamic-pressure, load, terrain, or glide-corridor inequalities would create many dense rows involving the complete control history and could offset the first-order structural advantage. Partial condensation or multiple shooting partitioned at constrained nodes would then offer a more appropriate compromise. 33 Control-path constraints should be distinguished from state-path constraints. The total-angle disk and its single-𝐴 

epigraph remain node-separable after condensation and admit a low-cost projection. A control-rate bound couples neighboring nodes and could be handled by a banded projection or additional dual rows, but either option increases the hot-loop workload. The present circular set removes the artificial preference for the four corners of independent box constraints; it does not guarantee directional continuity between arbitrary neighboring controls. Before flight use, the first-order-hold commands must still be assessed against attitude-guidance and actuator tracking bandwidth. 

E. Optimality and Engineering Interpretation 

Positive quadratic proximal terms give each local surrogate a unique primal optimizer. The outer scheme, however, has no conventional trust-region ratio, and Eq. (72) is not a high-accuracy KKT certificate. The reported trajectory should be interpreted as a numerically stationary near-optimal solution that passes original nonlinear propagation and all stated task-level checks. This interpretation is appropriate for receding-horizon guidance, whose immediate requirement is a stable and executable command before the next update. Section II.D proves that, after full sensitivity release and outside a degenerate switching arc, the total-angle epigraph admits a tight optimal solution. Equation (28) converts that limiting structure into a verifiable finite-iteration mechanism and drives the measured gap to roundoff scale. The theorem is conditional: it does not cover arbitrary aerodynamic databases, coefficient signs, control geometries, or the globally nonconvex continuous-time problem. Similarly, the empirical desktop latencies do not provide flight-qualified worst-case execution times. A target implementation must re-evaluate the math library, cache behavior, floating-point options, stack usage, interrupt jitter, and compiler transformations, and must retain timeout monitoring and a validated fallback trajectory. 

F. Model Limitations and Future Work 

The model is a three-degree-of-freedom point mass with linear or quadratic aerodynamic coefficients. It omits attitude and angular-rate dynamics, gimbal dynamics, aerodynamic moments, plume–freestream interaction tables, wind, navigation error, and parametric uncertainty. The 3 m s −1 terminal speed avoids a coordinate singularity but does not model touchdown; an attitude-control and terminal-guidance mode must complete the maneuver. The 100 ±5% initial-state cases constitute a local task-domain screen, not a flight-envelope Monte Carlo campaign. Future work should investigate objective-reduction-based low-cost updates of the proximal weights, actuator-rate and bandwidth models, shifted warm starts for repeated guidance, partial condensation in the presence of state-path constraints, replacement of Eq. (4) by vehicle-specific aerodynamic tables, and processor-in-the-loop, hardware-in-the-loop, and worst-case execution-time testing on a representative flight computer. 34 IX. Conclusions 

A condensed-PIPG sequential convex method has been developed for reusable-rocket powered landing in a velocity frame with attack- and sideslip-dependent strong aerodynamics. Directly lifting the squared-angle variable can create artificial axial force because the epigraph relation is one-sided, whereas freezing the coefficient removes the aerodynamic sensitivity needed for active deceleration. The proposed homotopy gradually transfers the axial coefficient from the reference squared angle to the epigraph variable, and a convex tightness term suppresses the resulting slack. Pointwise Hamiltonian minimization proves that the fully released physical subproblem always admits a tight epigraph optimum and identifies the interior, boundary, and degenerate switching cases. The primary contribution is the exact dynamics condensation and six-dimensional row-orthogonal preconditioning that reduce each 31-node subproblem to 101 primal variables and six terminal equalities and enable a customized projection-only PIPG hot loop. Deterministic geometric annealing, two-stage inner accuracy, and a threefold safeguarded full-trajectory extrapolation are supporting mechanisms that reduce the nonlinear outer workload. In the double-precision ablation, the final configuration decreases the SCP count from 48 to 13, the cumulative PIPG updates from 6795 to 2460, and the median latency from 6.875 to 2.293 ms, while changing propellant by less than 0.102 kg. Under the shared outer protocol, the formal single-precision nominal case requires 13 SCP iterations and 2450 PIPG updates, with a median of 2.243 ms, propellant consumption of 10 177.145 kg, and a terminal residual of 8 .58 × 10 −6.Full-state ECOS terminates in 15 SCP iterations, returns 10 177.055 kg, and requires a 79.114 ms median. Both solvers validate all 100 common ±5% initial-state perturbations, with mean outer counts of 15.05 and 13.90. Over 15–51 nodes, condensed PIPG spans 1.098–5.844 ms, compared with 28.062–133.253 ms for ECOS. The demonstrated claim is deliberately bounded: for a local replanning domain with terminal-only hard state constraints, no control-rate constraint, and a nonzero terminal speed, strong-aerodynamics powered landing can be solved from a cold start at millisecond-class latency while satisfying the stated nonlinear task-level checks and finite-accuracy near-optimal stationarity conditions. Safeguarded extrapolation improves local efficiency but does not prove global convergence or global optimality of the continuous nonlinear problem. Operational use requires shifted warm starts, actuator-trackability assessment, hard timeout protection, retention of the preceding validated command, and representative flight-processor verification.
