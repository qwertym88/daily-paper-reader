## Abstract 

In this work, we introduce Sparse Hierarchical Fourier Interaction Networks 1

(SHFIN), a novel architectural primitive designed to replace both convolutional 2

kernels and the quadratic self - attention mechanism with a unified, spectrum -sparse 3

Fourier operator. SHFIN is built upon three core components: (1) a hierarchical 4

patch -wise fast Fourier transform (FFT) stage that partitions inputs into local-5

ized patches and computes an O(s log s) transform on each, preserving spatial 6

locality while enabling global information mixing; (2) a learnable K-sparse fre-7

quency masking mechanism, realized via a Gumbel -Softmax relaxation, which 8

dynamically selects only the K most informative spectral components per patch, 9

thereby pruning redundant high -frequency bands; and (3) a gated cross - frequency 10 

mixer, implemented as a low -rank bilinear interaction in the retained spectral sub-11 

space, which captures dependencies across channels at O(K2) cost rather than 12 

O(N 2). An inverse FFT and residual fusion complete the SHFIN block, seamlessly 13 

integrating with existing layer-norm and feed-forward modules. 14 

Empirically, we integrate SHFIN blocks into both convolutional and 15 

transformer -style backbones and conduct extensive experiments on ImageNet - 1k. 16 

On the ResNet -50 and ViT - Small scales, our SHFIN variants achieve compara-17 

ble Top -1 accuracy (within 0.5 pp) while reducing total parameter count by up 18 

to 60% and improving end -to -end inference latency by roughly 3× on NVIDIA 19 

A100 GPUs. Moreover, in the WMT14 English–German translation benchmark, 20 

a Transformer - Small augmented with SHFIN cross -attention layers matches a 21 

28.1 BLEU baseline with 55% lower peak GPU memory usage during training. 22 

These results demonstrate that SHFIN can serve as a drop -in replacement for both 23 

local convolution and global attention, offering a new pathway toward efficient, 24 

spectrum-aware deep architectures. 25 

Keywords: Sparse Spectral ; Hierarchical FFT; Low - Rank Cross -Frequency Mixer 26 

## 1 Introduction 27 

1.1 Historical Background 28 

The past decade has witnessed a remarkable evolution in deep learning architectures, beginning 29 

with the resurgence of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in image recognition. Krizhevsky et 30 

al. ’s seminal work demonstrated that a deep CNN trained on over a million images could achieve 31 

unprecedented accuracy on ImageNet, igniting widespread interest in large-scale, data-hungry models 32 

[11 ]. Building on this success, the VGG family introduced very small (3×3) convolutional filters to 33 

increase depth while controlling parameter growth [ 18 ]; GoogLeNet proposed inception modules to 34 

capture multi-scale features efficiently [ 19 ]; and ResNet’s residual connections enabled networks 35 

exceeding one hundred layers to be trained in a stable way[ 7]. Alongside these “macro-architecture” 36 

Submitted to 39th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2025). Do not distribute. advances, researchers developed efficient variants, MobileNet’s depth-wise separable convolutions 37 

[8], ShuffleNet’s channel shuffling [ 26 ], and EfficientNet’s compound scaling rules [ 20 ], to meet the 38 

demands of mobile and embedded deployments. 39 

Despite their local inductive bias, CNNs struggle to capture long-range dependencies without stacking 40 

many layers or resorting to large kernels. The Transformer architecture replaced convolutions with 41 

self-attention, computing pairwise interactions across all tokens in O(N 2) time and achieving state-42 

of-the-art results in machine translation [ 23 ]. Vision Transformers (ViT) extended this paradigm 43 

to images by partitioning them into patches and treating each patch as a “token” [ 4 ]. Follow-up 44 

works such as MLP-Mixer [ 22 ], ConViT [ 5 ], and ConvMixer [ 21 ] further blurred the lines between 45 

convolutional and attention-based designs, but all inherit the quadratic or cubic scaling bottlenecks 46 

that hinder deployment on resource-constrained hardware. 47 

In parallel, the frequency domain has emerged as an alternative medium for global information 48 

mixing at subquadratic cost. The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) pioneered the application of Fourier 49 

transforms to learn mappings between function spaces, particularly for solving partial differential 50 

equations [ 13 ]. FNet demonstrated that replacing self-attention with dense FFTs yields competitive 51 

performance in NLP tasks, reducing complexity to O(N log N ) [ 12 ]. GFNet introduced spectral 52 

gating to filter frequencies dynamically [ 24 ], and AFNO partitioned the spectrum into blocks to 53 

improve flexibility [ 6 ]. More recent spectral-domain hybrids, SpectFormer [ 2 ], FourierFormer [ 17 ], 54 

and Frequency-Domain Multi-Head Self-Attention (FD-MHSA) [ 25 ], have incorporated hierarchical 55 

mixing and learned filters but still retain dense spectral representations or lack adaptive sparsity 56 

mechanisms. 57 

Although these Fourier-based architectures achieve compelling trade-offs compared to vanilla atten-58 

tion, they share three critical limitations: (1) they process all frequency coefficients, leaving redundant 59 

components unpruned; (2) they apply global FFTs without preserving local spatial hierarchy; and 60 

(3) they lack explicit mechanisms to learn or enforce spectrum sparsity. In contrast, natural signals, 61 

images, audio, and text embeddings, are often compressible in the frequency domain, with most 62 

energy concentrated in a small subset of bands. This observation suggests that a tailored, sparse 63 

Fourier operator could deliver global mixing and local sensitivity at dramatically reduced cost. 64 

To address these gaps, we introduce **Sparse Hierarchical Fourier Interaction Networks** (SHFIN). 65 

SHFIN’s core innovation is a three-stage spectral block that (i) splits feature maps into patches and 66 

applies patch-wise FFTs to preserve locality; (ii) learns a K -sparse binary mask via Gumbel -Softmax 67 

to select only the most informative frequency channels; and (iii) employs a gated, low - rank bilinear 68 

mixer to model cross - frequency interactions efficiently. By uniting hierarchical locality, spectrum 69 

sparsity, and low - rank mixing, SHFIN fully replaces either convolutional kernels or self - attention 70 

layers, achieving global context aggregation with parameter and FLOP counts that scale as O(K)71 

rather than O(N ) or O(N 2).72 

In the sections that follow, we detail the mathematical formulation of SHFIN, present extensive 73 

experimental results on ImageNet -1k, CIFAR - 10/100, and WMT14 En -De translation, and compare 74 

against state-of-the-art CNN, Transformer, and Fourier-based baselines. We conclude with a discus-75 

sion of SHFIN’s implications for efficient model design and outline promising directions for adaptive 76 

sparsity and hardware-aware optimization. 77 

1.2 Contributions and Novelty 78 

This paper introduces SHFIN, whose novelty lies in three mutually–reinforcing ideas absent from 79 

prior art: (i) hierarchical patch-wise FFTs that mediate between local context and global receptive 80 

field, (ii) a learnable K–sparse frequency mask selecting only the most informative coefficients, 81 

and (iii) a gated cross–frequency mixer that substitutes for convolutional filtering or attention–based 82 

token mixing at linear rather than quadratic cost. Together these elements produce an operator with 83 

constant parameter footprint in input length and sub-quadratic compute. 84

(SHFIN) block. The derivation proceeds through four conceptual stages. We begin by casting the 87 

input feature map into a hierarchy of local Fourier domains, thereby balancing locality and global 88 

2frequency context. We then introduce a learnable K-sparse masking mechanism that selects the 89

bilinear mixer that couples the retained spectral coefficients across channels. Finally, we return to the 91 

signal domain by an inverse transform and complete the block with a residual fusion step. A detailed 92 

complexity analysis concludes the discussion. 93

3.7 Discussion 207 

Across three benchmarks, SHFIN delivers competitive or superior accuracy while markedly reducing 208 

model size and compute. The block’s deterministic Fourier masking contributes to fast inference, 209 

and its low - rank mixer preserves cross - channel expressiveness at minimal cost. Ablation results 210 

confirm that a modest sparsity level ( K = 16 ) suffices, highlighting the compressibility of frequency 211 

representations. Overall, SHFIN provides a compelling drop -in alternative to attention or convolution 212 

for practitioners seeking efficiency without sacrificing performance. 213 

## 4 Limitations and Trade-Offs 214 

While SHFIN offers dramatic reductions in parameter count and inference time, these gains come 215 

with several practical limitations and architectural trade - offs. First, the introduction of a learnable 216 

K-sparse mask requires careful hyperparameter tuning: choosing an overly small K may prune 217 

critical high - frequency components and degrade accuracy, whereas a large K reduces sparsity benefits. 218 

The Gumbel - Softmax relaxation adds stochasticity to training, which can increase convergence 219 

variance and necessitate longer warm -up schedules or lower learning rates. Second, although FFTs 220 

run in O(s log s) time, real - world performance depends heavily on optimized library support; on 221 

hardware without efficient FFT implementations, SHFIN may incur latency overhead compared to 222 

highly optimized convolution or attention kernels. 223 

Moreover, SHFIN’s hierarchical patchwise design introduces a locality–globality trade -off: smaller 224 

patch sizes preserve fine spatial detail but increase the number of FFT invocations and overall 225 

computation overhead, whereas larger patches improve efficiency at the risk of losing localized 226 

structural information. The gated low - rank mixer provides efficient cross -frequency interactions, 227 

yet when either the sparsity K or internal rank r grows large, the bilinear projections contribute 228 

non -negligible compute cost. Finally, adopting SHFIN may require additional engineering effort to 229

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work 240 

This paper has presented Sparse Hierarchical Fourier Interaction Networks (SHFIN), an architectural 241 

building block that unifies three complementary principles of frequency–domain modeling: (i) a 242 

hierarchical patch-wise Fourier transform that affords simultaneous access to local detail and global 243 

context; (ii) a learnable, differentiable top -K masking mechanism which retains only the most 244 

informative spectral coefficients, thereby exploiting the natural compressibility of visual and linguistic 245 

signals; and (iii) a gated low - rank bilinear mixer that captures cross - band correlations at negligible 246 

incremental cost. The resulting operator can be dropped into standard deep networks as a replacement 247 

for either convolutional kernels or self -attention layers. Extensive experiments on ImageNet -1k, the 248 

CIFAR benchmarks, and WMT14 machine translation demonstrate that SHFIN attains accuracy on 249 

par with or exceeding state - of - the -art convolutional, transformer, and Fourier -based models while 250 

reducing parameter count, theoretical FLOPs, and wall - clock latency by large margins, up to 2.6× in 251 

our ImageNet studies. 252 

Beyond empirical gains, SHFIN offers a conceptually clean view of frequency - space computation 253 

in deep learning: sparse spectral selection provides an explicit inductive bias towards compact 254 

signal representations, and the deterministic nature of the block avoids the stochastic variance and 255 

training instabilities common in adversarial or variational frameworks. The block’s reliance on 256 

well-established FFT primitives further suggests favorable hardware realization prospects. 257 

5.1 Research Directions. 258 

Several lines of inquiry emerge naturally from this work: 259 

1. Content - adaptive sparsity. The present model fixes the retained spectrum size K uniformly 260 

across inputs. Allowing K to vary dynamically, either through a budgeted controller or a 261 

sparsity prior, could yield instance-specific computation and further latency reductions. 262 

82. Hardware co -design. Because SHFIN is FFT -centric, custom accelerator design that fuses 263 

hierarchical FFTs with sparse complex -valued arithmetic may unlock additional throughput 264 

and energy savings, particularly on edge devices. 265 

3. Extension to higher - dimensional domains. Many scientific workloads, including nu-266 

merical weather prediction and volumetric medical imaging, are naturally represented 267 

as 3- D or even 4- D fields. Generalising SHFIN to 3- D Fourier volumes and integrating 268 

physics -informed constraints constitute promising steps toward efficient modeling of such 269 

data. 270 

4. Theoretical analysis. While preliminary results indicate favorable expressivity and effi-271 

ciency trade - offs, a formal characterization of SHFIN’s approximation properties relative to 272 

convolution and attention remains an open problem. 273 

5. Integration with generative objectives. Finally, coupling the deterministic spectral dictio-274 

nary learned by SHFIN with lightweight latent priors may lead to controllable, high -fidelity 275 

generative models without the sampling expense of diffusion methods. This is explored and 276 

demonstrated in an upcoming publication [9] 277 

In summary, SHFIN contributes an efficient, interpretable, and hardware - friendly alternative to 278 

canonical neural operators, and we anticipate that the directions outlined above will broaden its 279 

applicability and deepen its theoretical foundations. 280

11 NeurIPS Paper Checklist 361 

The checklist is designed to encourage best practices for responsible machine learning research, 362 

addressing issues of reproducibility, transparency, research ethics, and societal impact. Do not remove 363 

the checklist: The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected. The checklist should 364 

follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count 365 

towards the page limit. 366 

Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For 367 

each question in the checklist: 368 

• You should answer [Yes] , [No] , or [NA] . 369 

• [NA] means either that the question is Not Applicable for that particular paper or the 370 

relevant information is Not Available. 371 

• Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for NA). 372 

The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission. They are visible to the 373 

reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will be asked to also include it 374 

(after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published 375 

with the paper. 376 

The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation. 377 

While "[Yes] " is generally preferable to "[No] ", it is perfectly acceptable to answer "[No] " provided a 378 

proper justification is given (e.g., "error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally 379 

expensive" or "we were unable to find the license for the dataset we used"). In general, answering 380 

"[No] " or "[NA] " is not grounds for rejection. While the questions are phrased in a binary way, we 381 

acknowledge that the true answer is often more nuanced, so please just use your best judgment and 382 

write a justification to elaborate. All supporting evidence can appear either in the main paper or the 383 

supplemental material, provided in appendix. If you answer [Yes] to a question, in the justification 384 

please point to the section(s) where related material for the question can be found. 385
