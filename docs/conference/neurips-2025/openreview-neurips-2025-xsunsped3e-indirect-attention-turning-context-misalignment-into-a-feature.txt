## Abstract 

The attention mechanism has become a cornerstone of modern deep learning ar-1

chitectures, where keys and values are typically derived from the same underlying 2

sequence or representation. This work explores a less conventional scenario, when 3

keys and values originate from different sequences or modalities. Specifically, 4

we first analyze the attention mechanism’s behavior under noisy value features, 5

establishing a critical noise threshold beyond which signal degradation becomes 6

significant. Furthermore, we model context (key-value) misalignment as an ef-7

fective form of structured noise within the value features, demonstrating that the 8

noise induced by such misalignment can substantially exceed this critical thresh-9

old, thereby compromising standard attention’s efficacy. Motivated by this, we 10 

introduce Indirect Attention, a modified attention mechanism that infers relevance 11 

indirectly in scenarios with misaligned context. We evaluate the performance of 12 

Indirect Attention across a range of synthetic tasks and real-world applications, 13 

showcasing its superior ability to handle misalignment. 14 

## 1 Introduction 15 

The attention mechanism is a cornerstone of modern deep learning, particularly as the fundamental 16 

building block of Transformer-based architectures [ 1 ; 2 ; 3]. It operates by aggregating information 17 

from a context via learnable weights, effectively constructing new representations through a similarity-18 

weighted sum of value vectors, where weights are derived from query-key dot products. This 19 

formulation inherently assumes a structural alignment in context, and keys and values originate from 20 

the same input data. 21 

This paper asks: what happens when that assumption breaks? Specifically, we study the behavior of 22 

the attention mechanism when value vectors are drawn from a distribution different from that of keys, 23 

either due to data heterogeneity, architectural design, or multimodal inputs. 24 

We focus on two settings: additive noise in the value vectors, and structural misalignment between 25 

the keys and values. First, we quantify the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the attention output under 26 

Gaussian perturbations of the values and identify a critical noise threshold beyond which output 27 

reliability degrades. We then model key-value misalignment as an effective noise process and show 28 

that even moderate misalignment can induce noise energy far exceeding this critical threshold. 29 

Our analysis reveals that additive noise in the values leads to an SNR decay that is independent of the 30 

model’s embedding dimension. In contrast, key-value misalignment induces an SNR degradation 31 

that scales with embedding dimension, introducing irreducible noise under standard initialization 32 

assumptions. While training could, in principle, mitigate these effects, we argue that low-SNR initial 33 

states impair optimization, particularly during early training and in deeper architectures where signal 34 

degradation compounds. 35

dominate and corrupt the final output. 37 

Yet, rather than treating misalignment purely as unwanted or a bug, we argue it may hold untapped 38 

potential. Misaligned context opens possibilities for decoupling semantic retrieval (keys) from 39 

representational content (values), enabling more flexible information fusion in multimodal and cross-40 

domain settings involving cross-modal retrieval, memory-augmented networks, or learned external 41 

context injection [4; 5; 6]. 42 

To operationalize this idea, we propose a new mechanism: Indirect Attention, designed to retain 43 

effectiveness even under misaligned contexts. We show that this architecture recovers useful behavior 44 

where standard attention fails. 45 

We validate our theoretical insights with controlled synthetic tasks and evaluate Indirect Attention on 46 

a challenging one-shot object detection benchmark. Results demonstrate that our method maintains 47 

robustness in scenarios where conventional attention is compromised. 48 

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: 49 

1. We present a theoretical analysis of attention under value perturbation, identifying a 50 

dimension-independent SNR scaling and deriving a critical noise threshold. 51 

2. We model context misalignment as an effective noise process and show its energy typically 52 

exceeds the critical level, leading to output degradation. 53 

3. We introduce Indirect Attention , a modified attention mechanism tailored to handle mis-54 

aligned context. 55 

4. We empirically validate our findings on both synthetic tasks and a real-world object detection 56 

setting concerning separate sequences. 57 

## 2 Related works 58 

2.1 Transformers robustness 59 

Several works have considered adversarial training [ 7] for transformers and attention mechanisms, 60 

which encourages resistance to small, worst-case perturbations in input space. [ 8; 9] are using 61 

input perturbation and contrastive learning to increase the robustness of language models for text 62 

classification. Another line of work [ 10 ; 11 ; 12 ] explores adding noise to data labels as a regularization 63 

method. Our work differs from these works in the sense that we theoretically model context 64 

misalignment as noise and perturbation; otherwise, we do not aim to add noise to the model input. 65 

2.2 Relative position bias 66 

In addition to the positional encoding added to the input sequence, many works [13; 14; 15; 16; 17] 67 

use a relative position bias (RPB) that is added to attention logits based on the distance between keys 68 

and queries. However, our work differs from these works in different ways. First, in our method, we 69 

consider the relation between the queries and the value. Second, we apply the bias to the mismatched 70 

keys and values. Most importantly, the bias in our method is not merely structural and positional but 71 

gets updated at each layer by the attention layer output. 72 

2.3 Cross-Attention: 73 

Cross-attention is a foundational component of transformer-based encoder-decoder models [ 1] and 74 

has become central in multi-modal architectures such as [ 18 ; 5; 19 ; 20 ], where queries from one 75 

modality attend to key-value pairs from another. In such cross-attention mechanisms, the primary 76 

role of the query sequence is to extract or fuse information from the key-value sequence. Instead of a 77 

direct interaction between two sequences, indirect attention facilitates a more mediated relationship. 78 

The query sequence attends to value vectors from one context, conditioned on the key vectors from a 79 

different context. This decoupling of key and value sources enables a more flexible and robust form 80 

of information processing rather than mere fusion. 81

## 5 Indirect attention 171 

In this section, we introduce Indirect Attention, a modified attention mechanism designed to exhibit 172 

inherent robustness to misaligned context. 173 

Definition 1 (Indirect Attention) . Indirect attention is an attention mechanism in which the key and 174 

value vectors are derived from distinct input sequences, while each query is constructed by combining 175 

a learnable embedding with a feature from the value input. Attention scores are computed based on 176 

query-key similarity, modulated by a learnable bias function over structured relations between query 177 

and value positions. This bias evolves across layers to capture context-dependent relational patterns. 178 

Mechanism description: Let X = [ x1, · · · , x n]T ∈ Rn×d be an input sequence of length n, and 179 

let Y = [ y1, · · · , y n]T ∈ Rn×d be another input sequence of same length or padded to same length. 180 

Then the queries Q = [ q1, · · · , q m] of length m ≤ n will be constructed from combination of 181 

learnable embeddings and a subset of size m of Y such that qi = mi + yπ(i) where mi is learnable 182 

embedding and π(i) ∈ { 1, · · · , n } selecting a position from Y . Then the key and value feature 183 

vectors are constructed such that K = XW k, and V = Y W v respectively. Let P ∈ Rm×n be the 184 

relative positional index matrix defined as Pij = j − i capturing the offset between query position i185

Below, we discuss core details, design choices, and theoretical implications. 194 

Attention bias: The attention bias serves as an addressing signal; it steers the attention mechanism 195 

toward the correct positions in the value sequence. Specifically, each query qi attends selectively to a 196 

specific position in the value sequence vj . This selectivity is made possible through the learnable 197 

attention bias function f (Pij ) and the context-based updating of P after each layer. Unlike standard 198 

relative position biases, our attention bias adapts dynamically to correct for context misalignment, and 199 

the positional offset matrix P is contextually updated across layers to reflect latent structural shifts 200 

between queries and values. The attention bias f (Pij ) injects a structural prior into the attention 201 

mechanism. It allows the model to learn that, for a given query i, certain relative positions j are more 202 

likely to contain relevant values. This interaction encourages each query qi to develop an implicit 203 

association with one or more likely positions in the value sequence. The model learns this alignment 204 

behavior not via explicit supervision over positions, but as a consequence of minimizing the end-task 205 

loss, where successful attention focusing leads to better predictions or reconstructions. 206 

Bayesian view on attention bias : While the attention mechanism is not trained as a probabilistic 207 

model, its form resembles a log-linear model where the attention weight can be interpreted as 208 

assigning higher scores to positions based on both content similarity and structural bias. Specifically, 209 

we observe that the attention score 3 has the same additive structure as the log of a joint probability 210 

over query-key similarity and a positional prior. This motivates an interpretive analogy: 211 

log p(j|i, q i) ∝ qi · kj + log p(j|i)

where the first term behaves like a data-driven compatibility (log-likelihood) and the second term 212 

acts as a log-prior over relative positions. While this analogy is not exact, it provides useful intuition 213 

for indirect attention. We interpret the attention bias f (Pij ) as learning a log-prior over alignment 214 

positions, akin to log p(j|i). Thus by substituting log p(j|i) by f (Pij ) gives the attention weights as 215 

in 4. In practice, we use a 2-layer MLP with a ReLU activation as f along the key, query, and value 216 

weight matrices, which is trained jointly with the model. 217 

Role of multi-head in indirect attention The Indirect Attention mechanism naturally extends to 218 

multi-head configurations. In Multi-Head Indirect Attention, each head h applies its bias function 219 

f h(Pij ) to modulate attention weights. Different heads can develop different attention patterns—some 220 

may rely more heavily on attention bias (higher f h(Pij )), while others focus more on content 221 

similarity (lower f h(Pij )). See Appendix B.2.1. 222 

## 6 Experiments 223 

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed indirect-attention mechanism, we conduct two synthetic 224 

experiments and one real-world experiment. The synthetic experiments are designed to involve two 225 

separate sequences. The real-world experiment demonstrates the applicability of indirect attention in 226 

a practical scenario of one-shot object detection. 227 

6.1 Synthetic Experiments 228 

We construct tasks involving two distinct input sequences per instance. These tasks enable us to 229 

explicitly assign one sequence as the source of keys and another as the source of values, thus enforcing 230 

a controlled form of misalignment that mirrors the theoretical setup. 231 

Task 1: Sorting by arbitrary ordering. This task extends the classical sequence sorting paradigm 232 

by requiring the model to sort an input sequence of letters according to a given reference ordering, 233 

rather than a fixed ( alphabetical) order. A simpler version of this task has been previously studied in 234 

6[22 ; 23 ], where sorting was performed with respect to a fixed canonical ordering. In our formulation, 235 

the model is provided with two input sequences: (1) a target sequence of letters of length 10, sampled 236 

uniformly with replacement from an alphabet of 10 symbols, and (2) a reference ordering, randomly 237 

selected from a pool of 5 distinct permutations of the alphabet. The task is to predict the index of 238 

each symbol in the target sequence such that, when sorted according to this index, the resulting 239 

sequence is consistent with the ordering constraints. The pool size of 5 reference orderings introduces 240 

generalization difficulty. While the task becomes trivial with only 2 possible orderings (nearly perfect 241 

accuracy is achievable). We generate 1000 training and 200 testing instances, each comprising a pair 242 

of input and ordering sequences. 243 

Task 2: Sequence retrieval. This task requires the model to identify the location of a smaller query 244 

sequence embedded within a larger reference sequence. Like the previous task, it involves two 245 

distinct sequences serving as keys and values in the attention mechanism. Specifically, an query 246 

sequence of length 3 is sampled uniformly (with replacement) from a vocabulary of 10 numerical 247 

tokens. Independently, a reference sequence of length 10 is sampled in the same manner. The query 248 

sequence is then inserted at a randomly selected position within the reference sequence, ensuring that 249 

it is a contiguous subsequence of the reference. The model is trained to predict the starting index at 250 

which the query sequence occurs within the reference sequence. The dataset consists of 1000 training 251 

and 200 test examples, each comprising a query-reference pair. 252 

We evaluate each model according to task-specific accuracy metrics. For the sorting task, we report 253 

per-token prediction accuracy. For the retrieval task, we report the accuracy of predicting the starting 254 

position of the input sequence within the reference sequence. We compare three transformer-based 255 

models on both tasks: one using the proposed indirect-attention mechanism, a second using a baseline 256 

attention mechanism with misaligned context (referred to as “naive misaligned attention”), and a third 257 

using cross-attention. Each model consists of 6 layers, 4 attention heads, and a hidden dimension of 258 

128. Details of the model variants are as follows: 259 

Indirect Attention Transformer : This variant replaces standard attention with the proposed Indirect 260 

Attention mechanism, where keys and values are derived from different sequences, reflecting the 261 

structure of the tasks. Specifically, keys are constructed from the context sequence (the desired 262 

ordering in the sorting task or the query in the retrieval task), while values are derived from the main 263 

content to be manipulated or located (the input to be sorted or the reference sequence). Queries are 264 

learnable embeddings that are enriched by incorporating the value sequences directly. The attention 265 

logits are modulated by an attention bias term. For the function f for the attention bias, we use a 266 

two-layer MLP with ReLU activation. 267 

Naive Misaligned Attention Transformer : This variant adopts a transformer-style architecture but 268 

uses conventional scaled dot-product attention directly on misaligned key and value, following the 269 

same key–value assignment as in the Indirect-Attention Transformer. The query is learned similarly. 270 

Cross-Attention Transformer : This model uses a standard cross-attention mechanism where queries 271 

are derived from one sequence (the target input or query sequence) and keys and values are derived 272 

from the second sequence (the reference ordering or reference sequence). 273

Given a training set consisting of seen classes Cb and a test set containing new classes Cn with 275 

Cb ∩ Cn = ∅, the task of one-shot object detection is to train a detector on Cb so that it can generalize 276 

to the test set and Cn without additional training or tuning. Specifically, with a sample instance, also 277 

known as the query image Q ∈ RH×W ×3 showing one instance of an object of a certain class, the 278 

detector is expected to display the bounding box B ∈ R4 of all instances of the same class as Q in 279 

the target image I ∈ RH×W ×3, assuming the target image contains at least one instance of the same 280 

class as the object in Q. This problem can also be viewed as a visual prompt task [ 24 ], where given 281 

the visual prompt Q, the detector is expected to locate similar instances in the target image. 282

## 7 Conclusion 333 

We have provided an analysis of the attention mechanism’s vulnerability to both context perturbation, 334 

particularly noisy value features, and the more insidious challenge of context misalignment. By 335 

formally modeling context misalignment as an effective noise, we have quantified its detrimental 336 

impact on the attention output exceeding a threshold SNR of 1. To address this challenge, we 337 

introduced Indirect Attention, a novel architectural modification specifically engineered to enhance 338 

robustness in the presence of misaligned context. Our evaluation, spanning carefully designed 339 

synthetic experiments and challenging real-world applications, demonstrates the efficacy of the 340 

proposed Indirect Attention mechanism in mitigating the adverse effects of misalignment, showcasing 341 

its potential to unlock more reliable and flexible information processing in diverse deep learning 342 

tasks. 343 

## 8 Limitations 344 

Our analysis in this work has primarily focused on the behavior of attention mechanisms at the 345 

initialization point, providing a foundational understanding of their inherent susceptibility to context 346 

perturbation and misalignment. However, we acknowledge that the dynamic processes of gradient 347 

descent optimization and subsequent training can induce complex and potentially mitigating behaviors 348 

that remain as an interesting direction for future work. 349

of the same class from a different image. During testing, for each class in the target image, query 508 

patches of the same class are shuffled using a random seed set to the image ID of the target image. 509 

The first five patches are then selected, and the average metric score is reported. 510
