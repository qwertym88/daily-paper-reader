## Abstract 

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) serves as an important component in Automated 1

Machine Learning. Compared with reinforcement learning and gradient-based 2

NAS approaches, evolutionary computation-based NAS (ENAS) has gained promi-3

nence due to its lower dependence on domain expertise and superior adaptability 4

across diverse problem domains. However, despite a lot of research, how to sig-5

nificantly reduce the computational cost while pursuing high accuracy is still a 6

huge challenge for ENAS. To address this issue, we propose a Social Hierarchy-7

guided Evolutionary Neural Architecture Search algorithm (SH-ENAS). In this 8

algorithm, inspired by the social hierarchy, a novel population organization struc-9

ture is designed, and based on it, effective guidance operations are designed for 10 

the subsequent evolutionary search process. Additionally, to further reduce com-11 

putational overhead, a progressive evaluation search method is proposed, which 12 

introduces weight inheritance and validation-loss-guided early stopping operation 13 

to prevent unnecessary evaluations of the architecture. The experimental results 14 

demonstrate that SH-ENAS achieves test errors of 2.50% and 16 .24% on CIFAR-15 

10 and CIFAR-100, respectively, outperforming existing state-of-the-art methods. 16 

In particular, SH-ENAS requires only 10 population individuals and 12 iterations 17 

to complete the search, with computational costs as low as 0.69 GPU days and 18 

0.83 GPU days, validating the significant advantages of the new algorithm in terms 19 

of accuracy, computational efficiency, and automation. 20 

## 1 Introduction 21 

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) represents a critical component of Automated Machine Learning 22 

(AutoML), focusing on the automated design of neural networks to reduce the cost and complexity 23 

associated with manual architecture development. It has demonstrated significant success across 24 

various domains, including image classification [ 1 , 2, 3 , 4, 5], object detection[ 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 ], and 25 

semantic segmentation[11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. 26 

Existing NAS algorithms can be categorized into three dominant paradigms based on optimization 27 

mechanisms: Reinforcement Learning-based NAS (RL-NAS), Gradient-based NAS (GD-NAS), 28 

and Evolutionary computation-based NAS (ENAS)[ 17 ].While RL-NAS has achieved remarkable 29 

performance through iterative policy optimization, its computational cost remains prohibitively high. 30 

Even for medium-scale benchmarks such as CIFAR-10, it typically requires more than 2000 GPU 31 

days. Gradient-based approaches, represented by Differentiable architecture search (DARTS) [ 18 ], 32 

improve efficiency by progressively relaxing the search space. However, their effectiveness heavily 33 

relies on carefully designed supernet architectures, which demand substantial domain expertise for 34 

topological initialization and hyperparameter tuning. 35

evolutionary mechanism. By implementing dynamic random crossover and mutation operations, 37 

ENAS reduces prior knowledge dependency while maintaining competitive performance. These 38 

characteristics make it particularly suitable for multi-modal tasks and complex task scenarios. 39 

Nevertheless, ENAS still faces critical challenges in terms of computational scalability. Even with 40 

acceleration techniques such as surrogate models and weight inheritance, it requires substantial 41 

computational resources, which significantly limits its deployment in resource-constrained environ-42 

ments. This computational overhead stems from the inherent exploration–exploitation trade-off in 43 

evolutionary algorithms and remains an open research problem hindering their widespread adoption 44 

in practical applications. 45 

In general, the typical ENAS framework comprises four key steps, including population initialization, 46 

encoding, performance evaluation, and an evolutionary search process involving crossover and 47 

mutation operations, which are commonly implemented using Genetic Algorithms (GA) [ 19 ].To 48 

mitigate the high computational cost of ENAS, extensive work has been conducted, as shown in 49 

Section 2, primarily focusing on reducing the overhead associated with NAS performance evaluation. 50 

Nevertheless, how to reduce the computational resource consumption of ENAS while maintaining 51 

search accuracy still remains a significant challenge. 52 

It should be pointed out that, in addition to the performance evaluation overhead, another critical 53 

factor that leads to the high computational cost of ENAS, which is often underestimated in most 54 

current studies, lies in its evolutionary search process, which can significantly increase resource 55 

consumption due to insufficient search capability and inefficiency. This is mainly due to two factors: 56 

1) Lack of effective guidance in evolution : GA used in current ENAS employs fixed-probability 57 

random operations for crossover and mutation. This leads to a lack of effective guidance in 58 

evolution, severely limiting the algorithm’s global search ability. As a result, it requires large 59 

population sizes and more iterations, incurring heavy computational overhead. 60 

2) Extensive redundant performance evaluations : During the current ENAS search process, 61 

each individual in the evolutionary population is evaluated. However, as the search progresses, 62 

some underperforming individuals have little to no impact on the global optimization in later 63 

iterations, but are still evaluated because they cannot be distinguished. These redundant 64 

evaluations not only fail to improve search quality but also lead to significant unnecessary 65 

computational consumption. 66 

Therefore, focusing on the optimization of the search process and the reduction of redundancy 67 

evaluation, this paper proposes a Social Hierarchical-Guided Evolutionary Neural Architecture 68 

Search (SH-ENAS) algorithm, which aims to improve the global optimization ability of evolutionary 69 

search, reduce the population size and the number of iterations required in the search process, and 70 

thus reduce the consumption of computing resources. 71 

The primary contributions of this paper are as follows: 72 

1) Inspired by the social hierarchy, a novel population organization structure is designed and 73 

integrated with the evolutionary algorithm to effectively guide the subsequent search process. 74 

a) In this hierarchy, populations are divided into three subpopulations: upper, middle, and 75 

lower. By employing different crossover and mutation rate methods, each subpopulation 76 

is responsible for a different task: global optimization, potential exploration, and 77 

diversity maintenance, respectively. 78 

b) Based on the information from the upper subpopulations, a dynamic mutation operator 79 

selection and an operation type selection method are designed. This enables the search 80 

process to flexibly and adaptively adjust the size and structure of individuals under the 81 

guidance of high-quality individuals, thereby improving its search efficiency. 82 

c) Under the guidance of the number of iterations and the fitness change rate, a population 83 

reduction method is proposed for the lower subpopulation, which reduces unnecessary 84 

computational overhead by dynamically adjusting the population size. 85 

2) To further reduce computational overhead, a progressive evaluation search method is pro-86 

posed by introducing weight inheritance and validation-loss-guided early stopping operation 87 

to prevent unnecessary evaluations of architectures. 88 

Experimental results on the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets demonstrate that SH-ENAS out-89 

performs both classical manually designed networks and state-of-the-art NAS algorithms in terms 90 

2of accuracy and computational efficiency. Specifically, SH-ENAS achieves test errors of 2.50% on 91 

CIFAR-10 and 16.24% on CIFAR-100, surpassing all compared algorithms. Furthermore, SH-ENAS 92 

completes the search process with only 10 individuals and 12 iterations, reducing search costs to 0.69 93 

and 0.83 GPU days, significantly outperforming most existing ENAS algorithms. These results un-94 

derscore the comprehensive advantages of SH-ENAS in terms of accuracy, computational efficiency, 95 

and automation. 96 

## 2 Related Work 97 

In general, all kinds of existing NAS approaches face the challenge of high computational costs 98 

[17 ].For example, NASNet[ 20 ] employs an RL-trained controller RNN to iteratively optimize neural 99 

network cells, achieving near state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on CIFAR-10. However, this 100 

method incurs extremely high computational costs; Han et al. [ 21 ] indicated that it requires over 101 

1800 GPU days to search on ImageNet, making it impractical for resource-constrained environ-102 

ments. To mitigate computational costs, researchers have introduced surrogate models for rapid 103 

architecture evaluation, reducing evaluation overhead [ 22 , 23 ]. Nevertheless, these approaches still 104 

rely on large-scale datasets with ground truth labels, limiting their practicality. Therefore, despite 105 

these improvements, RL-NAS is still computationally expensive and is far from being a practical 106 

solution for resource-constrained scenarios.Inspired by weight-sharing mechanisms, DARTS [ 18 ]107 

proposed a gradient-based NAS method that constructs a supernet for efficient performance evalua-108 

tion, significantly reducing search time. However, DARTS and its variants (e.g., PC-DARTS[ 24 ], 109 

Fair DARTS[ 25 ]) suffer from two major issues: (1) supernet construction requires domain expertise, 110 

making NAS less accessible to non-expert users and hindering true NAS automation; (2) performance 111 

collapse caused by parameter co-adaptation, where instability in subnet weights during supernet 112 

training deteriorates performance. 113 

The earliest ENAS methods trace back to Genetic CNN, which encodes network architectures as fixed-114 

length binary strings and requires 17 GPU days to train on CIFAR-10. Recent efforts have focused on 115 

optimizing performance evaluation to lower ENAS computational overhead. For instance, Qiu et al. 116 

[26 ] introduces performance predictor into ENAS, which employs a trained performance predictor 117 

to assess new architectures, reducing the need for full training and shortening search time to 1.9 118 

GPU days. However, this method requires large-scale training data, making predictor training itself 119 

costly. Zhang et al.’s EvoNAS [ 27 ] allows offspring to inherit partial weights from parents, reducing 120 

evaluation time. However, its mutation operation is restricted to swapping units within individuals, 121 

limiting global search capabilities and preventing optimal architecture discovery. Real et al. proposed 122 

AmoebaNet [ 28 ], employing large-scale evolutionary search with population elimination methods to 123 

identify high-performance architectures, but the method still incurs substantial computational cost 124 

(3150 GPU days). Additionally, inspired by DARTS, Yang et al. [ 29 ] and Han et al. [ 21 ] incorporated 125 

supernet models into the ENAS process, enabling offspring networks to inherit supernet weights for 126 

accelerated evaluation. Nevertheless, supernet construction still demands domain knowledge and 127 

engineering efforts, increasing learning costs and contradicting NAS’s goal of automation. Early 128 

stopping, proposed in [ 30 , 31 , 32 ], serves as an efficient mechanism to reduce computational overhead 129 

in evolutionary NAS by halting training when model performance plateaus. However, it may introduce 130 

evaluation bias due to varying convergence behaviors across architectures [33]. 131 

In summary, despite the progress made by NAS, it can be observed that how to significantly reduce 132 

the computational cost while pursuing high accuracy remains a huge challenge. 133

3.2.1 Social hierarchy-based population organization structure 141 

To enhance the global search ability of the GA algorithm and minimize the required population size, 142 

inspired by social hierarchy, we propose a new population organization structure and combine it 143 

with the GA algorithm to improve the evolutionary search efficiency of ENAS. In each iteration, we 144 

divided the whole population into three subpopulations - upper, middle, and lower - in descending 145 

order of fitness. The fitness is calculated using Equation 1, which considers both accuracy and 146 

model complexity. The higher the accuracy, the higher the fitness value of the individuals. And 147 

if the individual has a larger number of parameters, it will reduce its fitness. This encourages the 148 

development of lightweight, high-performance architectures. 149

The upper subpopulation contains the best architectures of the current population, which can provide 155 

positive guidance for the iterative update of the evolutionary algorithm, improving the efficiency of the 156 

search. The middle subpopulation includes structures with high potential. By designing and applying 157 

flexible and appropriate crossover and mutation operations, these structures can explore promising 158 

areas of the architectural space more efficiently.The lower subpopulation contains architectures 159 

that perform poorly within the population, and during the ENAS search process, the probability of 160 

crossover and mutation operations should be increased for these individuals to help them escape local 161 

optima, thus enhancing population diversity and search efficiency. Additionally, in later iterations 162 

of the evolutionary algorithm, computational overhead can be significantly reduced by stopping the 163 

evaluation of the worst individuals in this subpopulation. 164 

In this social hierarchy-based structure, the members of each subpopulation are not fixed. After each 165 

iteration, they migrate between subpopulations based on updated fitness values, maintaining a stable 166 

number of individuals in each group. 167

3.2.2 Social hierarchy-guided mutation 190 

Mutation is a critical step in evolutionary algorithms, introducing diversity by randomly altering an 191 

individual’s genetic structure to generate new architecture. Different from the majority of current 192 

ENAS, in SH-ENAS, we propose a dynamic mutation method ( Social hierarchy-guided mutation 193 

operator selection and Social hierarchy-guided Operation Type Selection ) to adaptively adjust the size 194 

and structure of the architecture under the guidance of the upper subgroup information to promote 195 

search efficiency and model adaptability. 196 

In SH-ENAS, the architectures are encoded using the NASNet-style scheme [ 20 ]. Specifically, 197 

each cell is represented by node information and final connection information (contact). The node 198 

information comprises multiple operation-node pairs arranged sequentially, determining the network 199 

structure, with every two consecutive pairs corresponding to one output node. Furthermore, the 200 

supported operations include five major categories and nine subcategories (see Appendix C for 201 

details). 202 

1) Social hierarchy-guided mutation operator selection 203 

In current ENAS methods, the mutation operation can only be achieved by modifying the 204 

nodes, which greatly limits the search space that the algorithm can explore. In SH-ENAS, we 205 

supplement and design two additional mutation operations—addition and deletion—to expand 206 

the search space more comprehensively. Details of the mutation operations can be found in 207 

Appendix C. 208 

In SH-ENAS, we propose an adaptive probability distribution for selecting mutation operators, 209 

which dynamically selects mutation operations according to the information derived from the 210 

upper subgroup, thereby improving the quality and efficiency of the search. 211

3.3 Social hierarchy-guided population reduction 248 

In the social hierarchy population, the lower subpopulation consists of a large number of individuals 249 

with relatively low performance. As the search proceeds, the performance of individuals in this 250 

subpopulation gradually stabilizes. However, these individuals continue to generate new candidates 251 

through crossover and mutation, whose evaluation leads to a substantial and increasingly unjustifiable 252 

rise in the overall search cost. 253 

To address this issue, in SH-ENAS, we design a population reduction to improve algorithm efficiency. 254 

In the early stage, diversity is crucial, so no reduction is applied. As the population stabilizes, 255 

we gradually increase the reduction rate. The fitness variation rate serves as a key indicator of 256 

convergence speed. In the process of evolution, when the fitness changes relatively quickly, it 257 

indicates that the population is still very valuable overall, and it is not appropriate to reduce its 258 

individuals at this time. Conversely, if the fitness value changes slowly, indicating that the population 259 

has stabilized, reduction at this time will help to improve the efficiency of the search. The fitness 260

3.4 Progressive Evaluation Search Method Guided by Validation-Loss 269 

In SH-ENAS, we propose a Progressive Evaluation Search method by introducing weight inheritance 270 

and validation-loss-guided early stopping operation. As shown in Figure 6, during the iterative process, 271 

a maximum training epoch limit n is imposed, and a weight inheritance mechanism is utilized to 272 

retain the parameter information from unchanged parent models. Within each iteration, a dynamic 273 

early stopping criterion based on validation-loss is introduced: if the validation-loss remains below a 274 

predefined threshold δ for m consecutive epochs, indicating that the model performance has stabilized, 275 

the current evaluation process is terminated to avoid unnecessary computational overhead. This 276 

method improves the utilization efficiency of computational resources while maintaining evaluation 277 

reliability. 278

## 5 Conclusion 338 

Conclusion : This study introduces Social Hierarchy-Guided Evolutionary Neural Architecture 339 

Search (SH-ENAS), leveraging social dynamics to enhance exploration and exploitation during 340 

architecture search. Experiments demonstrate SH-ENAS outperforms existing NAS methods in 341 

accuracy, efficiency, and robustness. 342 

Impact : SH-ENAS contributes theoretically and practically by integrating structured social dynamics 343 

into evolutionary NAS. It can extend to other domains like reinforcement learning and NLP, support-344 

ing resource-efficient NAS, especially in limited-resource scenarios, potentially influencing future 345 

algorithmic designs. 346 

Limitation : Although effective, SH-ENAS primarily targets CNN-based image classification tasks. 347 

Future research should explore broader applications and scalability via distributed computing or 348 

hardware-aware NAS, addressing real-world constraints like hardware limitations and energy effi-349 

ciency. 350

A.1 Crossover weight inheritance 488 

When generating the structure of an offspring individual through crossover, the training parameters 489 

of the parent networks are directly used as the initial weights for the corresponding nodes in the 490 

offspring. Since the crossover operation does not introduce any new nodes, all node parameters of 491 

the offspring can be directly inherited from the parents without the need for random reinitialization 492 

of weights. 493 

Figure 4 illustrates an example of offspring generation through weight inheritance in a crossover 494 

operation. In the cell-based encoding scheme, each edge represents an operation, meaning that each 495 

edge corresponds to a specific weight. Suppose individuals p1 and p2 are selected as parents for 496 

crossover. A crossover is performed between the fourth and fifth genes to produce two offspring, 497 

denoted as c1 and c2. Let θ represent the set of inheritable parameters received by the offspring. It is 498 

evident that all weights in the offspring are inherited from the parents. 499 

As shown in Figure 4, offspring c1 inherits four operations (highlighted in white boxes) from parent 500 

p1, while the remaining four operations (yellow boxes) are inherited from parent p2. Accordingly, 501 

weights w1, w 2, w 3, w 4 are assigned from p1, and w13 , w 14 , w 15 , w 16 are assigned from p2 to the 502 

corresponding edges in c1. Similarly, offspring c2 inherits four operations (white boxes) from parent 503
